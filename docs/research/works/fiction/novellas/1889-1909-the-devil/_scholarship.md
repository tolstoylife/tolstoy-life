# Secondary Scholarship Layer — The Devil (Дьявол, 1889/1909/1911)

Research prototype. Attribute, don't assert. Sources verified by web search/fetch unless marked `[unverified]`. Mainstream scholarship is contrast-to-read-critically, not a baseline to confirm.

---

## Received view

The dominant English-language scholarly framing reads *The Devil* as an autobiographical study of uncontrollable male sexual obsession and, to varying degrees, casts Stepanida as a dangerous or elemental female force — the "femme fatale" in peasant dress. The standard move is to ground the narrative in Tolstoy's own 1858–c.1862 affair with the serf Aksinya Bazykina and to treat the novella as a direct discharge of guilt; Rancour-Laferriere (1998) situates it within a psychoanalytic argument that Tolstoy's misogyny and anti-sexuality derive from early maternal loss, while biographical critics (Troyat 1965/1967; A. N. Wilson 1988) emphasize the autobiographical-confessional dimension without systematically interrogating the title's attribution of evil to the woman. The two-ending structure is routinely noted — murder vs. suicide — but usually described as Tolstoy's personal indecision rather than as a structural argument about where the destroying force is located. Møller's *Postlude to The Kreutzer Sonata* (1988) is the most important contextual study: it situates *The Kreutzer Sonata* within the 1890s sexual-morality debate and touches on *The Devil* as companion text, but the focus is *Kreutzer* and the public controversy, not *The Devil* specifically. The Oxford World's Classics edition (Maude trans., intro. Richard F. Gustafson, 2009) grounds the stories in autobiographical experience but, based on available description, does not appear to argue systematically that the title is Irtenev's projection rather than authorial verdict. Ruth Crego Benson's *Women in Tolstoy* (1973) and Barbara Heldt's *Terrible Perfection* (1987) address Tolstoy's women broadly; neither work appears to have given *The Devil* extended treatment (no direct evidence found). Amy Mandelker's *Framing Anna Karenina* (1993) argues against a simple misogyny reading but focuses on *Anna Karenina*, not the late fiction.

## Triangulation

- **Finding 1 — The "devil" is internal, not the woman.** The mainstream received frame — supported by popular editions (Penguin Random House; Melville House Art of the Novella), which describe Stepanida as an irresistible elemental force — reads the title literally: the woman *is* the devil. Rancour-Laferriere (1998) argues that Tolstoy's misogynistic projections are a psychic defense mechanism rooted in maternal trauma, which would make Irtenev's projection symptomatic and authored rather than endorsed — but Rancour-Laferriere frames this as Tolstoy's own pathology rather than as deliberate narratorial irony. No scholarly source found explicitly argues that the title is Irtenev's self-deceiving projection offered critically by the author. Relation to dive finding: `contradicts` (mainstream) / `extends` (Rancour-Laferriere's framework can support the dive's hypothesis, but he does not make this move for *The Devil* specifically — [unverified whether he treats the novella at length]).

- **Finding 2 — Autobiographical basis (Aksinya Bazykina / Domna / Ermil / Timofey).** Confirmed by multiple biographical sources. The affair with Aksinya Aleksandrovna Bazykina (née Annikanova, 1836–1920) lasted approximately 1858–c.1862; their son Timofei Ermilovich Bazykin (1861–1934) was never officially recognized by Tolstoy. Aksinya's husband was named Ermil (the name appears in Tolstoy's diary and also as a character name in the novella, a signal of closeness to the source). The Maude preface (1925/1926) additionally names a 1880s incident involving a cook named Domna as a secondary inspiration. A. N. Wilson (1988, p. 187 cited in secondary sources) confirms the Aksinya basis in his biography. Henri Troyat (1965/1967) covers the affair biographically. The protagonist's name Irtenev deliberately echoes Tolstoy's early trilogy (*Childhood / Boyhood / Youth*). Relation to dive finding: `confirms` (autobiographical basis is universal scholarly consensus) + `extends` (the dual Aksinya/Domna inspiration noted in Maude; the Ermil name-transfer from real husband to diary is not widely noted in English scholarship).

- **Finding 3 — The 1889 triptych.** The simultaneous composition of *The Devil*, *The Kreutzer Sonata*, and *Father Sergius* is noted in most biographical sources (Russian Wikipedia cites the three works as sharing concerns about sexuality; Møller 1988 covers the *Kreutzer* side of the cluster). Møller does not treat *The Devil* at length; the triptych connection and Tolstoy's own withdrawal of *The Devil* from the 1898 Doukhobor fund ("мотив один и тот же, что в Отце Сергии") does not appear to be a prominent point in English-language scholarship surveyed. Relation to dive finding: mainstream `confirms` the simultaneity but `complicates` the read — English criticism tends to pair *The Devil* with *Kreutzer Sonata* (sexual obsession), not with *Father Sergius* (the cure-through-anonymity arc that the dive tracks). The withdrawal-quote is in the PSS apparatus; its absence from English criticism is a genuine gap.

- **Finding 4 — Suppression and Sofia's 1909 discovery.** Confirmed: the Russian Wikipedia article on the novella states that in 1909 Sofia Andreevna became acquainted with the manuscript, after which there was "a difficult and very tense explanation with her husband." The PSS commentary (via RVB and tolstoy-lit.ru) confirms that Chertkov requested the autograph be kept "in a safe place, at my mother's in Petersburg" (E. I. Chertkova's house) — the autograph was in St Petersburg, not at Yasnaya Polyana, from approximately the early 1890s forward. This is confirmed by the PSS apparatus which the dive already holds. Relation to dive finding: `confirms`. See also the chair-anecdote verdict below.

## Reception & publication

**Russian first publication.** *The Devil* was published posthumously in **Volume I** of *Посмертные художественные произведения Льва Николаевича Толстого*, edited by V. G. Chertkov, published by A. L. Tolstaya (Tolstoy's daughter), Moscow: Tipografiya T-va I. D. Sytina, **1911**. The three-volume series ran 1911–1912. Volume I contained *Дьявол*, *Фальшивый купон* (The Forged Coupon), *После бала* (After the Ball), *Алёша Горшок*, *Живой труп* (The Living Corpse), and several shorter pieces. This is confirmed by the Russian bookseller catalog (star-knigi.msk.ru) and Litfund auction description. The **vol. I/II question**: the PSS commentary places *The Devil* in vol. I; no reliable source surveyed places it in vol. II. If any edition or reference gives vol. II, it likely refers to a different printing or the English "Posthumous Works" (Archibald J. Wolf trans., International Book Publishers, 1920), where *The Devil* appears in vol. III of a three-volume English set — a separate publication whose volume numbering is unrelated to the Russian original.

**English translations.** The standard English translation is by **Aylmer and Louise Maude**, completed by Aylmer Maude on **12 September 1925** (according to Maude's own preface, recovered from the Project Gutenberg text). Maude notes this was "translated into English for the first time" and that it had not appeared in the Nelson & Sons English posthumous works edition. The Maude translation was reprinted in the **Oxford World's Classics** *The Devil and Other Stories* (9780199553990), with an introduction by **Richard F. Gustafson** (Oxford University Press, 2009 reprint; the series volume dates to 1992 in the World's Classics series). The **Melville House Art of the Novella** edition (ISBN 9780974607832, c. 2006) also uses the Maude translation and presents both endings.

**Critical and popular reception.** Because the novella was posthumous, there was no living-author censorship controversy of the kind that surrounded *The Kreutzer Sonata*. The work received attention primarily as autobiography and as part of the cluster of late Tolstoyan sexual-morality fiction. Møller (1988) is the key contextual study for the cluster. No major stage or film adaptation of Tolstoy's *The Devil* specifically was found in the web survey (a 1921 film titled *The Devil* is an adaptation of Ferenc Molnár's play, not Tolstoy's). The novella is best known in English through the Melville House and Oxford editions.

## The chair anecdote

**Verdict: popular legend, unverified and contradicted by the archival record.**

Multiple popular and commercial sources (Penguin Random House product page; web retailer descriptions) assert that Tolstoy "hid the manuscript in the upholstery of a chair in his office so his wife wouldn't find it." No primary or scholarly source was found that gives this anecdote with a citation. The PSS commentary and the Russian Wikipedia article on the novella both point in a different direction: Chertkov arranged for the autograph manuscript to be kept at his mother E. I. Chertkova's house in Petersburg, at Tolstoy's request, where it remained until after Tolstoy's death. This is consistent with the PSS apparatus note that the 1911 publication was based on "a corrected copy in the handwriting of V. G. Chertkov and I. I. Gorbunov, verified against the autograph." The suppression mechanism was geographical distance (Petersburg vs. Yasnaya Polyana), not concealment inside furniture. The chair anecdote appears to be a retailer-level embellishment that has propagated without a traceable scholarly source. **Route to `needsReview`.**
