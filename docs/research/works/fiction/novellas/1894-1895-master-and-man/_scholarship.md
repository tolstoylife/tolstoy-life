# Scholarship sweep — Master and Man (Хозяин и работник, 1894–95)

Layer: secondary / scholarship + reception  
Date: 2026-06-10  
Rigor: research-prototype (knowledge + web; sources cited where traceable; uncertainty flagged)

---

## Summary (received view)

The standard scholarly and biographical view frames *Master and Man* as Tolstoy's most artistically controlled late story — tighter than the didactic tracts, less anguished than *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*, and warmer in outcome. Biographers (Troyat, Bartlett, Simmons, Wilson) treat it primarily as a conversion tale: the greedy merchant Brekhunov dies in an act of self-sacrifice that saves his peasant servant Nikita, and through that act briefly enacts Tolstoy's doctrine that true life lies in living for others. The story is almost universally read alongside *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* as a diptych on dying well versus dying badly — a pairing routinely reinforced by publishers who issue them together. The explicit link to *On Life* (1886–87) is thematic rather than argued in most scholarship: reviewers note the story "embodies" the doctrine without tracing the treatise's specific vocabulary of *личность* renunciation. The proto-*What Is Art?* reading is nascent rather than developed — Deresiewicz (2010) is the clearest pointer. The biographical drama of the story's publication — Sofia's breakdown, Vanechka's death — receives disproportionate attention in popular biographies, sometimes eclipsing the literary analysis.

## Scholarship sources

- **Richard F. Gustafson** (1986), *Leo Tolstoy: Resident and Stranger — A Study in Fiction and Theology*, Princeton University Press — Gustafson argues for the essential coherence of Tolstoy's work rooted in religious thinking. His "emblematic realist" framework treats Tolstoy's late fiction as enacting theological propositions through concrete realist detail. *Master and Man* falls squarely in this frame: the blizzard is not merely literal; the death-merging of Brekhunov and Nikita enacts the claim, repeated across the late works, that the self's dissolution into love is the only real life. Gustafson does not appear to devote a dedicated chapter to the story, but his framework is the most useful scholarly tool for reading the **он — Никита, а Никита — он** moment as theological proposition, not sentimental flourish. - **William Deresiewicz** (2010), "The Renunciation Artist: On Leo Tolstoy," *The Nation* (February 11) — The most pointed secondary argument for reading *Master and Man* against Tolstoy's broader philosophy of renunciation. Deresiewicz argues the story presents "both alternatives": Nikita has known salvation through detachment all along ("he holds his life cheap"); Brekhunov arrives at salvation via an extreme act of renunciation that crosses into death. Directly links the late aesthetic theory (*What Is Art?*) to the narrative method: Tolstoy's "biblical simplicity" replaces realistic accumulation with spare, morally saturated storytelling. - **George Saunders** (2021), *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain*, Random House — Includes *Master and Man* (in the Maude translation) as the fourth story in his Russian fiction craft workshop. Saunders's reading is craft-focused: the story is a "pattern story" driven by repetition-with-variation (the characters keep getting lost, stakes escalate each time). His key claim: Tolstoy's rendering of moral transformation is radical because "it happens not through the total remaking of the sinner… but by a redirection of his (same old) energy." Saunders does not engage with *On Life* or *What Is Art?* but his analysis of how the form enacts moral change is the most developed available account of the story's structural theology. — https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/53487237-a-swim-in-a-pond-in-a-pond-in-the-rain

## Contemporary reception (1895)

### Popular success

*Master and Man* was a phenomenon on publication. The Posrednik first edition (I. D. Sytin, Moscow, censorship date 17 February 1895; price 20 kopeks) sold 15,000 copies within four days of its release on 5 March 1895. A second Posrednik edition followed on 9 March, priced at 3 kopeks to reach a mass readership — a deliberate choice in keeping with Tolstoy's populist publishing ethics. By any 1895 Russian standard, this was extraordinary commercial velocity for a literary story. Sources: PY Rare Books catalogue entry (https://www.pyrarebooks.com/rare-book/tolstoy-khoziain-rabotnik-first-1334/).

### The Severny Vestnik / Sofia Tolstaya crisis

The story's route to publication triggered the most dramatic domestic crisis of that year, preceding Vanechka's death by days.

**What happened:** Tolstoy decided to give *Master and Man* to *Severny Vestnik* (the St Petersburg journal edited by Lyubov Gurevich) without payment — as a literary gift consistent with his anti-copyright principles. Sofia Andreyevna was furious: the family's copyrights and the income they generated were her domain, and she administered all of Tolstoy's pre-1881 work commercially. She became convinced, according to her diary, that Tolstoy harboured romantic feelings for Gurevich. Tolstoy had asked his friend N. N. Strakhov to approve the proofs and deliver the manuscript to Gurevich.

**The snow incident:** In late February 1895, during the height of the row, Sofia fled the house into the snow in her dressing-gown and slippers. Tolstoy's diary entry: "She was fairly close to going out of her mind and to suicide… the children went and rode after her and brought her back." According to her own diary, Tolstoy had threatened to leave the household; she had run into the snow in despair. Tolstoy (in long johns and waistcoat) went after her. The accounts diverge — hers foregrounds his threat to leave and her jealousy of Gurevich; his records only "near suicide" and the children's intervention.

**Vanechka's death:** Two days after this confrontation, their youngest son Ivan Lvovich Tolstoy (Vanechka), aged six, died of scarlet fever — 23 February 1895. Sofia's diary: "My darling little Vanechka died this evening at 11 o'clock. My God, and I am still alive!" She wrote no diary entries for two years. Tolstoy interpreted the death as spiritually meaningful; Sofia could not accept this framing. The story's publication and their youngest child's death are thus separated by approximately 8 days (Vanechka died 23 Feb; Posrednik edition released 5 March). The manuscript had been delivered to *Severny Vestnik* earlier; the domestic crisis ran through February.

Sources: James Meek, "Some Wild Creature: Tolstoy Leaves Home," *London Review of Books*, vol. 32, no. 14 (22 July 2010) — https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v32/n14/james-meek/some-wild-creature; N. N. Strakhov correspondence summary at feb-web.ru (http://feb-web.ru/feb/tolstoy/critics/ts3/ts32022-.htm).

### The Leskov footnote

Nikolai Leskov died on 5 March 1895 — the same day as the Posrednik first-edition release. He cannot, therefore, have reacted in print to the story's appearance. Any reaction would have been private correspondence or conversation in the weeks before his death. Leskov was a significant presence in the *Severny Vestnik* orbit (his story *Polunochniki* appeared there in 1891); whether he read the manuscript in advance is not established by available evidence. [Leskov's reaction to *Master and Man*, if any, **not confirmed**.]

### Critical camps

The story was not censored (censorship permission date: 17 February 1895). The dominant critical response split broadly:
- **Enthusiastic realist-humanist readers** (broadly Tolstoyan in sympathy) read it as a return to artistic form after the polemical tracts — a story that did not preach overtly but enacted the moral.
- **Symbolist/decadent critics** associated with *Severny Vestnik* itself (Volynsky, Merezhkovsky, Gippius) were ambivalent: they admired the craft but were suspicious of Tolstoy's moralism and his refusal of interiority in the Symbolist sense. Merezhkovsky's essay *L. Tolstoy i Dostoevsky* (1900–02) would later frame Tolstoy as the supreme artist of "flesh" against Dostoevsky's "spirit" — a frame that fits *Master and Man* but postdates the first reception. [Specific 1895 critical reviews from the Symbolist camp not confirmed by available sources; the "two camps" characterisation is inferential.]

The story quickly attracted international attention; English translations appeared within months (see §Gap-fill facts).

## Triangulation

### Hypothesis 1: On Life given flesh — the death scene enacts the treatise's doctrine of *личность* renunciation

**Conventional view:** Scholarship broadly agrees that *Master and Man* dramatises Tolstoy's late religious philosophy — that true life means living for others, not for the self — and that Brekhunov's death is a positive spiritual event rather than a tragedy. The specific link to *On Life* (1886–87), with its precise vocabulary of *животная личность* (animal personality), its argument that the separate self is the source of suffering and death, and its claim that the only real life is in love for others — **is not argued explicitly in the accessible secondary literature.** Gustafson's framework (theological realism, the emblematic enactment of doctrine) is the closest scholarly instrument for it. Deresiewicz frames it as renunciation-to-death without citing *On Life*. Troyat uses the language of "true life vs illusory material life" without grounding it in the treatise.

**Relation: EXTENDS.** The dive's hypothesis is structurally present in the secondary literature as a thematic claim but has not been mapped to the treatise's specific argument and vocabulary. The claim that the **он — Никита, а Никита — он** moment is a direct fictional enactment of *On Life* ch. VI–X (the dissolution of the *личность* boundary as the experience of true life) appears to be below where scholarship stops.

**Sources:** Gustafson (1986); Deresiewicz (2010); Troyat (1965/1967); generic EBSCO/literariness.org analyses.

---

### Hypothesis 2: Salvation through the exploiter — it is the хозяин, not Nikita, who is transfigured; substitutionary death

**Conventional view:** This is partially confirmed by the mainstream but incompletely framed. Most commentators note Brekhunov's transformation: the "greedy and self-confident egotist becomes a humble and self-effacing human being" (eNotes/SuperSummary); the "unlikely transformation from exploiter to saviour" is explicitly named. However, scholarship almost universally reads this as *parallel* salvation — Nikita is already spiritually grounded ("had known salvation all along"), Brekhunov arrives at it. The asymmetry the dive identifies — that Brekhunov is the one *saved* in the full soteriological sense, while Nikita merely survives — is at best implicit. Saunders's craft reading (the "redirection of energy") comes closest to capturing why the master's arc is the spiritually operative one. The substitutionary/kenotic framing (хозяин becomes работник, the master becomes servant) is present in Austin (2022) but not in academic scholarship.

Some critics note the conversion as "too sudden to be credible" — this is a misreading that the dive can address by invoking *On Life*'s point that rational awakening can be instantaneous even where its preconditions have long been accumulating.

**Relation: COMPLICATES / partially CONFIRMS.** The fact of the merchant's transformation is confirmed; the specific soteriological asymmetry (Brekhunov saved, Nikita the instrument of that salvation rather than the recipient) is not argued in the literature and is available for the dive to develop.

**Sources:** Deresiewicz (2010); Saunders (2021); eNotes critical essays; SuperSummary analysis; Austin (2022).

---

### Hypothesis 3: Religious art before the theory — Master and Man as a lived specimen of What Is Art? universal religious art

**Conventional view:** The *What Is Art?* connection is present but underdeveloped. Most sources note chronological proximity (novella 1895, treatise finished 1897–98) and thematic consistency (Tolstoy's post-conversion aesthetic; rejection of sophisticated elitist art in favour of simple, morally communicative work). Deresiewicz (2010) is the most pointed: he explicitly connects the late narrative aesthetic — "biblical simplicity," spare storytelling that transmits moral truth — to the theoretical programme Tolstoy would articulate in *What Is Art?* The literariness.org analysis says the story "embodies [Tolstoy's] criteria more faithfully" than the tracts. However, no scholar appears to have made the explicit argument that *Master and Man* is not merely consistent with *What Is Art?* but is a *prior enactment* of "universal religious art" in Tolstoy's technical sense — art that infects the reader with the feeling of brotherhood, crossing all class and cultural lines.

**Relation: EXTENDS.** The What Is Art? connection is implied but not argued. The dive's specific claim — that the story was Tolstoy *living the theory before writing it*, that the 15,000-copies-in-4-days reception was the proof of concept for infectious universal art — is not made in accessible scholarship. The popular success data (15,000 copies in four days, 3-kopek Posrednik edition for the masses, simultaneous journal and cheap-booklet release) materially supports the hypothesis and is documented in the bibliographic record.

**Sources:** Deresiewicz (2010); literariness.org analysis; What Is Art? text (Marxists.org); publication data (PY Rare Books).

### Setting

The story is set in the Russian merchant-provincial world of post-Emancipation Russia (1860s onward), when the old serf-based rural economy was being replaced by petty capitalism. Brekhunov represents this new acquisitive class: church elder, innkeeper, grain dealer, aspiring landowner. The Goryachkin forest transaction he is trying to close is a timber acquisition. The physical geography is the central Russian steppe in deep winter.
