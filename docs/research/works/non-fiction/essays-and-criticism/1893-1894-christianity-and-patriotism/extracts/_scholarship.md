## Received view (summary)

The dominant scholarly treatment of *Christianity and Patriotism* slots it into Tolstoy's post-1880 "prophetic" phase without analysing the essay on its own terms. Aylmer Maude — Tolstoy's friend, translator, and first major English biographer — devoted only six lines to the essay in his *The Life of Tolstoy: Later Years* (1910), a 600-page volume; this thinness is noted explicitly in contemporary pamphlet commentary attached to the 1894 English edition (web-verified via the Archive.org / Wikisource bibliographic note for *Christianity and Patriotism*). Maude's sustained engagement with Tolstoy's anti-state and anti-patriotism argument appears instead in his later *Leo Tolstoy* (1918), where he treats non-resistance and "antagonism to governments" as expressed in *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1893) — that 1893 work, not *C&P*, is treated as the theoretical summit. Ernest J. Simmons (*Leo Tolstoy*, 1946) likewise foregrounds *The Kingdom of God Is Within You*, *What I Believe*, and *What Then Must We Do?* when discussing Tolstoy's political-religious thought; the patriotism essay figures as illustrative polemic rather than a work requiring independent analysis (web-verified: Archive.org Simmons description).

Rosamund Bartlett (*Tolstoy: A Russian Life*, 2010) embeds Tolstoy's anarchism and Christian pacifism in a chapter titled "Sectarian, Anarchist, Holy Fool," locating his anti-patriotism within his broader rejection of institutional Christianity and state authority. Bartlett's framing is sympathetic but biographical rather than philosophical, and on available review materials she does not give *C&P* essay-level attention (web-verified: published review of Bartlett 2010). A. N. Wilson (*Tolstoy: A Biography*, 1988) centres the Sermon on the Mount in Tolstoy's thought and treats his political writings with sympathy, but the focus is the man and his arc rather than close reading of individual essays (web-verified: Goodreads / Kirkus entries). Alexandre Christoyannopoulos (*Tolstoy's Political Thought: Christian Anarcho-Pacifist Iconoclasm Then and Now*, Routledge/BASEES, 2013; *Christian Anarchism: A Political Commentary on the Gospel*, Imprint Academic, 2010) gives the most systematic scholarly treatment of Tolstoy's patriotism-critique — listing it under his anatomy of Tolstoyan anarchism alongside "War, militarism and imperialism" and "Legislation and coercion" — though the published chapter structure does not indicate that *C&P* receives essay-level close reading (web-verified: author's own publications page). Christoyannopoulos explicitly reports that Tolstoy's "pacifist views" have often been minimized as "utopian or naïve," and he treats this as a received charge to challenge rather than a finding (web-verified: ResearchGate abstract for Christoyannopoulos 2013/2025). Charlotte Alston (*Tolstoy and his Disciples: The History of a Radical International Movement*, Bloomsbury, 2014) shifts from texts to the movement they spawned, tracing Tolstoyan communities in Russia and Europe and their suppression by the Bolsheviks; her frame is sociological-historical (web-verified: Amazon / Project MUSE review listing). Colm McKeogh (*Tolstoy's Pacifism*, Cambria Press, 2009) calls Tolstoy "the most influential, challenging, and provocative pacifist of his generation" but frames him primarily as a *dissident* — refusing compromise with the state — rather than a theorist, and characterises his political method as "strict, literalist, and simplistic" (web-verified: Cambria Press page).

Net consensus on *C&P* specifically is thin: it is acknowledged as a significant anti-patriotism polemic occasioned by the 1893 Toulon festivities and fitted into the 1890s non-resistance arc, but it does not attract the essay-level commentary lavished on *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* or *Resurrection*.

## Reception & afterlife

### Contemporary reception (1894–1906)

The essay was first published in French in 1894 by Éditions Perrin & Cie, Paris, translated by Jules Legras, under the title *L'Esprit chrétien et le patriotisme* (web-verified: Alamy image of the first French edition, p. 128; corroborated by the dive's own primary extract `v67_010_ZHyulyuLegraJulesLegras.txt`, a Tolstoy–Legras letter). English and German translations followed the same year; the Russian text appeared 1895. This is the standard pattern of Tolstoy's political writings appearing abroad first to evade Russian censorship. The essay was banned in Russia under the general censorship of Tolstoy's non-fiction and circulated underground; the handoff's date for the ban lifting (1906) is consistent with the post-1905 liberalisation but was UNVERIFIED at article level by this search.

British press reaction is documented in *The Spectator*, 14 July 1894: a dismissive, hostile review characterising the argument as "a vast deal of fury and uncommonly little Christianity." The reviewer argues that *authentic* patriotism is the foundation of Christian universalism (citing Christ's love for Jerusalem, Paul's patriotism, "sympathy is founded on knowledge"), and that Tolstoy "attempts to destroy the root and yet gather the fruits" (web-verified: Spectator Archive). This typifies the English conservative-liberal response — Tolstoy read as an extremist denying the moral reality of national feeling.

Polish intellectual Marian Zdziechowski initiated correspondence with Tolstoy on 12 August 1895, prompted directly by *C&P*, partly to promote his own essay *Religious and Political Ideals of Polish Society: 1831–1895*. Tolstoy's reply registered a "complete divergence" on patriotism, Polish messianism, the state, and national liberation; Zdziechowski continued to defend Polish patriotism as occupying "a special and solitary place" among oppressed peoples (web-verified: ResearchGate / Springer abstract, "Marian Zdziechowski and Leo Tolstoy: on true Christianity and Polish patriotism," 2024).

### Does *C&P* bear on the 1901 excommunication? — PRECISE ANSWER: NO, not directly.

The Holy Synod's decree (dated 20–22 February 1901, no. 557; published 24 February 1901) does **not** cite *Christianity and Patriotism*. The proximate triggers were *Resurrection* (1899) — especially the satirical depiction of the head of the Holy Synod in Book II, ch. XXVII — together with Tolstoy's *Critique of Dogmatic Theology* and his rewritten/harmonised Gospels. The decree charges Tolstoy with disseminating teachings "repugnant to Christ and the Church" and with rejecting the sacraments; the works at issue are his religious-doctrinal writings, not the political essays (web-verified: Wikisource, *Decree of Excommunication of Leo Tolstoy*; socialbites.ca analysis; Christian Science Journal 1901). *C&P* is part of the broader corpus that established Tolstoy's heterodoxy in the Synod's eyes, but it is not implicated in the 1901 decree. The excommunication must NOT be presented as targeting his anti-patriotism writings.

### Later influence

Tolstoy's influence on Gandhi runs chiefly through *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1894), which Gandhi read in South Africa and said "overwhelmed" him, shaping his Satyagraha and the founding of Tolstoy Farm. The direct Gandhi–Tolstoy correspondence occurred in 1909–1910 (Tolstoy's last year), after *A Letter to a Hindu* (1908). *C&P*'s specific contribution to the Gandhi channel is not documented; the vehicle is *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (web-verified: British Library European Studies blog, 2024; mkgandhi.org Gandhi–Tolstoy letters edition). Charlotte Alston (2014) documents the broader Tolstoyan movement's inter-war reach and its Bolshevik suppression; that movement drew on non-resistance and anti-statism generally rather than on any single essay.

## Pacifist-movement link

### Bertha von Suttner and the organised peace movement

Bertha von Suttner (1843–1914) published *Die Waffen nieder! Eine Lebensgeschichte* in 1889 — a European bestseller (37 editions, 12 languages before 1914). In 1893 she founded and edited (until 1899) the journal *Die Waffen Nieder*. She co-founded the Austrian Society of the Friends of Peace (1891), lobbied for an international court of arbitration, supported the Inter-Parliamentary Union, and in 1905 became the first woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize (web-verified: UNC Hist259 site; Britannica; 1914-1918-Online encyclopedia). Tolstoy praised her novel as the peace movement's equivalent of *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (web-verified: multiple secondary sources). The handoff note that Tolstoy considered sending *C&P* to Suttner's journal is UNVERIFIED at the archival level by this search; treat with caution until a primary source is located.

### How Tolstoy differs from arbitration / congress pacifism

Suttner's programme and the 1890s peace congresses sought disarmament through interstate agreement, an arbitration court, and parliamentary lobbying — precisely the "arbitration" path Tolstoy explicitly rejects inside *C&P*, where he writes that the "проповедники мира посредством арбитрации" (preachers of peace by means of arbitration) reason wrongly, because peoples subordinate to governments cannot be rational actors in interstate negotiation. Tolstoy is therefore NOT a conventional peace-movement pacifist; any scholarship that flattens him into one should be flagged.

This is confirmed and concretised in his *Letter to the Peace Conference* (written 1899, in reply to Swedish correspondents about the Hague Conference): he argues an arbitration court enforced by armies is a logical impossibility — "the arbitration court's decision against the military violence of the states will be executed by means of military violence; that which is to be limited will itself be the means of limitation" — and that the only path to peace is individual refusal of military service, not institutional reform (web-verified: Marxists.org full text). Scholars have summarised his stance as a critique of "state pacifism" (web-verified: ProQuest-indexed article "L. N. Tolstoy and the 1899 Hague Peace Conference," abstract only, full text not accessed).

### Chronology note

*C&P* was written Oct 1893 – Mar 1894 and first published May 1894. The First Hague Peace Conference ran 18 May – 29 July 1899, convened by Tsar Nicholas II. *C&P* therefore PREDATES Hague 1899 by five years; Tolstoy's Hague critique extends and sharpens *C&P*, not the reverse.

## Franco-Russian alliance context

The alliance grew from mutual anxiety about the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy). Chronology:

- **July 1891** — French naval squadron at Kronstadt; Tsar Alexander III stood bareheaded for the republican *Marseillaise*, a gesture that shocked monarchist Europe.
- **August 1891** — Exchange of diplomatic notes: mutual consultation if either power were threatened.
- **17 August 1892** — Military convention drafted: each to aid the other if attacked by Germany (or Austria/Italy with German support); no separate peace.
- **13–29 October 1893** — Russian Mediterranean squadron under **Rear-Admiral Fyodor Karlovich Avelan** visited Toulon. Ships: battleship *Emperor Nicholas I*; cruisers *Memory of Azov*, *Admiral Nakhimov*, *Rynda*; Black Sea gunboat *Terets*. France made it a national festival — balls, banquets, fireworks, parades; President Sadi Carnot received officers in Paris; commemorative medals struck by the Paris Mint and private workshops. **This is the "exhibit" Tolstoy anatomises in *C&P*.**
- **27 December 1893** — Russia formally approved the military convention.
- **4 January 1894** — France announced approval, completing the alliance (later the basis of the Entente).

Sources: Naval Central Museum (St. Petersburg) online exhibition (web-verified); EBSCO Research Starters, *Franco-Russian Alliance* (web-verified); Cairn.info / *Cahiers du monde russe* 43/2-3 (2002), "On the Periphery of the Franco-Russian Alliance: The History of a Banquet" (abstract only, full text paywalled).

## Labels to attribute (framing watch)

Encountered during this search. **None to be asserted in the project's own voice — attribute each when cited.**

1. **"Utopian or naïve"** — Christoyannopoulos (2013) reports Tolstoy's Christian-anarchist and pacifist views have "often been dismissed as utopian or naive," framing it as a received charge he contests. (ResearchGate abstract.) Flag as label-to-attribute, not a finding.
2. **"Strict, literalist, and simplistic"** — McKeogh (2009) on Tolstoy's political method, while also calling him the era's most influential pacifist. An ambivalent frame. (Cambria Press.)
3. **"Dissident rather than prophet / activist / theorist"** — McKeogh (2009). Usefully distinguishes Tolstoy from peace-congress pacifists but risks minimising the analytical content of essays like *C&P*. (Cambria Press.)
4. **"Christian Anarcho-Pacifist"** — Christoyannopoulos (2013) book title. Accurate but risks collapsing the distinction between Tolstoy's non-resistance (individual refusal, no institutional reform) and conventional arbitration/congress pacifism. Use with the caveat that "pacifist" here means non-violent non-resistance, not peace-congress activism.
5. **"A vast deal of fury and uncommonly little Christianity"** — *The Spectator* (14 July 1894, anonymous). A period dismissal; flag as reception, not finding.
6. **"Patriotic mass hypnosis"** — used in secondary summaries (e.g. the 2024 Springer/Zdziechowski article) to paraphrase Tolstoy. Appropriate, but attribute to Tolstoy via the essay rather than adopt as the project's own description.
