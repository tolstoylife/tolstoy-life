# Scholarship & Context: *Bethink Yourselves!* (Одумайтесь!, 1904)

Secondary-layer research file. Primary evidence (PSS text, diaries, letters) is handled in separate extracts.
Sources are attributed and marked `[unverified]` where a claim rests on knowledge without a live URL.

---

## Received view

English-language biographical scholarship gives *Bethink Yourselves!* brief but consistent coverage as the most prominent of Tolstoy's anti-war interventions. The essay is typically glossed as Tolstoy's principled stand against jingoism on both sides of the Russo-Japanese War, situated between his 1901 excommunication and the 1905 Revolution, and read as a statement of his settled position that the state, the Church, and the press are jointly culpable for the perpetuation of war. Aylmer Maude, who was in regular correspondence with Tolstoy in this period and served simultaneously as his English-language biographer and trusted translator, does not appear to have left a separate published reaction to the essay in the sources accessible here; his two-volume *The Life of Tolstoy* (Constable, 1908/1910) covers the Russo-Japanese period but specific treatment of the essay is unconfirmed at the level of granularity available to this research pass. Ernest J. Simmons's 1946 Columbia biography (*Leo Tolstoy*, Little, Brown) covers the late period; his specific treatment of *Bethink Yourselves* relative to other 1904 writings is not confirmable from this pass. Rosamund Bartlett's *Tolstoy: A Russian Life* (2010) is the most recent major English-language biography and is known to cover the 1904 period; specific page treatment of the essay is unconfirmed here. The 2025 *Angelaki* article "Bethinking Ourselves" (Kessler et al., DOI 10.1080/0969725X.2025.2529731) demonstrates continued scholarly interest in the essay's contemporary relevance; it was inaccessible behind a paywall for this pass. Shūsui Kōtoku's 1904 response in *Heimin Shimbun* (no. 40) is the most precisely datable contemporary critical engagement found: Kōtoku praises Tolstoy's moral diagnosis but rejects his solution as circular, arguing war is a product of capitalist structure, not spiritual failure — an early instance of the socialist critique of Tolstoyan nonresistance. The note that even Nicholas II "could not help but give a finger at him, and his opinions were immediately reported to all nations by telegram" (Kōtoku, 1904) attests to the essay's immediate global reach.

## Publication & censorship

### Composition dates (PSS-sourced, from v36 commentary)
- First diary entry on the essay: 28 January 1904 OS.
- The title *Одумайтесь (μετανοεῖτε)* appears on the first autograph draft.
- Chapters I–X sent to Chertkov in England: April 1904.
- Chapter XI completed and sent: 17–18 April 1904 OS.
- Chapter XII (the coda with the sailor's letter) completed: c. 8 May 1904 OS.
- Final addition to chapter XII: c. 20 May 1904 OS — this is the essay's last compositional date.
- Essay date in the manuscript sent to Chertkov: **7 March 1904 OS** (Pirogovo) — the date Tolstoy inscribed on the ten-chapter version; the colophon in PSS and in the Free Age Press edition reads **2 May / 13 May 1904 OS/NS** for the final complete text.

### First publication (abroad)
- Published by **Издание «Свободного слова»** (Free Word / Free Age Press), Christchurch (Крайстчерч), England, 1904. Publisher: V. G. Chertkov; sub-title added by the editors: "Статья по поводу русско-японской войны."
  - Source: PSS v. 36 commentary, p. 604–621 (v36_604_621_Odumajtes_comments.txt, line 177).
- The Free Age Press was Chertkov's "No Rights Reserved" press, established after his 1897 exile from Russia; C. W. Daniel Ltd. (London, est. 1902) later acquired it and pasted its imprint over Chertkov's label on surviving copies.
  - Source: AbeBooks listing of the Free Age Press first edition, https://www.abebooks.co.uk/Bethink-Tolstoy-Leo-London-Free-Age/8852201442/bd
- In the same 1904 print run, a **second popular edition** (without epigraphs, aimed at ordinary readers and soldiers) and a **third edition** (correcting errors of the first) were issued from Christchurch.
  - Source: PSS v. 36 commentary (line 177).
- Serialised in ***The Times* (London)**: published 27 June 1904 (New Style). The Ginn & Co. (Boston, 1904) edition bears the subtitle "Reprinted from the *London Times*."
  - Source: Project Gutenberg edition #27189 title page, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27189/27189-h/27189-h.htm; confirmed by multiple secondary sources.

### Censorship and first Russian publication
- The essay was **never legally published in Russia in Tolstoy's lifetime** (he died November 1910).
- **First Russian appearance**: 1906, published by **«Обновление»** (Renewal) as a separate pamphlet — **confiscated** by authorities.
  - Source: PSS v. 36 commentary (line 179): «В России «Одумайтесь!» вышло впервые в 1906 г. в издании «Обновление» отдельной брошюрой (конфискована).»
- **Second Russian attempt**: 1911, printed in vol. 19 of the 12th edition of Tolstoy's collected works — the **entire volume was confiscated**.
  - Source: PSS v. 36 commentary (line 179): «В 1911 г. статья напечатана в девятнадцатой части двенадцатого издания сочинений Толстого (том конфискован).»
- First legal Russian publication was therefore in the Soviet period, presumably in the Jubilee edition (PSS, 1936, vol. 36). Exact first post-1917 legal Russian publication date not confirmed in this research pass — see Unresolved.

### The title: μετανοεῖτε / metanoeite
- The Greek word appears in the first autograph draft as part of the title: «Одумайтесь (μετανοεῖτε)». Tolstoy drew it from the Gospel phrase (Mark 1:15 / Luke parallel): «μετανοεῖτε καὶ πιστεύετε ἐν τῷ εὐαγγελίῳ» — "repent and believe in the gospel." The word is used by John the Baptist and Jesus in the Synoptics as a call to fundamental reorientation of life and conscience.
- In the essay's own text (chapter XI / the fire coda), Tolstoy cites the verse: "The time is fulfilled and the Kingdom of God is at hand; (μετανοεῖτε) bethink yourselves and believe in the Gospel."
  - Source: Wikisource Bethink Yourselves! text, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bethink_Yourselves!; PSS v. 36 commentary (line 19).
- The English rendering "bethink yourselves" is the translators' chosen gloss; "repent" is the standard biblical translation but carries narrower penitential connotations; "bethink" preserves the cognitive / turning-back sense.

## Translation lineage

### English (authorised)
- Translated by **V. G. Chertkov** (Vladimir Grigorevich Tchertkoff, 1854–1936), editor of the Free Age Press, and **I. F. M.** — identified as **Isabella Fyvie Mayo** (pen name Edward Garrett; 10 December 1843 – 13 May 1914), Scottish writer, suffragist, and reformer.
  - Mayo assisted the Free Age Press from c. 1900 until her death in 1914, translating and promoting Tolstoy's English editions.
  - The Ginn & Co. Boston edition (1904), edited with an introduction by **Edwin D. Mead**, published for the International Union, is reprinted from *The Times* and credits "V. Tchertkoff and I. F. M."
  - Sources: Project Gutenberg #27189, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/27189; Wikipedia: Isabella Fyvie Mayo, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_Fyvie_Mayo; Scotland-Russia project, http://www.scotland-russia.llc.ed.ac.uk/isabella-fyvie-mayo/

## Reception

### In Russia and the Church
- By 1904, Tolstoy had already been excommunicated by the Holy Synod (February–March 1901 — the Synod's circular letter; widely reported as full excommunication). His *Reply to the Synod* was his published rejoinder. The excommunication was not revoked and was still formally in force in 1904.
  - Source: Wikisource, Decree of Excommunication, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Decree_of_Excommunication_of_Leo_Tolstoy; Christian Science Journal, https://journal.christianscience.com/issues/1901/9/19-6/tolstoy-s-excommunication
- The essay could not be published legally in Russia — the 1906 pamphlet was immediately confiscated, and the 1911 collected-works volume likewise (PSS commentary). This is the direct measure of state reaction: not prosecution of the author (Tolstoy's celebrity made that politically difficult) but confiscation of the text.
- No specific Holy Synod pronouncement against *Bethink Yourselves* specifically has been found in this research pass; the Synod's standing condemnation of Tolstoy (the 1901 excommunication decree) was the operative framing. Any further specific Synod statement remains unconfirmed — see Unresolved.
- The essay was written with a Russian civilian and military audience explicitly in mind; Tolstoy repeatedly revised to soften direct attacks on named individuals (Nikolai, Bezobrazov, Witte, Suvorin, Metropolitan Antoni and Bishop Sergei — most proper names were struck from later drafts at Tolstoy's own instruction; PSS commentary, lines 89–101). The essay thus reached its intended Russian audience primarily via samizdat-style circulation and foreign-published copies smuggled in.
- In the 1905 revolutionary period, the essay circulated in Russia as part of a broader surge of Tolstoyan political pamphlets. The 1906 "Обновление" edition was part of this wave.
- Alexei Navalny cited the essay's opening sentence during his 2022 trial — a marker of its continued resonance in Russian civil-society memory.
  - Source: University of Kansas, Slavic/German/Eurasian Studies, https://sges.ku.edu/leo-tolstoy-russian-pacifist-thinker

### International reception
- Simultaneous publication in *The Times* (London, 27 June 1904) gave the essay immediate international visibility. It was reprinted in multiple formats within the year: Free Age Press (Christchurch), Ginn & Co. / International Union for Peace (Boston), the American Peace Society edition.
- The essay was translated into Swedish, German, and at least two French versions in 1904–1905 (see Translation lineage above), evidence of rapid European uptake.
- **Japan**: Shūsui Kōtoku published a response in *Heimin Shimbun* (no. 40, 1904) — the leading Japanese socialist newspaper — praising Tolstoy's moral courage but arguing his remedy (individual repentance) was inadequate to the structural causes of war. A scholarly study by Susanna Fessler (University at Albany) examines Anesaki Masaharu's reception of Tolstoy in Japan in this period. The essay "gained little traction among the [Japanese] intelligentsia" broadly (per secondary sources), but socialist and anarchist circles engaged it seriously.
  - Sources: Anarchist Library, https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/shusui-kotoku-commenting-on-tolstoy-s-anti-war-stance; Fessler, https://scholarsarchive.library.albany.edu/eas_fac_scholar/18/
- The essay became a reference text in early twentieth-century pacifist and anti-militarist circles. Edwin Ginn's International Union for Peace included it among the "impressive peace classics" disseminated 1902–1910.
  - Source: Columbia University Teachers College EPE entry on Edwin Ginn, https://www.tc.columbia.edu/epe/epe-entries/Howlett_EdwinGinn_21May09.pdf
- In 2022, during Russia's invasion of Ukraine, the essay was cited by Navalny and circulated widely as an anti-war reference, demonstrating durable reception beyond the academic sphere.

## Fact-check: The Petropavlovsk sinking (essay section XI)

- **Ship**: Battleship *Petropavlovsk* (1894), flagship of the Russian Pacific Squadron.
- **Date**: **13 April 1904 NS** (31 March 1904 OS). Struck one or more Japanese mines laid the previous night near Port Arthur's entrance, at 09:42; sank almost instantly.
- **Casualties**: 27 officers and 652 enlisted men killed (total 679), including:
  - Vice-Admiral **Stepan Makarov** (squadron commander; remains later recovered by Japanese divers and given a burial at sea; monument erected in St Petersburg 1913).
  - War artist **Vasily Vereshchagin**.
  - Survivors: 7 officers and 73 men.
- **Note on Tolstoy's text**: PSS commentary (line 169) records that in an early draft Tolstoy wrote "несколько тысяч" (several thousand) Japanese killed; Chertkov corrected this to "множество" (multitude/many) on the grounds that "по сведениям, имевшимся за границей, потоплено было не несколько тысяч японцев, а несколько сот" (the overseas information was that not several thousand Japanese had been sunk, but several hundred). The ship was Russian, not Japanese; the confusion may relate to a separate Japanese vessel loss near this time, or Tolstoy was referring to overall Japanese casualties in the broader engagement.
- Source: Wikipedia, Russian battleship Petropavlovsk (1894), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Petropavlovsk_(1894)
