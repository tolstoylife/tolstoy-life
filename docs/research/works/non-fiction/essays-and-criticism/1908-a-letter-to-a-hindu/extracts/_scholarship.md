# Scholarship layer — "A Letter to a Hindu" (1908)

Secondary sources compiled 2026-06-13 for the corpus-dive on Tolstoy's letter to Tarak Nath Das.
This is a contrast layer: attributed, skeptical, flagging genuine uncertainty.

## A. Gandhi reception — dated facts and quotes

### The chain of transmission

Tolstoy wrote the letter on 14 December 1908, addressed to Tarak Nath Das, editor of *Free Hindustan*. The letter was written in Russian and dictated to D.P. Makovitsky; Tolstoy edited it and sent it to V.G. Chertkov (prominent Tolstoyan and editor of Tolstoy's works in England). Chertkov produced an English translation — confirmed by a letter Tolstoy wrote to Chertkov on 17 September: "I have read the translation of the letter to Gandhy. What I do not like is not the translation but the style of the letter." This confirms Chertkov translated it and Tolstoy reviewed the English; whether Tolstoy "revised" it substantially or only complained about style is uncertain from this evidence alone. [SOURCE UNCERTAINTY: no single accessible source directly confirms Chertkov as the sole English translator with Tolstoy's direct revision. The phrase "Tolstoy revised" used in some PSS-adjacent notes may overstate what the Chertkov letter shows — flag for needsReview.]

The letter reached Gandhi in London in autumn 1909. He received a typewritten copy, verified its authenticity with Tolstoy, and — on the return voyage aboard the S.S. Kildonan Castle (November 13–22, 1909) — translated it into Gujarati. During that same voyage he also wrote *Hind Swaraj*. (Hazama, 2022/2023; Cambridge Vol. 10 table of contents.)

### Gandhi's letter to Tolstoy, 1 October 1909

Gandhi wrote from London asking permission to reprint "A Letter to a Hindu" in *Indian Opinion* and to print 20,000 copies. He asked Tolstoy to confirm the copy was accurate and sought his broader advice. Gandhi also raised the question of reincarnation, asking Tolstoy to reconsider his critique of the doctrine. (NonResistance.org, Correspondence text; Wikisource, Correspondence between Tolstoy and Gandhi.)

### Tolstoy's reply, 7 October 1909

Tolstoy confirmed authorship and granted permission: "I wrote the *Letter to a Hindu*, and am very pleased to have it translated." On reincarnation: "I for my part should not omit anything, for I think that faith in a re-birth will never restrain mankind as much as faith in the immortality of the soul." He allowed Gandhi to omit the reincarnation material if publishing for a mass audience — a significant concession that Gandhi scholarship has noted (Hazama 2023). (Wikisource, Correspondence.)

### Gandhi's two prefaces, 18–19 November 1909

The *Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi*, Volume 10 (covering August 1909–March 1910) opens with two items titled "Preface to Leo Tolstoy's 'Letter to a Hindoo'," dated 18 November 1909 (p. 1) and 19 November 1909 (p. 3). The preface dated 19 November (from S.S. Kildonan Castle) is the one reprinted with the letter in English editions.

Key quotes from Gandhi's preface (19 November 1909):

- Tolstoy described as "that great teacher whom I have long looked upon as one of my guides."
- "One need not accept all that Tolstoy says … to realize the central truth of his indictment." (MarginalIan / Transcend Media via the Tolstoy–Gandhi letter archive; confirmed in multiple secondary sources.)
- "There is no doubt that there is nothing new in what Tolstoy preaches. But his presentation of the old truth is refreshingly forceful." (MarginalIan.)
- "It is a mere statement of fact to say that every Indian, whether he own up to it or not, has national aspirations." (Cambridge edition; Wikisource.)
- Gandhi notes the letter was "a translation of Tolstoy's letter written in Russian in reply to a letter from the Editor of the Free Hindustan" and that he would "translate it myself into Gujarati" and encourage translations into "various Indian vernaculars."

[NOTE: The Wikisource text of the Introduction (A_Letter_to_a_Hindu/Introduction) does not contain the "one of the clearest thinkers in the western world" phrasing often cited in popular sources. That phrase appears in secondary accounts; it may conflate two versions of the preface or be a paraphrase. NEEDS REVIEW.]

### Publication in *Indian Opinion*

Gandhi published Tolstoy's letter in *Indian Opinion* (Phoenix, Natal) in three instalments:

- **25 December 1909** — first instalment (Gujarati: "Ek Hindu uparno Kāgaḷ")
- **1 January 1910** — second instalment
- **8 January 1910** — third instalment

The English version accompanied the Gujarati. (Confirmed by CWMG Vol. 10 context and a CWMG-citing secondary source recovered in search; the PSS commentary's "January 1910" is the completion of serialization, not the start. The 25 December 1909 start date resolves the discrepancy.)

### Pamphlet edition

Isabel Hofmeyr, in *Gandhi's Printing Press: Experiments in Slow Reading* (Harvard University Press, 2013), confirms that the International Printing Press at Phoenix, Natal printed a standalone pamphlet of "Tolstoy's *Letter to a Hindoo*." It carried a "no rights reserved" designation, consistent with Gandhi's rejection of copyright restrictions to allow free circulation. No precise date for the pamphlet is given in the accessible portion of Hofmeyr's text; it was produced in the 1910 period concurrent with serialization. [UNCERTAINTY: whether the pamphlet is from late 1909/early 1910 or later in 1910 is not confirmed by the sources available here.]

### Tolstoy's last letter to Gandhi, 7 September 1910

This is Tolstoy's final letter to Gandhi, written eight weeks before his death (7 November 1910). The letter is focused on the doctrine of non-resistance as love. Key passage:

> "The longer I live — especially now when I clearly feel the approach of death — the more I feel moved to express what I feel more strongly than anything else, and what in my opinion is of immense importance, namely, what we call the renunciation of all opposition by force, which really simply means the doctrine of the law of love unperverted by sophistries."

And on the Transvaal work:

> "Your work in the Transvaal…supplies the most weighty practical proof [of nonviolence's viability worldwide]."

(Marxists.org, Gandhi Letters; NonResistance.org Correspondence; Wikisource.)

[NOTE: The specific formulation "the most important of all the work now being done in the world" — cited in some popular accounts — does not appear in the versions of this letter I could verify. The sourced phrase is "most weighty practical proof." Both may derive from different translations of the same Russian. NEEDS REVIEW — check against PSS vol. 89 or the B. Srinivasa Murthy edition.]

## B. Received scholarly view

### Martin Green, *Tolstoy and Gandhi, Men of Peace: A Biography* (1983) / *The Origins of Nonviolence: Tolstoy and Gandhi in Their Historical Settings* (1986)

Green wrote the most sustained double-biography engaging the Tolstoy–Gandhi intellectual relationship. His argument: both men arrived independently at the same imperative — renunciation of the imperialistic, war-like modern Western world-system — despite dissimilar backgrounds. Green treats the 1908–1910 correspondence as a convergence, not a one-way influence. The *Origins of Nonviolence* (Penn State UP, 1986) traces the world-historical forces acting on Russia and India that produced nonviolence in each man. Green gives "A Letter to a Hindu" significant weight as the trigger of the correspondence. [LIMITATION: I could not access full text of Green's books; the above is from bibliographic descriptions and secondary summaries. His specific argument about the letter itself vs. the broader correspondence is not independently verified here.]

(Goodreads author page; Amazon bibliographic entries; secondary summaries in multiple search results.)

### B. Srinivasa Murthy (ed.), *Mahatma Gandhi and Leo Tolstoy Letters* (1987)

This is the primary scholarly edition of the Tolstoy–Gandhi letters in English, with introduction and notes. It reprints "A Letter to a Hindu" as part of the correspondence context. The mkgandhi.org PDF (inaccessible as a readable file in this session) is the standard reference for scholars. [LIMITATION: content of the introduction could not be extracted from the binary PDF.]

### Ramachandra Guha, *Gandhi Before India* (2013)

Guha covers the 1909–1910 period in detail as the formative phase before Gandhi's return to India. Key characterization: "Tolstoy's piece, now known as *Letter to a Hindu*, reached Gandhi through a friend, and he was very impressed by it." Guha's book covers how Gandhi's reading of Tolstoy in this period, combined with his encounter with Hindu revolutionaries in London advocating violence, pushed him toward an explicit philosophical commitment to non-violent methods. Guha describes Gandhi's "experiments with dissident cults such as the Tolstoyans" in South Africa. Guha is a cautious biographer who distinguishes intellectual influence from doctrinal adoption — he does not overstate the letter's role as source of satyagraha but treats the Tolstoy correspondence as one thread among several (Ruskin, Thoreau, Bhagavad Gita, Gandhi's own South African campaign experience). [LIMITATION: I could not access Guha's actual text; the above is derived from the book's Wikipedia page and search summaries.]

(Wikipedia, *Gandhi Before India*; search summaries citing Guha.)

### Eijiro Hazama, "Unravelling the Myth of Gandhian Non-violence: Why Did Gandhi Connect His Principle of Satyāgraha with the 'Hindu' Notion of Ahiṃsā?" *Modern Intellectual History* 20:1 (2023, published online March 2022)

This is the most rigorous peer-reviewed article recovered in this search on the intellectual history question. Hazama's central finding (confirmed from full abstract/extract):

- Gandhi encountered "A Letter to a Hindu" in London in 1909 and immediately requested permission to translate it into Gujarati. The timing proved critical: Gandhi was then encountering Hindu revolutionaries advocating violence.
- Tolstoy's work convinced Gandhi to ground his Transvaal campaign entirely in principles of love and non-resistance. Gandhi composed *Hind Svaraj* during the return voyage — immediately after encountering the letter.
- However, Gandhi "never used the word 'non-violence' during his South African years" and relied instead on *dayā* (compassion) and *prem* (love) drawn from both Tolstoyan and Indian bhakti traditions.
- Gandhi only began employing *ahimsa* as a framework after returning to India in 1915 — not during South Africa — and the reasons were partly political: cultivating Jain and Ahmedabad mill-owner patrons for whom *ahimsa* held religious significance.
- The English term "non-violence" was coined by Gandhi in 1919.

Key implication for this dive: Hazama complicates the standard story that "A Letter to a Hindu" directly produced satyagraha. The letter shaped the South African phase, but the ahimsa-satyagraha linkage came later and for reasons partly unrelated to Tolstoy.

(Hazama, Eijiro, 2022/2023, "Unravelling the Myth of Gandhian Non-violence," *Modern Intellectual History*, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-intellectual-history/article/unravelling-the-myth-of-gandhian-nonviolence-why-did-gandhi-connect-his-principle-of-satyagraha-with-the-hindu-notion-of-ahimsa/AE0B11F66A8048CD92587964BA110C53)

### Karuna Mantena, "Another Realism: The Politics of Gandhian Nonviolence," *American Political Science Review* (2012)

Mantena argues Gandhi's nonviolence was not pacifist quietism but a form of "political realism" — contextual, consequentialist, and moral-psychological. Gandhi's critique of violence turned on "exposing the futility of violence" as a political tool, not (as in Tolstoy) on an absolute spiritual imperative against resistance. Tolstoy was among Gandhi's critics of violent revolution but the theoretical architecture differs. Mantena's argument implies that scholars who treat the Tolstoy–Gandhi link as a simple influence chain misread both thinkers.

(Mantena, Karuna, 2012, "Another Realism: The Politics of Gandhian Nonviolence," *APSR*, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/another-realism-the-politics-of-gandhian-nonviolence/7BE21CF751176FAF880BD41A9E5EF10F; also summary lecture: https://blogs.law.columbia.edu/uprising1313/karuna-mantena-theoretical-foundations-of-satyagraha/)

### Rosamund Bartlett, *Tolstoy: A Russian Life* (2010)

Bartlett's biography covers the late-period Tolstoy (the letter falls in his final three years) and the Gandhi correspondence. The biography is described as paying "close attention to the later, polemical phase" of Tolstoy's career, drawing on Russian sources newly accessible after the Soviet collapse. I could not extract her specific assessment of the letter from the accessible sources in this session. ## C. Marquee triangulation

### CONFIRMS: The letter shaped Gandhi's anti-violence position in South Africa

The confirmation is strong and multi-source. Gandhi said so himself: "what I had learned from Tolstoy has fructified a hundredfold." Hazama (2023) places the encounter with the letter as immediately preceding *Hind Svaraj* — written on the same return voyage. Guha covers the London encounter with revolutionary nationalists as the context in which the letter's message resonated. The asthabharati.org scholarly article (citing Bhattacharya and Tendulkar) notes Gandhi "requested Tolstoy's approval to print 20,000 copies" — the scale indicates he saw it as a movement document.

However, the dominant scholarly caution (Hazama especially) is that "shaped his South African position" is not the same as "produced satyagraha." The two get conflated in popular accounts.

### COMPLICATES 1: Tolstoy's non-resistance vs. Gandhi's satyagraha — a fundamental difference in structure

This is the central scholarly complication, supported by multiple independent sources:

- Tolstoy's non-resistance is **individual and anarchist**: refusal to participate in military service, courts, taxation, government. The Doukhobors are his model. It is withdrawal, not organized confrontation.
- Gandhi's satyagraha is **organized, mass, and politically tactical**: large-scale civil disobedience that directly confronts state power and seeks to change it. It requires discipline and strategic escalation.

Gandhi acknowledged this himself: "the non-resistance method I had cultivated and elaborated in South Africa was different from the non-resistance Tolstoy had written upon." (asthabharati.org, citing Bhattacharya.)

Mantena (2012) makes this the core of her argument: Gandhi's nonviolence is "political realism," not spiritual withdrawal. The distinction is not a minor inflection — it is a different theory of how social change happens.

The scholars who soft-pedal this distinction (many popular accounts, some devotional Gandhi scholarship) should be treated with skepticism. Watch for accounts that make Tolstoy the "origin" of satyagraha; this flattens Gandhi's independent innovations.

### COMPLICATES 2: The letter's reception by its intended audience — Das's rejection

Tarak Nath Das wrote a formal rebuttal, "Open Letter to Count Leo Tolstoy in Reply to His 'Letter to a Hindoo,'" dated **16 October 1909** (preserved at SAADA, South Asian American Digital Archive, Norwich University collection). Key arguments from Das's reply:

- "Non-violence is an absolute Dogma…violence and benevolence are measured by the relative value of the actions and motives underlying them."
- "Resistance to despotism is [the] first of all human duties."
- Non-violent resistance was futile against a militarily entrenched empire like Britain's.

Das also wrote a longer four-part response titled "Young India's Reply to Count Tolstoy," stressing that "tyranny under any guise could never be justified."

This is the sharpest available counter-evidence to the letter's main claim. Das had originally sought Tolstoy's endorsement for the independence struggle — he was a member of Anushilan Samiti (preaching revolutionary violence) and co-founder (later) of the Ghadar Party. He wanted international moral authority for armed resistance. Tolstoy's letter delivered the opposite: a lecture that India's subjection was self-imposed and that all organized resistance, including political agitation, was insufficient without individual spiritual renunciation. Das's rejection is documented and public; it is not merely an inference.

[NOTE: The SAADA item (https://www.saada.org/item/20120828-1086) returned 403 Forbidden in this session; the above is drawn from search summaries of the item's content. The document is confirmed to exist and to be dated 16 October 1909. The specific quotes require verification against the original.]

### COMPLICATES 3: Gandhi edited the letter — the reincarnation question

Tolstoy explicitly permitted Gandhi to omit the reincarnation material for publication, and Gandhi's preface notes he received the letter as a translation and translated it further himself. Gandhi's published version is therefore not simply Tolstoy's text but an edited/curated version shaped by Gandhi's own theological preferences. Scholars (Hazama, implicitly) note that Gandhi was already working to excise what he saw as sectarian Hindu elements from his framing — the reincarnation question is a small case of this broader editorial tendency.

### EXTENDS: Scholars rarely read the letter's specific text closely

The dominant scholarly pattern is to treat the Tolstoy–Gandhi link thematically (non-resistance → satyagraha, with acknowledgment of differences) without close reading of the letter's own argument structure. Specifically:

- The Spencer epigraph inversion — that the letter is structured as a direct rebuttal to the Herbert Spencer motto Das's journal ran ("Resistance to aggression is not simply justifiable but imperative; non-resistance hurts both Altruism and Egoism") — is not discussed in any source I found. [NOTE: the Spencer epigraph is mentioned in the primary dive context but did not appear in the Gutenberg HTML version I fetched; it may be in the Russian original/PSS text but not in all English translations. NEEDS REVIEW.]
- The letter's internal structure (seven sections with Krishna epigraphs from Bharati's book, building to the consent-as-subjection argument) is not analyzed in any scholarly source recovered here. Scholars cite the "thirty thousand men" passage but do not trace how the epigraph spine builds to it.
- The letter's dismissal of Vivekananda and Baba Bharati ("your Vivekanandas, Baba Bharatis, and others…") is not discussed in Gandhi scholarship (they were not directly relevant to Gandhi's Transvaal work). This means the dive has a reading-of-the-text finding that scholarship does not cover.

## D. Gap-fill facts

### Tarak Nath Das (1884–1958)

Full name: Taraknath Das (also written Tarak Nath Das). Born 15 June 1884, Kanchrapara, 24 Parganas, Bengal Presidency; died 22 December 1958, New York City. Member of Anushilan Samiti (preaching revolutionary violence against British rule) from his teens. Sailed to Japan 1905; arrived in Seattle 1906 (travelling as "Jogendranath Das" to evade British surveillance). After riots against Indian immigrants in Bellingham and Vancouver (1907), founded the journal *Free Hindustan* in Vancouver, 1907 — described as "the first South Asian publication in Canada, and one of the first in North America." A Gurumukhi edition (*Swadesh Sevak*) was launched simultaneously. Das later co-founded the Indian Independence League with Pandurang Khankhoje and helped establish the Ghadar Party alongside Lala Hardayal. Tried in the Hindu-German Conspiracy Trial (1918) — the Indo-German Plan to arm an Indian revolution during World War I — sentenced to 22 months at Leavenworth Penitentiary. Married Mary Keatinge Morse 1924. PhD from the University of Washington; later professor of political science at Columbia University and visiting faculty at Georgetown. (Wikipedia, "Tarak Nath Das"; Military Wiki; Scroll.in summary.)

PSS name variants ("Таракуатта Дас," "Free Hindustham/Hindusthan") are garbled. The correct name is Tarak Nath Das (or Taraknath Das); the correct journal title is *Free Hindustan*. Both confirmed across multiple sources.

### Baba Premananda Bharati (1858–1913)

Born Surendranath Mukherjee, Dhaka, 1858, into a prominent Bengali colonial family. Trained as a journalist (University of Calcutta; worked for *Lahore Tribune* in the 1880s, where he knew Rudyard Kipling). Initiated into ascetic renunciation 1890 by Brahmananda Bharati; joined the Hari Sabha (Vaiṣṇavite group). Arrived New York 1902; established the city's first Krishna temple in Los Angeles 1911. Published *Sree Krishna: The Lord of Love* (The Krishna Samaj, New York, 1904) — described as the first full-length work presenting theistic Hindu practices to a Western audience by a practicing Hindu missionary. Tolstoy translated passages from this book into Russian and used extracts as epigraphs in "A Letter to a Hindu." Also published *The White Peril*, critiquing Western imperialism. Returned to India 1911 due to failing health; died 1913. (hindoohistory.substack.com, "Baba Bharati: Journalist, Swami, Anti-Colonial Agitator"; scottpresso.wordpress.com; search summaries.)

### Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902)

Born Narendranath Datta, Calcutta, 1863. Chief disciple of the mystic Ramakrishna. Famous for his address to the 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago (opened: "Sisters and Brothers of America"), which introduced Vedanta to a Western audience. Founded the Ramakrishna Math and Ramakrishna Mission (1897). Credited with making Hinduism a "world religion" and introducing Vedanta and Yoga to the West. Died 1902, aged 39. In "A Letter to a Hindu," Tolstoy names him dismissively as one of the "new interpreters" whose "explanations and justifications of old religious superstitions" Indians do not need. Tolstoy's critique is of Vivekananda's project of presenting Hindu metaphysics as a universal spiritual system — which Tolstoy viewed as intellectualist mystification rather than the plain ethical law of love. (Wikipedia, "Swami Vivekananda"; multiple encyclopaedia sources.)

### Krishna epigraphs — source confirmed

The letter uses multiple epigraphs attributed to "Krishna" (with page citations such as "P. 212") derived from Baba Premananda Bharati's *Sree Krishna: The Lord of Love* (1904). This is confirmed: multiple sources state Tolstoy "translated passages of Bharati's book into Russian and incorporated excerpts" into the letter. The Gutenberg HTML version opens with a Krishna epigraph ("Whenever thou feelest that thy feet are becoming entangled…"). The page-number citations in the original letter (e.g., "P. 212") refer to Bharati's book. The book is available at Project Gutenberg (https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/67019) and the Internet Archive (https://archive.org/details/sreekrishnalordo00premiala); specific pages can be verified against the cited passage text. ### German translation

*Brief an einen Hindu*, trans. Dr. A. Škarvan (Albert Škarvan, 1869–1926), foreword by Dr. E.H. Schmitt (Eugen Heinrich Schmitt, 1851–1916). Published by Waibel, Heidelberg and Leipzig, 1910. XII + 24 pages. Described as an "authorized translation." (ZVAB bibliographic record; search results confirmed consistently.)

Albert Škarvan was a Slovak physician, writer, and translator; he was Tolstoy's personal doctor and translated some of his works. (Wikipedia, "Albert Škarvan.") He is not a direct collaborator in the usual Chertkov/Free Age Press network — his involvement in the German edition is via a separate Austro-German Tolstoyan circle.

Eugen Heinrich Schmitt (Hungarian-German: Jenő Henrik Schmitt) was a Hungarian-German anarchist journalist and religious philosopher. He founded the journal *Die Religion des Geistes* (Leipzig, 1894–96), which included Tolstoy among its authors; edited the German-Hungarian anarchist weekly *Ohne Staat* (Budapest, from 1897). He developed a philosophy he called "Neugnosis" and consistently advocated non-violence and disobedience to state and church authority. He had to flee Austria in 1908 following involvement in a Hungarian agrarian socialist movement. He was a prominent figure in the German Tolstoyan reception. (de.Wikipedia "Eugen Heinrich Schmitt"; biographien.ac.at; anthrowiki.at.)
