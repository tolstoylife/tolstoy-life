## 1. Grounded in primary / in-circle sources

*(Biryukov vol. III ch. 6 + ch. 19; Sofya Andreyevna's reminiscences as quoted by
Biryukov; Tolstoy's letters quoted in the same. English from the platform's own
translation in `docs/research/doukhobors/biryukov-vol3/en/`.)*

The authorized biography and S. A. Tolstaya's reminiscences give a continuous,
first-hand account that the dive should treat as ground truth. Five things stand out.

**The drama was made *for* the people's theatre, fast, by a man recovering from
near-fatal illness.** Biryukov dates the first act to 20 October 1886 and reports
the whole play "written in two weeks" between A. A. Stakhovich's two visits — the
visit that lit the fuse being Stakhovich's reading-aloud of Ostrovsky: "By your
reading you stirred me up. After you, I wrote a drama" (Tolstoy to Stakhovich, in
Biryukov III.6). The intent was explicitly popular-theatre, not literary: in S. A.
Tolstaya's words, "He had wished simply to write a drama for the popular theatre,
and thought it would be given in the show-booths. 'Had I known it would please so,
I should have tried to write it better,' L. N. said jokingly." (Biryukov III.6,
quoting the 1912 *Tolstoy Yearbook*.) Biryukov names the plot source: "the theme of
this drama is taken from real life and was related to L. N. by Nik. Vas. Davydov, at
that time the prosecutor of the Tula circuit court."

**The censorship account is told from inside the household.** S. A. Tolstaya
records the Savina benefit-performance collapse first-hand: Savina came to Moscow to
ask permission, Tolstoy "readily agreed, but on 2 April 1887 a telegram was received
from Savina saying that the play had been banned by the censorship, and not only for
the theatre but for printing as well." She wrote "a perplexed letter to Feoktistov,
the head of press affairs" — the same E. M. Feoktistov who would later ban *The
Kreutzer Sonata* (cf. the Kreutzer dive's censorship layer) — who answered that "in
*The Power of Darkness* there was a cynicism of expressions impossible for the nerves
of the stage," but gave "no answer whatever" on the print ban. On the print
permission finally granted, she notes the cuts: "only the epigraph from the Gospel
and two or three expressions sharp with respect to the church were struck out." She
gives a sales figure — "in three days, in a quantity of 250,000 copies" (note: the
PSS apparatus the dive treats as ground truth says 100,000+; the two figures should
be flagged, not silently merged). The stage ban dragged: A. Potekhin's letters to
her (12 and 22 March 1887) report the Alexandrinsky rehearsals halted "through the
Ministry of the Court… All the actors are terribly distressed."

**The court reading is documented as a near-success that the regime then reversed.**
Biryukov prints A. A. Stakhovich's own letter describing his reading before Alexander
III on 27 January 1887: the Tsar wrote down the dramatis personae himself, the 4th
act "gripped everyone," and after the 5th "all were silent for a long while, until
the voice of the Sovereign rang out. 'A wonderful thing'" — «Чудная вещь» — "and
these two words unsealed everyone's lips," with rapturous talk "of Nikita's heartfelt
confession, of Akim's holy joy." This is the primary-record anchor for the marquee:
the court audience read the play's moral centre exactly as the marquee hypothesis
locates it (Nikita's confession + Akim's truth), *before* Pobedonostsev's 18 Feb
letter reversed the Tsar.

**The peasant-reception complication is in the primary record, in Stakhovich's own
telling.** This is the single most important fact for testing the marquee honestly.
Biryukov quotes Stakhovich on the Yasnaya Polyana reading to "no fewer than forty
peasants": "The peasants listened in silence. Only one — Mikh. Fom., the butler —
noisily expressed his rapture in loud guffaws." Asked how he liked it, the elderly
ex-pupil answered only: "'How shall I put it to you, Lev Nikolaevich. Mikita managed
the business cleverly at first… but then he muffed it…' Tolstoy asked nothing more
of anyone…" Tolstoy was "out of spirits" that evening, blaming the butler's guffaws
for keeping "the peasants from rightly understanding the merit of the play." The
plain reading: the actual peasant audience did not grasp the moral architecture the
salons and the court seized on at once.

**Tolstoy's own recorded attitude was discomfort at the salon success.** To Strakhov:
"of late I am decidedly tormented by the consequences of my drama. Had I known how
much time this would take from me, I should never have printed it. A strange folk,
the people of our circle!" And to N. N. Ge (December 1886): "no sooner do others
begin to praise (as it is now with me over the drama) than at once there appears the
wish for a personal reward… a stupid self-satisfaction." The success he got was the
success of "our circle," not the people he wrote for.

**The delayed staging, and the Moscow students.** Biryukov III.6 dates the first
imperial-theatre performance to St Petersburg, 18 October 1895, "for the benefit of
the actress Vasilyeva" (an amateur society staging by "Madame Priselkova" had
preceded it in 1890). Biryukov III.19 records the Moscow Maly Theatre premiere "in
the winter of 1896": after it, per P. A. Sergeenko, "a crowd of students set off
straight from the theatre to the Khamovniki Lane, to L. N. Tolstoy, in order to
express to him their feelings of gratitude" — Tolstoy "fell into the utmost
confusion" and "for some time could not speak." (Note: the educated young, again —
not the peasantry.)

**In-circle framing of the play's meaning.** Biryukov names the moral centre in his
own voice: "the depth of the idea expressed in it — that lingering force of sin and
the brilliance of truth in the humble form of Akim." In the English edition of his
*Life of Tolstoy* (Cassell), Biryukov glosses the play as one in which "crime is
committed by a man not evil by nature, but who has become entangled in an illicit
alliance" — the chain-of-sin reading, stated from inside the circle.

## 2. Received scholarly view (attributed, per-topic)

*Each topic below names who holds the view. The dive's own voice does not adopt
"naturalist" or "temperance play" as settled labels — both are reported as readings.*

### 2.1 — The naturalism / Zola question (contested, not settled)

The "naturalist" label is the most persistent and the one to handle most carefully.
- **English Wikipedia** files the genre flatly as "Naturalistic drama" and asserts
  the play "offers a cold and unsparing look at Russian peasant life."
- The **German reception** of the 1880s–90s grouped Tolstoy "along with Zola and
  Ibsen… as a teacher of the naturalist movement" (per the naturalism-survey
  literature); the young German naturalists took from him "the fearless love of
  truth in depicting everyday life… the courageous ability not to retreat before
  depicting the most terrible events."
- The **Paris debate of 1888** is the contemporary fault line: Antoine's Théâtre
  Libre staging split French letters — **Dumas *fils*, Sardou and Augier** argued
  the play "could only be read and not performed," while **Émile Zola and Melchior
  de Vogüé** backed Antoine. So Zola did not claim the play *as* naturalism so much
  as defend its stageability; the equation "Tolstoy = Russian Zola" is the German
  critics' framing, not Zola's.
- The strongest **push-back** is from inside the Russian theatre: **Konstantin
  Stanislavski**, who staged it at the Moscow Art Theatre (opened 5 December 1902),
  drew the line explicitly — "Realism only becomes Naturalism when it is not
  justified by the artist from within," and judged that "the external realism of the
  production of *The Power of Darkness* revealed the absence of inner justification."
  This is a practitioner *rejecting* the naturalist reading of his own staging.
- The most useful scholarly synthesis is **Jefferson J. A. Gatrall, "Child Murder,
  Peasant Sins, and the Infantilizing of Evil in Tolstoy's *The Power of Darkness*"
  (*Theatre Survey* 49.2, Nov. 2008, pp. 253–275)**: Gatrall argues the play's
  "oft-disparaged moralism proves no less groundbreaking than its cutting-edge
  naturalism" — i.e. the moral frame and the naturalist surface *work together*
  rather than the moralism being a flaw bolted onto a naturalist play. He inverts
  the usual emphasis on the chain-of-sin proverb, redirecting "focus from causation
  to victimhood, foregrounding suffering rather than transgression's origins," and
  reads the murdered child as the play's "moral and artistic climax."
- **Aylmer Maude** (translator/biographer) and **Ernest J. Simmons** treat the
  realism as necessity, not movement-affiliation: Simmons defends even the dialect —
  "Though he has been criticized for reproducing a peasant dialect in the play, he
  had no alternative in the case of the men and women he portrayed" (*Introduction
  to Tolstoy's Writings*, 1968, ch. 11).

**Net:** "naturalist" is a real and contemporary description (German critics,
Wikipedia genre tag, the play's unflinching surface), but it is contested by a
practitioner (Stanislavski), complicated by a modern scholar (Gatrall), and was never
the way the in-circle sources or Tolstoy described the work. Report it as a reading.

### 2.2 — "Folk drama / art for the people" and the relation to *What Is Art?*

- **Sizova (Irina I. Sizova, "Little-known Details from the History of the Creation
  of L. N. Tolstoy's Play *The Power of Darkness*…," *Literaturnyi fakt* /
  *Literary Fact*, no. 28, 2023)**, working from Russian State Archive (RGALI)
  materials, situates the play directly inside the "declaration of literary and
  critical thought of the 2nd half of the 19th century about the nationality /
  accessibility of art [народность], the active creator of which was Tolstoy." She
  documents M. V. Lentovsky's role as the **Skomorokh** people's-theatre
  entrepreneur (theatre active 1882–1897), the 1886–87 and 1895 stagings, and shows
  via N. A. Popov, Chertkov's correspondence, and R. M. Khin-Goldovskaya's diary that
  the "accessibility of art" declaration was itself "contested and redefined" around
  the staging.
- The relation to ***What Is Art?* (1897–98)** is the marquee's load-bearing claim
  and is best supported through the project's own [What Is Art? dive]: that treatise
  defines art as the "infection" (заражение) of feeling and ranks as *good* art the
  two kinds that are universally accessible — "religious (love of God and neighbour)
  and universal (the simplest feelings common to everyone)." *The Power of Darkness*
  (1886), built in peasant speech for the show-booth stage and turning on Akim's
  inarticulate truth and Nikita's confession, is a plausible *practice* of that later
  *theory*. But note the project's own caution (Kreutzer/What-Is-Art dives): the
  scholarship that ties practice to theory is largely the dive's synthesis, not a
  claim mainstream criticism makes explicitly — so it is offered as a reading, with
  the peasant-reception complication (§1, §3) attached.

### 2.3 — The temperance / anti-vodka reading

- The play's surface plainly foregrounds drink: popular and reference summaries
  describe the drama as revolving around "excessive drinking, greed, murder,
  repentance and forgiveness." Tolstoy founded a temperance society — the **Union
  Against Drunkenness** (Согласие против пьянства), c. 1887 — in the same period, and
  "hoped to affix a label to all vodka bottles marking them as poison."
- But the in-circle record does **not** present the play as a temperance tract: S. A.
  Tolstaya and Biryukov frame it as a chain-of-sin / power-of-evil drama (Akim's
  truth, Nikita's confession), not an anti-alcohol message play. The temperance
  reading is real but secondary — drink is *one* of the entangling sins, not the
  thesis. Report it as a thematic strand, not the play's purpose.

### 2.4 — The Théâtre Libre premiere and the European career

- **World premiere abroad: André Antoine's Théâtre Libre, Paris, 10 February 1888**,
  as *La Puissance des Ténèbres* — French sources place it at the **Théâtre des
  Menus-Plaisirs** (English-language sources sometimes say Théâtre Montparnasse; the
  discrepancy should be flagged). Translation **by Pavlovsky and Oscar Méténier**
  (per English Wikipedia; the French Wikipedia article omits the translators).
  Antoine himself played **Akim**. Antoine's programme of premiering banned/new work
  (Ibsen, Hauptmann, Strindberg, Verga, Zola) is the context.
- **European-before-Russia:** the recovered sources confirm the *principle* (the play
  ran in major European theatres while still banned at home) but only firmly document
  **Paris 1888**. Specific Berlin / Geneva / Milan productions and their dates were
  **not confirmed** in this sweep and are flagged `needsReview` — do not assert them.
- **Russia:** banned for the stage until Nicholas II lifted it; first Russian
  performances Oct–Nov 1895 (St Petersburg 18 Oct 1895 per Biryukov; Maly Theatre
  Moscow winter 1896 per Biryukov III.19); Stanislavski's MAT production opened
  5 December 1902.

### 2.5 — The variant fourth act and the staging of the infanticide

- The infanticide (Nikita crushing the newborn under a board in the cellar) is the
  scene the censors fixed on and the one Tolstoy reworked. **English Wikipedia /
  Stanislavski sources:** Stanislavski "had persuaded Tolstoy to rewrite act four
  along lines that Stanislavski had suggested" (for an intended 1895 production that
  did not materialise), and Tolstoy "agreed to provide an alternative for the play's
  most powerful and horrific scene" to satisfy the censor. The softer variant moves
  the killing offstage / narrates rather than enacts it.
- **French editions** are catalogued in two states — "without the variant of the 4th
  act" and "with the text of the variant of the 4th act" — confirming a stable,
  published alternative Act 4 exists (French Wikipedia).
- **Gatrall (2008)** is the scholarly anchor: the murdered child is the play's "moral
  and artistic climax," which is precisely why the scene was both the censors' target
  and the artistic core — cutting/softening it is not a neutral edit.

### 2.6 — Title (Luke 22:53) and epigraph (Matthew 5:28–29)

- The title renders Christ's words at his arrest, **Luke 22:53** ("this is your hour,
  and the power of darkness"). The **epigraph from Matthew 5:28–29** (the Sermon on
  the Mount — lust in the heart, "if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out") was
  **struck out by the censor** from the printed text: S. A. Tolstaya's first-hand
  confirmation — "only the epigraph from the Gospel and two or three expressions
  sharp with respect to the church were struck out" (Biryukov III.6). The Matthew
  epigraph ties the play's sexual-sin root to the same Gospel verse (Matt. 5:28) that
  grounds *The Kreutzer Sonata*'s chastity argument (cf. the Kreutzer dive) — a
  doctrinal through-line worth noting.
- The **subtitle is the proverb** «Коготок увяз — всей птичке пропасть» — Simmons
  renders it "If a Claw Is Caught, the Bird Is Lost" and calls it the work's
  thematic weight, "the terrible evil-begetting power of evil" (1968, ch. 11).

### 2.7 — Biographers' treatments (roster)

- **Pavel Biryukov** (authorized biography, vol. III chs 6 & 19) — *primary in-circle*;
  the household account (Davydov source, two-week composition, Savina/Feoktistov ban,
  the 27 Jan 1887 court reading, the Yasnaya peasant non-comprehension, the 1895/1896
  stagings). The dive's baseline.
- **Aylmer Maude** — translator (with Louise Maude) of the standard English text and
  biographer; treats the realism as necessity; warmest English voice on Tolstoy's art
  generally (cf. What-Is-Art dive).
- **Ernest J. Simmons** — *Introduction to Tolstoy's Writings* (Chicago, 1968), ch. 11
  on the dramatic writings: confirms the Davydov crime source ("a peasant confessed…
  that he had murdered a child"), the bedridden composition, the proverb-subtitle, the
  dialect defence, Akim's confession line, and the Pobedonostsev reversal ("negation
  of ideals," "too realistic and frightful").
- **Henri Troyat** — *Tolstoy* (trans. Nancy Amphoux, Doubleday, 1967) — biographical
  context for the writing period and Tolstoy's intentions for the play.
- **A. N. Wilson** — *Tolstoy* (1988), the standard modern English literary biography.
- **Rosamund Bartlett** — *Tolstoy: A Russian Life* (2010), draws on post-Soviet
  sources; names *Vlast tmy* a "harrowing play about peasant life" among the late work.

## 3. Marquee triangulation

> **MARQUEE:** *The Power of Darkness* enacts Tolstoy's chain-of-sin doctrine
> («коготок увяз — всей птичке пропасть») in peasant speech, and locates redemption
> not in the naturalist horror (the infanticide that drove the censors) but in
> Akim's inarticulate moral truth and Nikita's public confession — i.e. it is "art
> for the people" *practised* a decade before *What Is Art?* (1897–98) *theorised* it.

**Classification: `confirms` (the doctrine + the locus of redemption) + `extends`
(the practice→theory claim), with one strong `complicates` that the dive must carry
openly (the achieved reception was salon/court, not peasant).**

**What the evidence confirms.**
1. *The chain-of-sin doctrine is the play's spine, and it is named as such from
   inside the circle, not imposed by the marquee.* Tolstoy made the proverb the
   subtitle; Biryukov in his own voice calls the idea "that lingering force of sin and
   the brilliance of truth in the humble form of Akim," and his English biography
   glosses the play as crime by "a man not evil by nature… entangled in an illicit
   alliance." Simmons reads the proverb-subtitle as the work's whole thematic weight.
   The doctrine-in-peasant-speech reading is solidly grounded.
2. *Redemption is located in Akim's truth + Nikita's confession — and the best
   audience read it exactly there.* The single strongest piece of primary evidence is
   the court reading of 27 Jan 1887 (Stakhovich's letter in Biryukov III.6): after
   the 5th act the talk was "of Nikita's heartfelt confession, of Akim's holy joy."
   The marquee's claim about *where* the play puts redemption is confirmed by the
   contemporary response, not just by the dive's reading.

**What the evidence extends.**
3. *The "practice-before-theory" link to* What Is Art? *is plausible and the dive can
   make it — but as a synthesis, not a received finding.* The 1886 play is built for
   the show-booth audience in folk speech and stakes everything on a feeling
   universally legible (a father's grief, a man's confession) — which is precisely
   what the 1897–98 treatise will later define as *good*, universally-accessible art
   ("infection" + the simplest common feelings). Sizova (2023) independently places
   the play inside the *народность*/accessibility-of-art declaration "the active
   creator of which was Tolstoy," which supports the continuity. This is an `extends`:
   the corpus links a practice (1886) to a theory (1898) that no single mainstream
   source draws as sharply — so it is the dive's contribution, attributed as such.

**What the evidence complicates — and this is the honest heart of the test.**
4. *"Art for the people" describes Tolstoy's intent far better than the play's
   achieved reception.* The primary record is unambiguous and self-incriminating:
   the play was written *for* the people's theatre, yet the forty Yasnaya Polyana
   peasants who heard it "listened in silence," only the butler guffawed, and the
   best a thoughtful peasant listener could say was that Mikita "managed the business
   cleverly at first… but then he muffed it" — Tolstoy "asked nothing more of anyone"
   and was "out of spirits." Meanwhile the play triumphed in aristocratic salons, at
   the imperial court ("Чудная вещь"), and — when finally staged — among Moscow
   *students* who mobbed Tolstoy's gate. Tolstoy himself recoiled from this: the
   success was that of "the people of our circle," and it "tormented" him. So the
   marquee's "art for the people" must be qualified: as *intent and method*, yes; as
   *achieved reception*, the evidence points the other way. The play theorised in
   *What Is Art?* as universally accessible was, in its own first hearing by actual
   peasants, **opaque** — which is itself a quiet complication of the *What Is Art?*
   thesis, not only of this play's marquee.

**What the evidence does *not* support (guard against over-claiming).**
5. *The naturalism label should not be smuggled in as the dive's own.* The marquee
   correctly puts redemption *not* in "the naturalist horror." Good — but the dive
   should resist both the German-critic equation (Tolstoy = Zola) and the flat
   Wikipedia genre tag. The most defensible position is Gatrall's (2008): the moral
   frame and the naturalist surface are not rivals; the murdered child is at once the
   "cutting-edge naturalism" and the "moral and artistic climax." And Stanislavski —
   the man who staged it — explicitly denied that its realism was naturalism "not
   justified from within." Naturalism is a reading to report, not a verdict to adopt.

**One-line verdict for the dossier:** the marquee `confirms` (doctrine + locus of
redemption, grounded in Biryukov, Simmons, and the court reading) and `extends` (the
1886-practice → 1898-theory continuity, the dive's own synthesis backed by Sizova),
but is `complicates`d by the peasant-reception record: *art for the people* is
Tolstoy's intent and method, not the play's first achieved reception, which was
salon, court, and student — a gap Tolstoy felt and recorded himself.
