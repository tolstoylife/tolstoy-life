# Scholarly sweep — 1903 folk tales (Phase 3)

*Scope: secondary/English-first web sweep, 2026-06-11. Not an adversarial harness —
mainstream scholarship is contrast material, not a baseline to confirm.*

---

## A. Received view

### How the 1903 tales are treated in general scholarship

The 1903 tales — «Асархадон», «Три вопроса», «Труд, смерть и болезнь», «Это ты», and
«Разрушение ада» — are almost never the *object* of a study. They surface instead as
support material inside broader discussions of Tolstoy's pogroms response, his nonviolence
ethics, or his anticlericalism. The dominant English-language container is Aylmer Maude's
1906 preface to *Twenty-Three Tales*, where the three tales contributed to the Kishinev
anthology get a single sentence: Maude notes Part VII "consists of stories Tolstoy
contributed in aid of the Jews left destitute after the massacres…in 1903" and says
nothing further about their literary or philosophical content (Maude 1906 preface,
marxists.org). The tales are thus classified from the start as *occasioned* charity texts,
not independent works of art.

The 1968 chapter "Tolstoy's Later Short Stories" (anonymous scholarly chapter, *Introduction
to Tolstoy's Writings*, 1968, ourcivilisation.com) is the closest thing to specialist
treatment of the late folk tales found in this sweep. It discusses the three 1903 pogrom
tales by title, frames them alongside the broader народные рассказы project, and identifies
a real tension: Tolstoy "strove to retain the artlessness of folk literature" while
embedding his moral convictions, and some tales suffer when "the instructional purpose
remains too close to the surface." This is the canonical "didactic risk" framing. The
chapter does not, however, judge the 1903 tales to have *fallen* into that trap — it simply
notes the general risk.

One more recent data point: Peyman Vahabzadeh's *Violence and Nonviolence: Conceptual
Excursions into Phantom Opposites* (University of Toronto Press, 2019) includes a section
on «Асархадон» and «Три вопроса» as illustrations of Tolstoy's nonviolence metaphysics.
This is the only monograph-level treatment found in this sweep that engages both tales
directly on philosophical rather than biographical grounds.

«Разрушение ада» receives even thinner treatment in English scholarship: it appears in
discussions of Tolstoy's 1901 excommunication and anticlericalism, rarely analysed as a
literary object. The Ukrainian scholar I. Y. Tishchenko published a dedicated article
("Artistic parameters of socio-political and religious-moral foresight in the legend
'Destruction of Hell and Its Restoration'," *Science and Education: A New Dimension.
Philology*, V(37), Issue 137, 2017) — the only specialist study found — but the full text
was inaccessible (403 Forbidden) so its argument cannot be characterized beyond the
abstract-level claim that it examines "the prognostic function of literature" via
genre, composition, style, image-system, and narrative.

### The Kishinev response — received account

All major biographical sources agree on the broad outline: after the April 1903 pogrom,
Tolstoy signed a collective letter of protest from Moscow intellectuals to the Kishinev
mayor, cabled a North American newspaper blaming "the government, in the first place for
excluding the Hebrews…from the common law, and in the second place for forcefully inspiring
the Russian people to substitute idolatry for Christianity" (Kishinev pogrom, Wikipedia),
and contributed three tales to Sholom Aleichem's Yiddish relief anthology *Hilf: a
Zaml-Bukh fir Literatur un Kunst* (Warsaw, published 1903 or 1904 — sources disagree,
see Completeness check below). Alexandra Popoff (russianliteratureandbiography.com, 2013)
emphasizes Tolstoy's active engagement and quotes his 6 May letter to Aleichem; she
explicitly argues against accounts (Honig, Bernstein) that claim Tolstoy was reluctant
or dismissive. Benjamin Ivry (*The Forward*, 2014) acknowledges the contribution but
situates it inside Tolstoy's decades-long contradictory record on Jews, citing Harold
Schefski's 1982 article "Tolstoi and the Jews" and L. G. Barats' 1961 study. Schefski
(per Ivry) argues that Tolstoy — despite the Kishinev intervention — was throughout his
career unable or unwilling to portray a significant Jewish character in his fiction.

### Vedanta / theosophy / unity-of-life

There is no scholarly article in this sweep that treats Tolstoy's engagement with «tat tvam
asi» and the Theosophical route specifically in connection with the 1903 tales. The general
picture established by Jeff Love's chapter "Eastern Religion" in *Tolstoy in Context*
(Cambridge University Press, ed. Orwin) is that Tolstoy turned to Buddhism, Daoism, and
Hinduism after his 1880s conversion to identify "universal wisdom about the good life" and
that his advocacy of self-resignation has roots in these Eastern traditions — but the
chapter (Love) does not mention the 1903 tales or the Theosophischer Wegweiser specifically
(chapter is paywalled; summary is the available evidence). Ajay Kamalakaran (Russia Beyond,
2014) confirms Tolstoy read the Vedas, Upanishads, Ramayana, and Mahabharata at Yasnaya
Polyana, citing Bruce Wilkinson as a Sanskrit scholar source; Schopenhauer's guided tour
through Buddhism and Hinduism (via Afanasy Fet's Russian translation of *The World as Will
and Representation*, 1870s) is cited as the gateway. Neither source reaches the 1903 tales.

The most specific scholarly articulation of the «Das bist du» → «Асархадон/Это ты» source
chain found in this sweep comes not from a Tolstoy scholar but from a 2023 paper presented
at the Barcelona Symposium on Comparative Semitics ("Tolstoy's 'Esarhaddon, King of
Assyria'," University of Barcelona / IPOA, author identified as "Sr. del Olmo" from PDF
metadata, 8 pp.). The paper discusses how the German fable "Das bist du" (*Theosophischer
Wegweiser*, Vol. V, No. 5, 1903, pp. 163–166, Theosophische Central-Buchhandlung, Leipzig)
expresses the idea that "all life is one" and that violence against another also impacts
the self. The full scholarly argument of the paper could not be extracted (binary PDF).

### «Разрушение ада» and anticlericalism

The standard account situates «Разрушение ада» inside Tolstoy's post-excommunication
anticlerical campaign: the 1901 excommunication, his "Reply to the Synod's Edict," and
his "Appeal to the Clergy" (1902, also called «Обращение к духовенству») form a cluster.
The MDPI article "Leo Tolstoy's Anticlericalism in Its Context and Beyond" (*Religions*,
2016, MDPI, doi:10.3390/rel7050059) addresses this complex but was inaccessible in this
sweep (403 Forbidden). The connection to the Shchegolyonok legend told to Tolstoy in 1879
is confirmed by the biographical record but no English-language scholarly article treating
that specific source chain was found. The censorship dates (banned 1906, 1910, 1913; first
printed in Russia 1917) are attested in the PSS commentary but no English scholarly article
in this sweep names those dates specifically.

## B. Triangulation candidates

- **The three tales as Kishinev response** (Асархадон, Три вопроса, Труд смерть болезнь).
  Conventional view (Maude 1906; Popoff 2013; Wikipedia): the tales are "aid literature" —
  charitable gestures, classified by their occasion. The corpus finding is that they are
  simultaneously the most concentrated expression of Tolstoy's unity-of-life metaphysics
  (the Vedantic kernel) and a deliberately simple teaching form aimed at a Yiddish-reading
  audience. The occasioned framing softens the philosophical weight.
  *Relation: **complicates** (the charity-text frame absorbs the metaphysics).
  Source: Maude 1906 preface; Popoff 2013.*

- **«Это ты» as near-translation of «Das bist du»** (Theosophischer Wegweiser, 1903 №5).
  Conventional view: not discussed in any English scholarly source found in this sweep
  beyond the Barcelona/IPOA 2023 paper stub. The tat tvam asi source chain — theosophical
  journal → Tolstoy → Posrednik 1906 — is confirmed as a factual matter but has received
  no literary-critical treatment in English scholarship.
  *Relation: **extends** (scholarship has not resolved the literary implications of Tolstoy
  choosing a theosophical translation of Vedanta, not a direct Upanishadic source).
  Source: IPOA 2023 paper (partial); search results citing Theosophischer Wegweiser V/5.*

- **«Три вопроса» genesis — the Leskov relay** (1887 sketch → Leskov «Час воли божией»
  1890 → Tolstoy 1903). Conventional view (Wikipedia article on «Три вопроса»; University
  of Toronto Tolstoy bibliography): confirmed, treated as a biographical note. No English
  scholarly article was found that interprets the Leskov relay as meaningful — i.e. that
  Tolstoy's dissatisfaction with Leskov's version (per the corpus finding) shaped his own
  1903 text.
  *Relation: **extends** (the corpus grounds this in Tolstoy's own letters; the relay's
  literary implications are unresolved in English scholarship).
  Source: Wikipedia "The Three Questions"; rvb.ru Leskov commentary.*

- **«Разрушение ада» as companion to «Обращение к духовенству»** and its censorship arc.
  Conventional view: grouped with post-excommunication anticlericalism, rarely analysed
  as a standalone literary work. The Tishchenko 2017 article exists but its argument is
  inaccessible. The PSS-documented censorship arc (1906/1910/1913/1917) is not treated in
  any English article found here.
  *Relation: **extends** (English scholarship has simply not engaged this work as a literary
  or political object; the corpus finding that it was conceived as an illustration to
  «Обращение к духовенству» is not reflected anywhere in the sweep).
  Source: Tishchenko 2017 (abstract only); MDPI Religions 2016 (inaccessible).*

- **Tolstoy's condemnation of the Kishinev pogrom — "одно правительство"** (the private
  letter blaming the government, too hot to print legally in Russia). Conventional view
  (Kishinev pogrom Wikipedia; Popoff 2013): his cable to the North American press blames
  the government. Ivry 2014 / Schefski 1982 reframe: even in the Kishinev moment, Tolstoy's
  engagement with Jews was non-fictional (letters, tales) rather than through fictional
  characters, and his wider record is contradictory. The corpus finding — that he explicitly
  said he could not publish in a legal Russian outlet what he told Aleichem — is not
  contradicted by scholarship but is underemphasized: the received account presents his
  response as simply "active," eliding the *censorship constraint*.
  *Relation: **complicates** (the constraint is real; framing it as unqualified moral courage
  soft-pedals the politics).
  Source: Ivry 2014; Schefski 1982 (via Ivry); Popoff 2013.*

- **Tolstoy's engagement with Vedanta / theosophy as gateway to unity-of-life**.
  Conventional view (Kamalakaran 2014; Love in *Tolstoy in Context*): Tolstoy read Hindu
  scripture broadly; Schopenhauer was the gateway; he advocated for self-resignation rooted
  in Eastern traditions. No source connects the *theosophical journal* as a specific vehicle.
  *Relation: **extends** (the scholarly account stays at the level of Hindu scripture in
  general; the mediation via German theosophy is not examined).
  Source: Love in *Tolstoy in Context* (Cambridge, ed. Orwin); Kamalakaran, Russia Beyond 2014.*

## D. Completeness check

**What is plausibly missing from the corpus sweep?**

1. **«Это ты» as a separate work** — the dive PRIMARY findings name it as a near word-for-word
   translation of «Das bist du»; the corpus sweep apparently captured it. But the relationship
   between «Это ты» and «Асархадон» is that *both* derive from the same theosophical source,
   and the two are usually grouped with the three Kishinev tales. «Это ты» was NOT sent to
   Sholom Aleichem and was published separately (Posrednik, 1906). Whether it belongs to the
   "1903 folk tales" cluster or stands alone as a 1903 theosophical parable is a classification
   question the corpus should resolve. *In scope.*

2. **Date of Hilf publication: 1903 or 1904?** Sources disagree. The Russian text at
   tolstoy-lit.ru and several encyclopedic sources say 1903 (Warsaw); Wikipedia's Sholem
   Aleichem article says 1904. The MEMORY notes (corpus finding) say 1903. This is a
   bibliographic open item, not a missing episode. *In scope; needs primary verification.*

3. **Tolstoy's 1903 letter to the Kishinev mayor** — mentioned in the dive PRIMARY findings
   and confirmed by Popoff (2013). The exact text of this letter and whether it is published
   in PSS is not confirmed by this sweep. *In scope; low risk of being absent from PSS Tom 74.*

4. **Tolstoy's 1903 diary entries on the pogrom and the tales** — the corpus sweep covered
   primary-source TEI; the diary record for April–August 1903 was not surfaced in the
   scholarship sweep. Likely covered in the corpus but worth confirming for the "odnotolko
   pravitelstvo" attribution. *In scope.*

5. **Gary Jahn's specific article on народные рассказы** — the 1990 *Russian Language Journal*
   article was referenced in the University of Toronto bibliography but full title and argument
   are not available from the web. This is the most likely English specialist treatment of the
   folk tales as a genre that the sweep missed. *In scope; recommend retrieval via library access.*

6. **Donna Tussing Orwin's treatment of 1903** — her major study (*Tolstoy's Art and Thought,
   1847–1880*, Princeton 1993) ends in 1880 and does not cover 1903. Her edited *Anniversary
   Essays on Tolstoy* (Cambridge) covers late works but her specific attention to the 1903
   tales is unconfirmed. *Borderline; low priority.*

## E. Framing flags

1. **"Aid literature" frame erases the metaphysics.** Maude (1906) and all downstream sources
   that call the three tales "stories Tolstoy contributed in aid of the Jews" implicitly classify
   them as occasion-driven charity gestures. This soft-pedals the fact that the tales carry
   Tolstoy's most compressed statement of unity-of-life doctrine (the tat tvam asi kernel via
   the Theosophischer Wegweiser) and that they were the *form* Tolstoy chose to answer a
   political atrocity — not a separable charitable act. Attribute this framing to Maude / the
   English editorial tradition; do not inherit it.

2. **"Active engagement" softens the censorship constraint.** Popoff (2013) argues against
   portrayals of Tolstoy as reluctant or dismissive re: the pogrom, and she is right to
   correct that. But the correction tips into presenting his response as freely expressive.
   The corpus finding — that he explicitly told Aleichem he could not say in a legal Russian
   publication what he was saying — adds a material fact the "active engagement" framing
   elides. The tales going out in Yiddish first, to a non-Russian audience, was not a neutral
   choice.

3. **Schefski 1982 / Léon Poliakov's "unable to identify with a Jew"** — this claim (Ivry
   2014) is about fictional characterization, not political action. It should not be used to
   diminish the Kishinev intervention, but it should not be ignored either. It sits alongside
   the tales, not against them: Tolstoy could write a parable about universal human unity
   (all are one) while being unable to inhabit a *particular* Jewish subjectivity in fiction.
   The tension is real and belongs in the wiki ingestion.

4. **"Didactic decline" risk.** The 1968 scholarly chapter identifies the "instructional
   purpose remaining too close to the surface" as a risk in the late folk tales. This is the
   most common soft-dismissal pattern in Tolstoy scholarship on the shorter late works. No
   source in this sweep explicitly applies it to the 1903 tales specifically, but the framing
   is latent. Attribute it precisely if it appears in ingestion sources; do not treat it as
   established for these tales.

5. **Excommunication as the master frame for «Разрушение ада».** Every source places «Разрушение
   ада» inside the 1901 excommunication story. This is not wrong, but it risks making the work
   derivative — a reaction to a church action — rather than an independent literary-theological
   argument. The dive finding that it was conceived as an *illustration to* «Обращение к
   духовенству», and that its source is an 1879 oral legend (Shchegolyonok), means the work
   has roots going back twenty years before the excommunication. The excommunication brought
   the political stakes into relief; it did not generate the work.
