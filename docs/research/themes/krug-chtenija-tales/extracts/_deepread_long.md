## 1. Корней Васильев

### Plot / content
A realist village tragedy across forty years, told in six chapters. **Корней Васильев** — a prosperous, proud, fleshly 54-year-old cattle-and-lumber trader — returns home to the village of **Гаи** after long absence. On the road the one-eyed driver **Кузьма**, plied with vodka, tells him the village gossip: his young second wife **Марфа** has taken her former lover **Евстигней Белый** on as a hired hand and lives with him. That night Корней confronts Марфа; she first denies it, then, after he beats her savagely — breaking two of her ribs, smashing her head, and wrenching the arm of the two-year-old daughter **Агашка (Агафья)** when the child runs to her mother — she throws the truth in his face: she does live with Евстигней, has lived with him, and Агашка is not Корней's child. Корней leaves before dawn and vanishes.

Chapter II jumps **seventeen years**. A bent old beggar with a grey beard but still-black brows is overtaken by a herd; the young woman driving it (married into a rich family in Андреевка) kindly invites him to lodge — she is the grown Агашка, her arm still crooked, and neither recognizes the other. Chapter III recounts Корней's ruin: after the beating he failed to buy the grove, drank, lost his three thousand roubles, sank from clerk to cattle-drover to wandering beggar, all the while blaming his "злодейка жена." Chapters IV–V: lodging with Агафья's family, he hears his own story told back to him by the talkative mother-in-law (that Корней "убить хотел" the child over the hired hand, who has since died); he weeps. He drags himself to Гаи, climbs the steps he once sat on eating snow, and meets Марфа — now a dry, wizened old woman who recognizes him and harshly drives him off as a beggar; his son **Федька** and the now-grown deaf nephew give him bread. The old hatred is gone; an "умиленная слабость" overcomes him. Chapter VI: back in Андреевка he dies on the stove-shelf, asking Агафья to forgive him and to tell Марфа that "the wanderer came to say goodbye." He hands over his soldier's ticket — "Слава богу, развязались все грехи." Марфa, unable to sleep, finally walks to Андреевка to make peace and bring him home — but arrives to find him already laid out, a crowd in the hut, the psalter being read. Reconciliation comes one hour too late.

### Genre assessment
Sustained realist psychological narrative: dense physical detail (the snow eaten off the porch rail, the deaf nephew's joy over the waistcoat, the chaly horse), free indirect interiority, a named real geography (Гаи, Андреевка, Черкассы, Москва, Киев, Новый Афон), and a non-schematic, irony-laden ending (forgiveness arrives too late; the dead face gives no answer). It is not a fable — there is no narrator-stated moral, no "once there was," no allegorical transparency.
**Verdict: realist `short_story`. Confidence: high.**

### Theme / doctrine
Repentance and the dissolution of hatred — the late-Tolstoyan conviction that the self-poisoning of anger can fall away and be replaced by an "умиленное, восторженное чувство смирения." Carried almost entirely through action and bodily detail, not statement: the seventeen-year ruin shows злоба consuming the man from inside; the turn is dramatized (he cannot raise the old hatred against the wizened Марфа, only weeps). The doctrine of mutual forgiveness is delivered by withholding it in time — the keystone irony is that repentance redeems the penitent inwardly while the human reconciliation is forever missed. Death as the loosening of sin ("развязались все грехи") frames it religiously without sermonizing.

### Keystone passages
1. **The cruelty hinge** (ch. I, the beating):
   > Корней ухватил девочку за руку, оторвал от матери и как котенка бросил в угол.

   Context: as Марфа cries "Разбойник! Ребенка убил" and tries to reach the daughter. This is the act whose consequence (the crooked arm) outlives everyone's memory of the cause and seals the tragedy.

2. **Марфа's revelation** (ch. I, climax of the confrontation):
   > И живу с Евстигнеем и жила. На, убей до смерти. И Агашка не твоя дочь; с ним прижила, — быстро выговорила она и закрыла локтем лицо, ожидая удара.

   The truth that drives Корней out of his own life; the pivot from prosperity to forty years' ruin.

3. **The hatred falls away** (ch. V, the reunion at the old house):
   > Корней привалился спиной к стене и, упираясь на клюку, пристально смотрел на нее и с удивлением чувствовал, что у него не было в душе той злобы на нее, которую он столько лет носил в себе, но какая-то умиленная слабость вдруг овладела им.

   The doctrinal turn dramatized — the lifelong злоба simply dissolves; this is the tale's spiritual hinge.

4. **The deathbed forgiveness** (ch. VI, dying, to Агафья):
   > Гаша, — сказал он тихим голосом, — время мое доспело. Я помирать хочу. Так вот ты прости меня Христа ради.

   The repentance made explicit — he asks forgiveness of the very child he maimed.

5. **The unanswerable ending** (ch. VI, last lines):
   > Ни простить, ни просить прощенья уже нельзя было. А по строгому, прекрасному, старому лицу Корнея нельзя было понять, прощает ли он, или еще гневается.

   The realist refusal of a tidy moral close — reconciliation missed by an hour, the dead face giving no verdict.

### Political / censorship-sensitive content
Minimal. This is a domestic-moral tale with no indictment of state, church, or capital punishment. The nearest "authority" note is Марфа's cry "Али на тебя суда нет?" (is there no court for you?) — a private appeal to justice, not a political charge. Low sensitivity.

## 2. За что?

### Plot / content
A historical tale (subtitled by setting "В 1830 году…"), drawn from the aftermath of the **1830–31 Polish uprising**, in twelve chapters. In 1830 the young Pole **Иосиф (Юзеф/Юзё) Мигурский** visits the estate **Рожанка** of old **пан Ячевский**, a Kościuszko-era patriot. He comes intending to propose to the elder daughter **Ванда** but falls instead for the younger, fifteen-year-old **Альбина** — vivid, life-loving, with wide-set blue eyes — and leaves without proposing. The Paris and Warsaw risings break out; Мигурский joins the Polish army (the victory at Stoczek), but the rising is crushed; he is stripped of his estate and sentenced to serve as a common soldier in a line battalion in **Уральск** (Uralsk). Альбина, who has inherited 300,000 złoty, defies her stepmother, travels to Uralsk, and marries him there (ch. V–VI). They have five years of relative happiness and two children — until both children die of fever within days for want of a doctor. Альбina is broken; she asks "За что? за что?" of men and God and gets no answer.

A fellow exile, **Росоловский**, arrives and recounts in cold detail the running-the-gauntlet execution of the conspirators of the **Сироцинский** plot — Doctor Шакальский and finally Сироцинский himself flogged through two battalions of soldiers, reciting the *Miserere*. This galvanizes Альбина: she devises an escape. Мигурский will fake his suicide by drowning in the Ural; she will obtain permission to take her dead children's coffins home, and instead of the coffins a box in the tarantass will carry the hidden Мигурский to Saratov, thence by boat down the Volga to the Caspian, Persia, freedom. The plan very nearly works. But the escorting Old-Believer Cossack **Данило Лифанов** — dutiful, devout, literal — hears a man's voice from the box, hears the little dog Трезорка whine at it, and from rigid loyalty to his oath denounces them to the police at Покровская слобода. Мигурский is seized, put in handcuffs; Альбина collapses with hysterical laughter, screaming "За что, за что?", crying "Всё я! Я одна виновата." Мигурский is sentenced to run the gauntlet of 1000, commuted (through Ванда's Petersburg connections) to perpetual Siberian exile; Альбина follows him. The Cossack, tormented by whether he did right, drinks away everything he owns. The tale closes on **Николай Павлович (Nicholas I)**, rejoicing that he has crushed the hydra of revolution and, flattered by men in stars and gilt uniforms, sincerely believing himself a great man whose life was a blessing — "на развращение и одурение которых были бессознательно направлены все его силы."

### Genre assessment
Fully realist historical fiction: dated, named, documented setting (1830 partition politics, Stoczek, Dwernicki's detachment, the Sirociński conspiracy, Nicholas I by name and characterization); rounded characters with interior development (Альбина's growth from girl to grieving wife to schemer; Мигурский's de-idealization into a flesh-and-blood "ягненок"); a slow-built suspense plot with a tragic reversal. No allegory, no fable schema, no timeless frame. The title-question "За что?" functions as a thematic refrain, not a parable device.
**Verdict: realist `short_story` (historical). Confidence: high.**

### Theme / doctrine
The destruction of innocent human happiness by the **state / autocratic power** — and the unanswerability of "За что?" (for what? / why?). The doctrine is anti-statist and anti-imperial: the Polish family wants nothing but to live as their forebears lived, and the machinery of empire (conquest, conscription-as-punishment, the gauntlet, the surveillance that defeats the escape) grinds them down. Carried through action and structure: the children's death (no doctor because of exile), the gauntlet narration, the foiled escape, and finally the cold portrait of the autocrat who caused it all and believes himself a benefactor. The Cossack Данило embodies a sub-theme: blind obedience to oath/authority as itself a destroyer — duty performed becomes a sin he must drink to forget.

### Keystone passages
1. **The cause named — power crushing the rising** (ch. III):
   > Опять бессмысленно повинующиеся десятки тысяч русских людей были пригнаны в Польшу и под начальством то Дибича, то Паскевича и высшего распорядителя Николая I, сами не зная, зачем они делают это, пропитав землю кровью своей и своих братьев поляков, задавили их и отдали опять во власть слабых и ничтожных людей, не желающих ни свободы, ни подавления поляков, а только одного: удовлетворения своего корыстолюбия и ребяческого тщеславия.

   The tale's explicit indictment of the regime: the state as senseless, blood-soaked coercion serving petty vanity. Central to the dive's "encryption" argument.

2. **"За что?" — the human cry against the state** (ch. VI, Альбина at the children's grave):
   > «За что? за что? — думала она. — И Юзё, и я — мы ничего ни от кого не хотим, кроме того, чтоб ему жить так, как он родился и жили его деды и прадеды, а мне только чтобы жить с ним, любить его, любить моих крошек, воспитывать их».

   The title-question located: the innocent want only to live as they were born, and power takes it from them.

3. **The gauntlet — state cruelty in documentary close-up** (ch. VII, Росоловский's account):
   > Два батальона солдат стояли в два ряда, длинной улицей, у каждого солдата в руке была гибкая палка, такой высочайше утвержденной толщины, чтобы три только могли входить в дуло ружья.

   The "высочайше утвержденной толщины" (thickness approved at the highest level) ties the torture directly to the sovereign's authority — the keystone of the censorship charge.

4. **Obedience-to-oath as destroyer** (ch. XII, the Cossack's question):
   > только на другую ночь, проснувшись в канаве, перестал думать о мучившем его вопросе: хорошо ли он сделал, донеся начальству о полячкином муже в ящике?

   The sub-theme: a good, devout man's literal loyalty to authority is what dooms the innocents — and poisons him.

5. **The closing portrait of the autocrat** (ch. XII, final paragraph):
   > Николай же Павлович радовался тому, что задавил гидру революции не только в Польше, но и во всей Европе, и гордился тем, что он не нарушил заветов русского самодержавия и для блага русского народа удержал Польшу во власти России.

   Nicholas I named and skewered — the human catastrophe authored by a man who believes himself a great benefactor. The most directly censorship-sensitive sentence in the three tales.

### Political / censorship-sensitive content
Very high. This tale is the political core of the cluster:
- **Names and condemns Nicholas I** by name, repeatedly, as vain, cruel, self-deceived, and as having unconsciously directed all his powers toward "развращение и одурение" (the corruption and stupefaction) of the Russian people.
- **Indicts the autocracy as a system** ("заветов русского самодержавия") and the conquest/retention of Poland as evil.
- **Depicts state torture** — running the gauntlet — in documentary detail, explicitly authorized "высочайше" (at the highest level).
- **Condemns conscription-as-punishment** and the surveillance state that crushes the escape.
- Catherine II ("апокалипсическая… блудница Екатерина II") and Poniatowski are also denounced (in Ячевский's voice).
A direct treatise saying this about a named Tsar would have been unpublishable; the fictional/historical frame is the encryption.

## 3. Божеское и человеческое

### Plot / content
A multi-strand tale set "в 70-х годах… в самый разгар борьбы революционеров с правительством," thirteen chapters, interweaving three lives. **(A)** A German **генерал-губернатор** of the southern region signs, among routine papers, the death warrant of **Анатолий Светлогуб**, a Novorossiysk University candidate condemned to hang for conspiracy to overthrow the government. His weak heart flutters; he could recall the order, but quiets his conscience with a proverb ("Ein jeder macht sich sein Bett…") and the memory of the Tsar's charge to him, then joins his wife's drawing-room guests, where a wit tells an anecdote that makes everyone laugh — while Светлогуб's mother, elsewhere, screams against God. **(B)** Светлогуб, a rich young man long ashamed of his privilege, had moved from philanthropy to народ-education to terrorism under the influence of **Меженецкий**; arrested after agreeing to hide dynamite for a disliked comrade (**Прохоров**), he sits two months in solitary, his nerve failing. A governor's wife leaves a Gospel in the cell; reading it transforms him — the Sermon on the Mount, "кто потеряет свою душу ради меня, тот сбережет ее," the grain of wheat that must die. He writes, burns (to protect Прохоров), and rewrites a letter of love and forgiveness to his mother; he goes to the gallows in an exalted, "умиленно-торжественное" peace, pities his executioners ("не знают, что творят"), and asks the hangman "и не жалко тебе меня?" — a question that haunts the executioner, who afterward refuses the office and drinks himself into the hospital. **(C)** An **old Old-Believer schismatic** ("Табачная держава"), seeking the true faith in the Book of Revelation, watches from his cell window as the radiant, lamb-like youth with a Gospel rides to execution and concludes "Этот познал истину."

The strands then track forward across fourteen years. **Меженецкий**, the revolutionary leader who recruited Светлогуб, is transferred toward Petersburg; the Old-Believer, also in transit, seeks him out to learn the youth's faith, but Меженецкий answers only with the creed of force and elected government — and the old man, deciding "не в том вера того юноши," bows and leaves. In the Peter-and-Paul fortress Меженецкий nearly goes mad in solitary, attempts suicide, then saves himself by an iron regime of disciplined imagination, living an entire fictional Petersburg life in his head for seven years. Transferred to Siberian katorga, at Krasnoyarsk he meets the **next generation of revolutionaries** — **Роман** and a contemptuous **brunette** — who dismiss his terrorism (the assassinations of Kropotkin [the governor], Mezentsov, Alexander II) as a string of errors that only produced reaction; the future, they say, is industrial proletarianization and Marxist organization ("Прочтите Каутского"). Humiliated, his life's meaning denied from both sides — by the lamb-faith of the dying Old-Believer and by the new doctrinaires — Меженецкий is seized again by murderous злоба and the old hallucinations. The dying Old-Believer, in the same prison, sends for him one last time to "open" the faith of the Lamb of Revelation; Меженецкий thinks him raving. That night the old man dies in radiant vision ("agnец победит я"), while Меженецкий, the corridor finally empty, takes the firewood-cord, makes a noose, and hangs himself. Their two bodies are laid side by side in the mortuary.

### Genre assessment
A complex, realist, multi-plot novella with strong ideological and visionary elements. It has rounded psychological characters (the general's fluttering heart and self-deception; Светлогуб's failing nerve and conversion; Меженецкий's seven-year imaginative regime and final collapse), a specific historical frame (the 1870s–80s народническая terror, named assassinations, Alexander II/III, the rise of Marxism), and dense realist surfaces (the drawing-room, the gauntlet-thickness papers, the execution procedure). The Old-Believer strand introduces a quasi-hagiographic / apocalyptic register (the "lamb," the Revelation citations, the dying vision) but it is embedded in a realist structure as one character's faith, not as the narrative's own allegorical machinery. The title — "Божeское и человеческое" (the divine and the human) — announces a thematic antithesis, but the tale dramatizes it through contrasted lives rather than schematizing it.
**Verdict: realist novella / `short_story` with a hagiographic-visionary strand — hybrid, but the dominant mode is realist. Confidence: high (realist core); the visionary Old-Believer thread is the one fable-adjacent element.**

### Theme / doctrine
The opposition of **divine law to human law** (the title): the human/state order — courts, gallows, the general's signature, the priest who speaks of mercy while serving the execution — versus the divine truth Светлогуб finds in the Gospel (give your soul rather than save it; love your enemies; the grain that dies bears fruit; "не знают, что творят"). Two paths to meaning are set against the state and against each other: Светлогуб's Gospel-love and the Old-Believer's apocalyptic faith in the Lamb both "conquer by good and humility," and both die at peace; Меженецкий's revolutionary force and злоба, and the new generation's Marxist science, both end in spiritual death (Меженецкий's suicide). Carried through action and juxtaposition — above all the structural rhyme of the two corpses in the mortuary: the man who died in radiant faith and the man who hanged himself in злоба, laid side by side. The doctrine is non-resistance / death-as-awakening: Светлогуб's transformation is the spiritual centre.

### Keystone passages
1. **Светлогуб's transformation — the Gospel turn** (ch. IV, reading the Gospel in the cell):
   > — Да, да, это самое! — вдруг вскрикнул он со слезами на глазах. — Это самое я и хотел делать. Да, хотел этого самого: именно, отдать душу свою; не сберечь, а отдать. В этом радость, в этом жизнь.

   The hinge of his conversion: the Sermon-on-the-Mount logic of giving (not saving) the soul becomes his own. The spiritual centre of the tale.

2. **The forgiveness of his betrayer / "they know not what they do"** (ch. IV, his letter to his mother):
   > И не сердись на них, ни на тех, с которыми я работал, ни на тех, которые казнят меня. Ни те, ни другие не могли иначе: прости им, они не знают, что творят.

   Светлогуб extends Gospel forgiveness to both comrades and executioners — the doctrine of non-resistance and enemy-love made personal.

3. **Death-as-awakening dramatized** (ch. V, going to the gallows):
   > «Смерть? Ну, так что же? Уйду. Да, это хорошо. Всё хорошо», думает Светлогуб, чувствуя, как возвращается к нему то умиленно торжественное состояние, в котором он был вчера.

   The transformed man meets execution as awakening, not extinction — the "божеское" pole of the title embodied.

4. **The human/divine antithesis — the executed youth recognized as truth** (ch. VI, the Old-Believer at the window):
   > «Этот познал истину, — думал он. — Антихристовы слуги затем и задавят его веревкой, чтоб не открыл никому».

   The state ("антихристовы слуги") named as the enemy of divine truth — the human law killing the bearer of the divine. Direct statement of the title's opposition.

5. **The two corpses — the structural verdict** (ch. XIII, final lines):
   > Тело Меженецкого снесли в мертвецкую и положили на нары рядом с телом старика раскольника.

   The closing image: the man who died in злоба and suicide beside the man who died in radiant faith — the divine and the human laid side by side. The tale's whole argument compressed into one sentence.

(Supplementary, the general's self-exculpation, ch. I — the "human law" pole:
> Я исполнитель высшей воли и должен стоять выше таких соображений
the bureaucrat of capital punishment quieting his own heart.)

### Political / censorship-sensitive content
Very high — arguably the most charged of the three on church + capital punishment + state:
- **Indicts capital punishment** in unsparing procedural detail: the general signing the death warrant over provision-transport accounts; the gallows, the hood, the hangman's work; the executioner destroyed by what he did ("и не жалко тебе меня"). Tolstoy's anti-death-penalty argument (cf. "Не могу молчать," 1908) in narrative form.
- **Indicts the autocrat**: the Tsar's personal charge to the general ("как ты не жалел себя на войне, ты так же решительно будешь поступать в борьбе с красными") makes the sovereign the author of the execution.
- **Indicts the church as servant of the state**: the priest who offers the cross "участвующему в совершаемом над ним деле," speaking of mercy while officiating at a hanging; the Old-Believer's "никонианскую церковь" and the "табачная держава" (the post-Petrine state-church called Antichrist).
- **Sympathetic interior of a revolutionary/terrorist** (Светлогуб, Меженецкий) and explicit depiction of the народническая terror and the assassination of Alexander II — politically explosive subject matter, handled with sympathy for the condemned and condemnation of the executing state.
- Note the **balance**: the tale also dramatizes the spiritual bankruptcy of revolutionary force itself (Меженецкий's злоба and suicide) and the cold doctrinairism of the new Marxists — so its political charge is not endorsement of revolution but condemnation of the whole human order of violence (state and revolutionary alike) against the divine law of love. This is the "encryption" at its most sophisticated: a fiction that could carry an anti-state, anti-death-penalty, anti-church argument a treatise could not publish, while refusing to endorse the revolutionaries the censor most feared.

## Cross-tale note for the dive

The hypothesis holds. Against the 1903 sibling cluster (all parables/legends), these 1905–06 tales swing **decisively back to realist fiction**:
- **Корней Васильев** — realist domestic `short_story` (repentance/forgiveness).
- **За что?** — realist historical `short_story` (the state destroys innocent happiness; Nicholas I named).
- **Божеское и человеческое** — realist multi-plot novella with one hagiographic-visionary strand (divine vs human law; capital punishment; the Gospel vs revolutionary force).

The "encryption" argument is strongest for the latter two: **За что?** and **Божеское и человеческое** carry, inside fiction, indictments of the named autocracy, of capital punishment, of state torture, and of the church-as-state-servant that a signed treatise could not have published in Russia. The recurring "За что?" cry links the two т. 42 tales thematically. **Корней Васильев** is the outlier — a private moral tale with no political charge — and should anchor the "repentance/forgiveness" rather than the "encryption" line of the dive.

A recurring **Old-Believer Cossack/raskolnik** figure appears in both т. 42 tales (Данило Лифанов the dutiful denouncer in *За что?*; the truth-seeking raskolnik in *Божеское и человеческое*), used to opposite ends — obedience-to-authority as destroyer vs. apocalyptic faith as the one who "познал истину." Worth flagging for the entity/theme layer.
