# Scholarly context — 1905–06 Krug chtenija tales (Phase-3 sweep)

Scope: the six недельное-чтение tales — Алёша Горшок, Корней Васильев, Молитва, Ягоды, За что?, Божеское и человеческое — plus Gorbunov-Posadov prosecution and English translation status. Ground in primary record; mainstream scholarship as contrast.

---

## Received view

English-language scholarship on Tolstoy's post-1880 short fiction clusters around a small canon: *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*, *Hadji Murat*, *Master and Man*, *Father Sergius*, *The Kreutzer Sonata*, *The Power of Darkness*, and the 1880s folk-parables (*What Men Live By*, *How Much Land Does a Man Need?*). The late weekly-reading tales written for Круг чтения (1905–06) fall almost entirely outside this canon and are treated, when noticed at all, as a minor appendage to the anthology's aphoristic apparatus.

The dominant frame has two layers. First, a genre-homogenisation: scholars who address the Krug chteniya tales at all tend to group the entire sequence of 52 недельные чтения as uniform "Sunday reading stories" — didactic vignettes, parables, moral sketches — without distinguishing the 1903 legend-cluster from the 1905–06 realist cluster. The Kirkus review of Sekirin's 2000 translation (*Divine and Human*, Zondervan) is representative of popular reception: it describes the stories as "heavily moralistic" with many structured as parables, praises Tolstoy's "clarity of expression and narrative economy," but concludes the collection is "not essential Tolstoy." *Publishers Weekly* (2000) labels them "lovely artifacts" offering "insight into Tolstoy's notions of wisdom and spirituality" — a spiritualising frame that displaces the political charge entirely. Neither reviewer distinguishes the realist historical tales from the fable-schema ones.

Second, a de-politicisation: the English reception of Круг чтения has been shaped by the anthology's English avatar — Peter Sekirin's *A Calendar of Wisdom* (1997, North Star), a selective translation of the aphoristic daily-reading material with the 52 weekly stories omitted entirely. That version was the dominant English point of access for a decade. The two 2000 translations (Sekirin, Zondervan; Spence, Northwestern) partially corrected this, but both are out of the main scholarly conversation: Spence's three-story volume (*What For?*, *Divine and Human*, *Berries*) is the single English-language edition to engage the political import head-on — his introduction, per the Northwestern catalogue, stresses "the political import and allegory" of tales written "around the time of the Russian revolution of 1905." It remains a small-press academic edition without wide secondary uptake.

The critical figure for the late fiction as a whole is D. S. Mirsky, who in his *History of Russian Literature* (1926) calls *Alyosha the Pot* "one of his most perfect creations" — the high-water mark of English praise for any of these six tales. The "holy fool" / *yurodstvo* reading of Alyosha has some traction in Slavic studies (Pevear's translator notes; the Ploughshares blog piece by Andrew Pine). The "infection" link to *What Is Art?* is made in passing by several commentators (Pine 2020, Slavic Lit Pod) but has not been developed into a scholarly argument beyond note-level.

Scholarship on the Gorbunov-Posadov trial (8 November 1911) has almost no English-language footprint independent of Russian-language archival work. The facts are in the PSS commentary (Tom 42) but have not been synthesised into an English monograph or article as a named case study. Wikipedia's English article on Gorbunov-Posadov does not mention the trial.

## Triangulation

### Finding 1 — The realist return

**Conventional view:** The late weekly-reading stories are treated as generically uniform "didactic" or "parabolic" prose, continuous with the 1880s folk tales. Kirkus (2000): "heavily moralistic, with many structured as parables." The *ourcivilisation.com* digest of Tolstoy's later short stories (drawing on Semmens, 1968) notes that later stories "shift from realism toward parabolic and didactic forms" and that "some final stories revert to earlier realistic methods" but names only *Alyosha* in this company.

**Relation:** **complicates / extends.** The primary record shows a decisive generic split within the 1905–06 group itself. *Корней Васильев* (realist domestic story, 13 redactions, dense physical geography, non-schematic ending), *За что?* (realist historical fiction, named documented setting, 15 redactions, "углубление психологического анализа" per PSS commentary), and *Божеское и человеческое* (realist multi-plot novella, 49 manuscript units, historically attested prototype Lizogub) are formally realist in the ways the 1880s parables are not — named characters with psychological arcs, specified historical frames, ironic or ambiguous endings. *Молитва* is a hybrid (realist grief-frame + a dream-vision). The genre-homogenisation in scholarship flattens a distinction the primary record makes legible. The corpus extends below what scholarship resolves: the realist/parabolic fault-line within the late tales has not been named or argued in English-language criticism.

**Sources:** Semmens, E. J. (1968), *Tolstoy's Later Short Stories* (ourcivilisation.com digest); Kirkus Reviews, "Divine and Human" (2000); PSS Tom 42 commentary (Eikhenbaum, Nikiforov, Spiridonov).

### Finding 2 — Encryption / the political charge

**Conventional view:** The English reception de-politicises the anthology. The *Publishers Weekly* (2000) review calls the stories "lovely artifacts" offering spiritual insight. Sekirin's introduction (Zondervan edition) frames them as spiritual and ethical reflection. The only English-language scholarly edition to foreground politics is Spence (Northwestern, 2000): his introduction stresses "the political import and allegory of the tales, all three of which were written around the time of the Russian revolution of 1905," and the Maximov appendix (*Siberia and Penal Servitude*) makes the documentary source of *За что?* available to English readers for the first time in that volume.

**Relation:** **extends.** The argument that the fiction was chosen by Tolstoy to carry what a signed treatise could not legally print — specifically that Толстой stated "При составлении «Круга чтения» я имею в виду именно нецензурное" (PSS Tom 42 general apparatus) — is not developed in English scholarship as a formal claim about these tales. Spence comes closest but focuses on the three tales in his own volume. The full argument — that *За что?* and *Божеское и человеческое* carry, inside the anthology's protective framing, an anti-autocracy / anti-capital-punishment / anti-church-as-state-servant critique that had no other legal route to print in Russia circa 1906 — is not made in English-language scholarship as a named thesis. The corpus extends below what the scholarship resolves.

**Sources:** Spence, Gordon (2000), intro to *Divine and Human and Other Stories*, Northwestern University Press; PSS Tom 42, general apparatus (Spiridonov); Sekirin, Peter (2000), intro to *Divine and Human*, Zondervan.

### Finding 3 — The Gorbunov-Posadov prosecution

**Conventional view:** In English-language scholarship this case is effectively undocumented. The English Wikipedia article on Gorbunov-Posadov (accessed June 2026) contains no mention of the 1911 trial, the 8 November verdict, the one-year fortress sentence, or the order to destroy 12 passages. The standard English-language Tolstoy biographies (Troyat 1967; Wilson 1988; Bartlett 2010) do not use the prosecution as a named episode. There is no English-language article or monograph chapter specifically on this trial as a press-freedom case.

**Relation:** **extends.** The primary record is unambiguous: Gorbunov-Posadov was indicted 31 May 1911, tried 8 November 1911, found guilty, sentenced to one year in a fortress; a parallel case (9 December 1911, St Petersburg) tried V. A. Maximov (*Yasnaya Polyana* imprint, 30,000 copies). Twelve passages of the anthology were ordered destroyed. Defence by V. A. Maklakov and N. K. Muravyov. This is documented in PSS Tom 42 (Spiridonov) with the indictment charges quoted. English scholarship has not incorporated these facts. The corpus extends below what the scholarship resolves, and the gap is documentable: the trial is a more substantive press-freedom case than most English sources acknowledge the anthology to have generated.

**Sources:** PSS Tom 42, general apparatus (Spiridonov) — primary documentation, internal to the Russian edition; no English secondary source located that covers this case.

### Finding 4 — Алёша Горшок as the *What Is Art?* infection ideal

**Conventional view:** This is the best-documented scholarly claim in the cluster, and it broadly confirms the reading. Mirsky (1926) calls it "one of his most perfect creations." The *ourcivilisation.com* Semmens digest calls it "a perfect masterpiece in miniature." Pevear (translator notes, via Ploughshares) links the *yurodivyi* holy-fool figure to Tolstoy's inability to attain the purity he depicts. Andrew Pine (Ploughshares, 2020) argues the story exemplifies the *What Is Art?* framework: Alyosha embodies Tolstoy's ideal of art conveying "the simplest everyday feelings of life, such as are accessible to everyone in the world," without statement or moralising. The Slavic Lit Pod discussion invokes "verbal iconography" and notes that Tolstoy's dismissal of the story ("very bad. Gave it up") coexists with its posthumous canonical status.

**Relation:** **confirms / extends.** Scholarship confirms the tale as a late masterpiece of simplicity and connects it to the *WIA* infection ideal. The extension the corpus provides: the PSS commentary confirms the *Why* of Tolstoy's dissatisfaction — it was written very fast (4 days, 24–28 February 1905), a single autograph survives with minimal revision (only two manuscripts), and he excluded it from Круг чтения. The PSS records T. A. Kuzminskaya's protest that the fictional Alyosha bore no resemblance to the real "полуидиот Алеша Горшок" she remembered — a pointed reminder that the tale's apparent transparency conceals a substantial aesthetic transformation of the source. None of the English critical discussions engage the compositional record; they read the published text in isolation.

**Sources:** Mirsky, D. S. (1926), *History of Russian Literature*, ch. on later Tolstoy; Pine, Andrew (2020), "The Singular Character of Tolstoy's Alyosha," *Ploughshares* blog; Slavic Lit Pod, "Alyosha the Pot" episode (2021); PSS Tom 36, commentary (Mishin).

**Summary — standalone translations before 2000:**
- **Алёша Горшок:** yes — Hagberg Wright 1912 (first); Maude c. 1922/Centenary Edition; Clarence Brown 1985. Well-established in English.
- **Корней Васильев:** uncertain — one prior translation asserted by Sekirin but unidentified; certainly translated by Sekirin 2000.
- **Молитва:** no standalone translation located before Sekirin 2000.
- **Ягоды:** no standalone translation located before Sekirin/Spence 2000.
- **За что?:** no standalone translation located before Sekirin/Spence 2000. The Marxists Archive listed it as a missing/untranslated work prior to 2000.
- **Божеское и человеческое:** no standalone translation located before Sekirin/Spence 2000. The Marxists Archive listed it as missing/untranslated. An 1906 English-language translation is referenced without a translator name in the EBSCO research article (possibly the Chertkov-supervised Anglophone intermediary used for the two 1906 Russian journal printings, per PSS Tom 42), but this has not been traced to a published English edition.

**Key finding:** Of the six tales, only *Алёша Горшок* had a published English translation before the year 2000. The other five (Корней Васильев, Молитва, Ягоды, За что?, Божеское и человеческое) first appeared in English in the two parallel 2000 translations (Sekirin/Zondervan and Spence/Northwestern). The sibling dive's finding that Sekirin's *A Calendar of Wisdom* (1997) omits all 52 weekly stories stands: the Weekly Reading tales had no English-language home until 2000.

**Caution on overclaiming "first English":** Do not claim that Алёша Горшок was untranslated before the Maudes — Hagberg Wright 1912 predates them. Do not claim any of the five 2000 translations are "first" for *Корней Васильев* without locating the prior translation Sekirin alludes to.

## Gaps — what scholarship treats as central that the corpus missed

**1. The Lizogub scholarship gap.** *Божеское и человеческое* is built on the Odessa execution of Dmitri Lizogub (8 August 1879). Stepnyak-Kravchinsky's *Underground Russia* (*Podpolnaya Rossiya*) is cited in the PSS as one of Tolstoy's two sources for the character. The intersection between Tolstoy's tale and the Anglo-American reception of the Populist revolutionary movement — Stepnyak-Kravchinsky wrote in English and was well known in London — is not addressed by any source encountered in this sweep. Whether English-language reviews of *Underground Russia* (published 1882 in English) inform the British/American reception of *Divine and Human* is an open question.

**2. The Polish-reception track.** PSS Tom 42 documents that Tolstoy himself wanted *За что?* known to Polish readers (letter to journalist Vrublevsky, 4 July 1908). The Polish-language reception and translation history of this tale — which concerns the 1830 insurrection and names Kościuszko-era patriots — is absent from English-language scholarship and was not pursued in this sweep (outside scope: English-first).

**3. The Gordon Spence introduction.** The Northwestern 2000 edition is the only English-language scholarly framing that engages the political charge directly, but the introduction itself was not obtained in full text. Spence is described as the author of *Tolstoy the Ascetic* (a title not located in full). His political reading of the three tales may constitute the most developed argument in English for the "encryption" thesis, but it has not been incorporated into the secondary literature that this sweep found.

**4. Кrug chtenija monthly-reading censorship track.** The PSS notes that the "monthly readings" in the anthology never passed Russian censorship and appeared only in the 1907 German translation (Dresden, Shkarvan/Schmidt). English scholarship has not picked up this parallel censorship story, which would reinforce Finding 2 about the недельные чтения as a censorship-adjacent publishing strategy.
