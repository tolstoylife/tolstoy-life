## Dive: The break with the Church — Tolstoy, the Holy Synod, and the 1901 excommunication
## Cluster slug: 1901-1902-the-break-with-the-church
## Date: 2026-06-13

---

## A. RECEIVED VIEW

The mainstream scholarly account runs roughly as follows. By the late 1870s–early 1880s Tolstoy had already broken decisively with Orthodox Christianity as a lived institution — rejecting the sacraments, the divinity of Christ as dogma, and the ecclesiastical hierarchy. His novels and tracts (especially *Resurrection*, 1899, which satirised an Orthodox prison chapel service) made the conflict public and unavoidable. On **20–22 February 1901** the Holy Synod of the Russian Orthodox Church passed *Определение № 557* — a "Circular Letter" (not a formal anathema) declaring that Tolstoy had placed himself outside the Church. Published in *Церковные ведомости* on **24 February 1901**, the document was simultaneously reprinted in all major newspapers across the Empire.

**Who drafted it.** Scholarly consensus (Kolstø 2022; Wikipedia/Anthony Vadkovsky; contemporary reporting) now locates the initiative with the **priestly members of the Holy Synod**, principally Metropolitan Anthony (Vadkovsky) of St Petersburg and Ladoga, who chaired the Synod and is identified as "a principal initiator." Ober-Procurator Konstantin Pobedonostsev — long assumed by Soviet-era researchers to have driven the decision (Kolstø 2022 shows those archival claims were fabricated) — in fact **opposed** the edict on strategic grounds, fearing it would amplify Tolstoy's prestige rather than diminish it. The decree was signed by seven hierarchs: Metropolitan Anthony (Vadkovsky), Metropolitan Theognost of Kiev, Metropolitan Vladimir of Moscow, Archbishop Jerome of Kholmsk, and Bishops Jacob, Markela, and Boris.

**Anathema vs. отлучение.** This is the single most important historiographical point in the scholarship. Multiple scholars and the Church itself stress that the edict was **not a formal анафема (anathema)** — the solemn liturgical curse of the sort pronounced on heretics at Councils. It was an *отлучение* — a declaration that Tolstoy had already, by his own public statements, separated himself from Church membership. The document's own language runs: the Church "does not reckon him as its member and cannot include him until he repents." Tolstoy himself seized on this distinction, calling the action either "illegal" or "intentionally equivocal" (*незаконно или умышленно двусмысленно*): if it was excommunication proper, it violated Church procedure; if it was merely a statement of non-membership, it was self-evident and served only to incite hostility (Wikisource, "My Reply to the Synod," April 4, 1901, tr. Aylmer Maude). Kolstø (2022) describes the decree as "both an excommunication, and not an excommunication" — a document that attempted simultaneously to warn the faithful and to leave a door open for repentance, and that ended up satisfying neither goal. Prince Mirsky, cited approvingly by Aaron Taylor (2010), put the point bluntly: the edict "register[ed] a matter of common knowledge — that Tolstoy had ceased to be an Orthodox Churchman." The Church's own 2010 statement (Archimandrite Tikhon Shevkunov, Patriarch Kirill's council on culture) reiterated this framing: "The decision of the Most Holy Governing Synod merely stated an accomplished fact."

**Scholarly framing of the break's causes.** Rosamund Bartlett (*Tolstoy: A Russian Life*, 2010) places the break in the context of Tolstoy's last three decades as a "social and religious crusader," emphasising his clashes with ecclesiastical and state authority. Inessa Medzhibovskaya (*Tolstoy and the Religious Culture of His Time: A Biography of a Long Conversion, 1845–1887*, Lexington Books, 2008) — the first book-length study in any language of Tolstoy's religious development — treats his transition not as a "crisis-laden tragedy" but as "a massive philosophical and religious project," contextualising it through European and Russian conversion patterns (Paul, Augustine, Luther, Pascal, Rousseau, Gogol, Dostoevsky). This reading supports the dive's primary finding that the break with the *institution* preceded 1901 by roughly twenty years, and that what 1901 formalised was an already-settled estrangement. Aylmer Maude (*The Life of Tolstoy: Later Years*, 1910) — who knew Tolstoy personally from 1888 onward and was authorised to write his biography — covers the excommunication in Chapter 16, emphasising the popular backlash and Tolstoy's immediate written reply, and translating the key documents into English. The 1901 contemporary account by Robert Edward Crozier Long (marxists.org) describes the edict's form as "a very mild one" by Russian Church standards.

**Kolstø (2022) as the current scholarly benchmark.** Pål Kolstø's *Heretical Orthodoxy: Lev Tolstoi and the Russian Orthodox Church* (Cambridge University Press, 2022, "Ideas in Context" series) is the most thorough recent scholarly treatment. Its Chapter 12 ("The 'Excommunication' and Its Aftermath") argues the edict was a "miscalculation" — the Church attempted to do two incompatible things at once. Kolstø also corrects the Soviet archival distortion: Soviet-era researchers systematically misattributed the initiative to "tsarist state authorities" (i.e., Pobedonostsev), whereas archival documents show it originated with the Synod's own priestly members. Reviewer Regula M. Zwahlen (University of Fribourg, H-Soz-Kult, 2024) notes Kolstø's finding that the Church "went to extreme lengths to try to find a way to allow a requiem mass to be said for Tolstoi," further complicating the simple heretic-cast-out narrative.

## C. RECEPTION FACTS

### The edict

- **Full title:** Определение Святейшего Синода от 20–22 февраля 1901 г. № 557.
- **Dates:** passed 20–22 February 1901; published in *Церковные ведомости* (Church Gazette, no. 8) on **24 February 1901**; reprinted the following day in all major Russian newspapers.
- **Signatories:** Metropolitan Anthony (Vadkovsky) of St Petersburg (chair); Metropolitan Theognost of Kiev; Metropolitan Vladimir of Moscow; Archbishop Jerome of Kholmsk; Bishops Jacob, Markela, Boris. (Source: ignisdivine.substack.com, citing Dr Martin George Holmes, *Heretic or Scapegoat?*)
- **Nature:** Not an anathema. The text declares the Church "does not reckon him as its member and cannot include him until he repents." The phrase "cut himself off from any communion with the Orthodox Church" characterises the action as registering an existing state, not imposing a new one.
- **Initiative:** Metropolitan Anthony Vadkovsky and the Synod's priestly members. Pobedonostsev (Ober-Procurator) opposed the move. This reverses the Soviet-era attribution. (Kolstø 2022; Wikipedia "Anthony Vadkovsky")
- **Translation of the decree:** Louise and Aylmer Maude (Wikisource, partial).

### Sofia Tolstaya's protest letter and Metropolitan Anthony's reply

- Sofia Andreevna Tolstaya wrote letters to **both** K. P. Pobedonostsev and Metropolitan Anthony Vadkovsky following publication of the edict. The archival originals are held at the Russian Presidential Library and the Russian State Historical Archive (RGIA). (prlib.ru/en/node/466290)
- The letters are dated **February–March 1901** (exact date noted as February 26 in some sources; the Scribd document cited Alexandra L. Tolstaya's 1953 two-volume *Father*, New York: Chekhov Press, as source for their existence and content).
- Contemporary reporting (Long 1901, marxists.org) specifies that Sofia "wrote a very vehement letter of protest to M. Pobyedonostseff." The Presidential Library catalogue confirms she also wrote separately to Metropolitan Anthony.
- Metropolitan Anthony replied to Sofia. According to Scribd/Alexandra Tolstaya: "Metropolitan Anthony's answer did not satisfy Sophia Andreevna, and Tolstoy simply did not read it."
- **CAUTION / needsReview:** The full text of Sofia's letters is not publicly available online. Specific dates for Antony's reply are not confirmed in accessible sources. The best secondary source for the letter's content is Alexandra Tolstaya's 1953 *Father* (paywalled; not directly read for this report).

### Public reaction

**Pro-Tolstoy:**
- The edict "called forth among unenlightened and unreasoning people anger and hatred toward him, which went so far as threats of murder" (Tolstoy's own words in his Reply; Christian Science Journal, Sept 1901).
- On the day of publication in Moscow, crowds in the streets recognised Tolstoy near the Kremlin and "surrounded, acclaimed, and treated [him] with the greatest manifestations of respect and sympathy" (Maude, *Life* Ch. 16).
- Flowers, telegrams and deputations poured in from across the country. Tolstoy said he was feted "as if it were his birthday" (Maude).
- **At the 29th Wanderers exhibition (spring 1901)**, Ilya Repin's new full-length portrait of Tolstoy (now at the State Russian Museum) was "showered with flowers" by visitors (gw2ru.com; Repin scholarship confirms the 1901 date of the portrait).
- Students marched **"to the number of five hundred to the Kazan Cathedral"** and demanded they also be excommunicated — one of the most-cited acts of solidarity (Long 1901; Maude *Life* Ch. 16). **needsReview:** sources differ on whether this was the Kazan Cathedral in St Petersburg or Moscow; Kazan Cathedral in St Petersburg is the more likely venue given its prominence for political demonstrations in that era.
- Tolstoy received a Nobel Peace Prize nomination that same year (gw2ru.com).

**Anti-Tolstoy / clerical voices:**
- **John of Kronstadt** (Fr. Ioann Sergiyev) — the most influential parish priest in Russia — denounced Tolstoy as "a heretic who surpassed all the heretics" and "laughed at Church with Satan's laughter" (rbth.com; gw2ru.com). Earlier in the 1880s he had called Tolstoy an atheist. In 1908 he reportedly prayed publicly for Tolstoy's death (rbth.com). These statements represent a hardline clerical reaction separate from the Synod's more measured tone.
- Right-wing and clerical press: Tolstoy's Reply to the Synod itself refers to "anger and hatred" and "threats of murder" stirred up among "unenlightened" people by the edict's publication — the only documented source for death threats that was accessible for this report. Specific newspapers are not named in accessible English-language sources.

### The Orel Missionary Congress and "On Religious Tolerance"

- In **September 1901**, M. A. Stakhovich, leader of the Orel nobility, delivered a speech at the **Missionary Congress in Orel** calling for freedom of conscience and the abolition of criminal penalties for lapsing from Orthodoxy. This speech triggered an all-Russian controversy.
- Tolstoy wrote "On Religious Tolerance" (Russian: *О веротерпимости*) in 1901, published 1902 in the *What is Religion?* collection (T. Y. Crowell, 1902), in direct response to — or in solidarity with — the Stakhovich speech and the wider debate it provoked.
- **needsReview:** The precise textual link between "On Religious Tolerance" and the Stakhovich speech (i.e., whether Tolstoy explicitly names Stakhovich or the Orel congress) was not confirmed in accessible sources. One DOAJ article was found treating the "consequences of Stakhovich's speech in the Orel diocese" but was not opened.

### The 2001 centenary request

- In **January 2001**, **Vladimir Tolstoy** (great-great-grandson, director of the Yasnaya Polyana estate museum) wrote to Patriarch Alexei II asking not for the excommunication to be lifted, but for "dialogue on this painful subject" on the centenary of the edict.
- Patriarch Alexei did **not reply** to the letter.
- Senior Moscow Patriarchate official **Vsevolod Chaplin** stated that any review "would make sense only if some proof were discovered that Tolstoy changed his views before his death." He described excommunication as "an attestation that the writer's beliefs very seriously disagreed with Orthodox teaching." (Christianity Today, March 2001)
- **2010:** On the centenary of Tolstoy's *death* (November 20), Sergei Stepashin (President of the Russian Book Union, former Prime Minister) wrote to Patriarch Kirill requesting a "public display of compassion." Archimandrite Tikhon Shevkunov, responding at Kirill's direction, refused: "Count Tolstoy excommunicated himself from the church." (OrthoChristian.com, November 22, 2010)
- **Note on "descendants asked in 2001":** Vladimir Tolstoy's January 2001 letter was the documented approach by a family member at the centenary of the *edict*; it asked for dialogue, not rescission. The often-cited claim that "grandchildren asked for rescission" in 2001 is an imprecision — Vladimir explicitly clarified he was not requesting the excommunication be lifted. (Christianity Today 2001)
