# Volume IV, Chapter 15. 1909 (continued). Henry George. Stockholm. N. N. Gusev

At the end of May L. N. was visited by I. I. Mechnikov, the famous Parisian scholar.

On 30 May L. N. records in his diary:

> "Mechnikov and the correspondents have arrived. Mechnikov is agreeable and seems, as it were, broad. I have not yet had time to talk with him."

N. N. Gusev relates this as follows:

> "31 May. Yesterday I. I. Mechnikov arrived for a single day with his wife. There were no especially significant conversations between him and L. N., at least not at the times when I had occasion to listen.
>
> After luncheon Mechnikov began to speak with enthusiasm of L. N.'s artistic works. L. N. expressed his usual attitude toward them and then added:
>
> — Just as at a fairground booth a hare jumps out and performs all sorts of tricks in order to lure the public inside, where the real performance is, so do my artistic works play the very same role: they draw attention to my serious things.
>
> Further on, L. N. said that he sees the significance of art in this — that it unites people in one and the same feeling.
>
> — If this feeling is good, — said L. N., — then the work of art too will be good; but if this feeling is a bad one — of sensuality, of pride — then the work of art too will be harmful.
>
> Madame Mechnikova said that, in her opinion, the significance of artistic works lies in their disclosing the soul of the person whom they portray. L. N. fully agreed with this.
>
> After Mechnikov's departure L. N. said to me:
>
> — On the way (they had driven over to Chertkov's) I tried to broach the subject of religion with him; out of respect for me he did not object, but I saw that this does not interest him in the least. I am even glad that I myself spoke little, and left it to him to speak."

The correspondent of *Russkoye Slovo* (Russian Word) conveys the following opinion of Mechnikov, which he set down from L. N.'s own words:

> "I did not find in him the usual trait of the narrowness of specialists, of learned people. On the contrary, a broad interest in everything, and especially in the aesthetic sides of life.
>
> On the other hand, the most specialized questions and discoveries in the field of science he set forth so simply that they involuntarily captivated one by their interest.
>
> I was utterly struck by his energy: in spite of a night spent in a railway carriage, he was so animated and vigorous that he presented an excellent proof of the soundness of his hygienic — indeed in part even morally hygienic — regime, in which, to my mind, it is of great importance that he neither drinks, nor smokes, nor plays any games whatever.
>
> — Did you speak of artistic works?
>
> — Yes. Among other things, he would by no means believe that I had forgotten the contents of *Anna Karenina*...
>
> I told him that if I were to write something even now, it would be of the sort of the second part of *Faust*, that is, the same kind of nonsense. And he related to me his own explanation of that second part — a very ingenious one...
>
> In conversation we recalled that I had known his brother, Ivan Ilyich — indeed, my tale *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* bears a certain relation to the deceased, a very dear man, a former prosecutor of the Tula court...
>
> Lev Nikolaevich pondered for a moment and then recalled yet another very interesting episode:
>
> — After a conversation about vegetarianism, of which the household had been speaking, Mechnikov began to tell of a tribe of anthropophagi living in Africa, in the Congo. He related interesting details of how they eat their captives. First the captive is led to the military chief, who marks out on his skin the piece which he reserves for himself. Then the captive is brought in turn for such markings to the rest — according to seniority, until they have scored him all over.
>
> This interested me in the highest degree, and I asked Mechnikov:
>
> — Have these people a religious world-view?
>
> And to this he answered. According to him, they believe in the 'deification' of ancestors.
>
> I asked him to send me more detailed materials concerning the life of these people, and he promised to send them to me, and also to send his work *Les essais optimistiques* (*), in which is set forth his explanation of the second part of *Faust*.
>
> (* "Optimistic Essays." *)
>
> — On the whole, — said Lev Nikolaevich in conclusion, — from this meeting I have gained far more of all that good which I had expected."

The conclusion about this meeting, however, to be found in his diary, is not so favorable:

> "31 May. Mechnikov turned out to be a very frivolous man — areligious. I purposely chose a time to talk with him one to one about science and religion. About science nothing but a faith in that state of science the justification of which I demanded. About religion, silence. Evidently a denial of what is considered religion, and an incomprehension, that is, an unwillingness to understand, what religion is.
>
> There is no inward definition either of the one or of the other, neither of science nor of religion. The old aestheticism of the Hegel-Goethe-Turgenev kind. And very talkative. I let him talk and am very glad that I did not hinder him."

It is evident that in the conversation with the correspondent, intended for the press, L. N. expressed himself far more mildly, taking only the one favorable side of the meeting with his famous guest.

This visit was followed by another, which brought L. N. far more satisfaction.

On the morning of 2 June L. N. received the following telegram:

> "May I visit. Be so good as to answer. Henry George the son."

L. N. answered immediately: "Very glad to see you. I await you." Understandable is the joyful agitation that took hold of L. N. in expectation of this meeting. There was coming to him the son of that man in whose creations L. N. had found the resolution of the most needful of life's questions, the land question, and moreover its resolution upon religious and moral foundations.

He wished to mark this meeting by something, and knowing that there would be reports of it in the newspapers, L. N. resolved to make use of this occasion in order to remind the broad public once again of what the essence of the idea proclaimed by Henry George the father consists in.

And on that same day he wrote a short article on this matter, which we here reproduce:

> "2 June 1909. I have today received a telegram from the son of Henry George, expressing a desire to visit me. The thought of a meeting with the son of one of the most remarkable men of the 19th century vividly recalled to me all that he did, and all that inertness, not only of our Russian government, but of all the governments of the so-called educated world, with respect to that radical resolution of all economic questions which already many years ago was given by this great man with such irresistible clarity and persuasiveness.
>
> The land question — in essence, the question of the liberation of people from the slavery produced by landed property — presents itself to me in our time as standing in just that same position in which the question of serfdom in Russia and of slavery in America stood in my youth. The difference is only this, that the injustice of landed property, just as crying as the injustice of personal slavery, far more broadly and deeply takes hold of all human relations, is spread everywhere (whereas then, among Christian peoples, slavery existed only in Russia and America), and is far more tormenting for the slaves than personal slavery. So strange — one would like to say ludicrous, were they not so cruel and did they not call forth such sufferings of the majority of the working population — are those attempts at social reconstruction undertaken and proposed by both hostile camps, the governmental as well as the revolutionary, by means of all sorts of the most diverse measures, with the exception of that one which alone can destroy that crying injustice from which the enormous majority of the population suffers, and at once extinguish that revolutionary mood of the people which, driven inward, is yet more dangerous than when it shows itself openly. All these attempts to resolve political questions by means of new enactments, without destroying landed property, recall Henry George's excellent comparison of all such enactments with the act of a fool who, having laid the whole burden into one of two baskets hung upon the back of an ass, laid into the other basket an equal weight of stones.
>
> But whether or not those classes which enjoy the advantages of this injustice wish it, and however the learned people of these classes strive to conceal this injustice and to pretend that they do not understand it, this cruel injustice cannot but be — and even very soon must be — destroyed. It must be destroyed because it is already perfectly clearly perceived by all the real Russian working people, who in their majority have never recognized and do not recognize the right — rather, the violation of right — that consists in landed property.
>
> And therefore I think with joy that, however far now both the governmental and the revolutionary leaders are from a reasonable resolution of the land question, it will nevertheless be — and very soon — resolved, and precisely in Russia, and by no means by means of any strange, baseless, arbitrary, unrealizable, and, above all, unjust theories of expropriation, and still more absurd governmental measures for the destruction of the commune and the establishment of small landed property, that is, the strengthening and confirmation of that against which the struggle lies ahead, but will be and can be resolved only by one thing: the recognition of the equal right of every man to live and to feed himself upon that land on which he was born, which is so irresistibly proved by the whole teaching of Henry George.
>
> I think so because the thought of the equal right of all people to the land, in spite of all the efforts of 'educated' and 'learned' people to extirpate this thought, by means of projects of expropriation or of the destruction of the commune and other measures, from the consciousness of the Russian people, nevertheless lives in the real Russian people and sooner or later — I think soon — must receive realization."

Henry George the son himself related to a newspaper man about his visit to Yasnaya Polyana in these terms:

> "You will understand with what feeling I was filled as I drove up to Yasnaya Polyana, if I tell you that to see Lev Tolstoy was my father's cherished dream. From my late parent and from other intimate friends I had always heard reverential opinions of the great writer. How my father longed to pass even a few hours in personal converse with Tolstoy! An untimely death deprived him of this happy meeting, and so I, the faithful follower of my father's behests, resolved at all costs to realize this dream. I am all filled with the impressions of this meeting. From afar I saw how Tolstoy was being driven in a wheeled chair. Judging by the first impression, I thought that I was seeing a decrepit, exhausted old man. But hardly had Tolstoy got down from the chair and turned to me with his affable smile, when I was convinced that my first impression was mistaken. Tolstoy is vigorous, full of strength and energy. One forgets about his advanced years. What wise, good serenity breathes from him, what fresh spiritual simplicity!
>
> Now, I had heard from some people regret that the great writer's memory had supposedly begun to weaken. It is possible that his memory has indeed weakened, but it is still stronger than the memory of ordinary mortals. I do not know what memory he had in his youth, but in any case he cited to me very many quotations, extracts from various teachings. He remembers even small details so vividly that there can be no talk of any loss of memory.
>
> I was struck, among other things, by the cool-headed impartiality with which he spoke of the death awaiting him. For the first time it has fallen to me to meet an old man peering so calmly into the abyss of the grave.
>
> During my visit there was with Tolstoy the balalaika-player Troyanovsky with his companions. Tolstoy was very much pleased with their playing. He evidently yields easily to the mood evoked by music, listens pensively to characteristic melodies. Music he undoubtedly loves and knows how to value.
>
> By his hospitality I am charmed. The great writer led me about the garden, spoke much of mother nature. He long questioned me about the personality of my father. He took an interest in his life and with a kindly feeling recalled certain theses of his theory. Tolstoy more than once emphasized his solidarity in the questions about the land with my father Henry George. Becoming acquainted with Tolstoy's opinions about certain phenomena of our spiritual life, I was convinced of the broad tolerance of the Russian genius and of the complete absence of proselytizing strivings. And, as it seems to me, his influence upon the peasants is explained only by personal charm, but by no means by an ardent preaching of his teaching."

In these last words there was reflected the whole psychology of the American. For all his boundless respect toward L. N., he cannot allow that the "wild," anarchic ideas of L. N. could influence the people.

Taking leave of his guest, L. N. said to him:

— We shall not see each other again; tell me, what charge do you give me for your father in the next world.

— Tell him that I am continuing his work, — answered George.

L. N. could not restrain his tears at these words of his guest.

On 8 June L. N. went to his daughter Tatyana Lvovna at their estate of Kochety and stayed there as a guest until 3 July.

There he saw his friend V. G. Chertkov, who had been permitted to come to the Sukhotins.

Living at his daughter's, amid the general contentment and the general disposition toward him, L. N. nevertheless continued to feel all the weight of the class distinction, and recorded in his diary:

> "From the morning, in bed, I wrote a prayer for little Sonya. All is not well. Nothing got done in my work. I read 41 letters with an unkind feeling. I rode on horseback. Very tired. The main thing, though, is the tormenting feeling of the poverty — not poverty, but the humiliation, the downtroddenness of the people. The cruelty and madness of the revolutionaries is pardonable. Then at dinner, Sverbeyeva, the French language, and tennis — and side by side, slaves, hungry, naked, crushed by work. I cannot endure it, I want to run away."

In this same diary, written at the Sukhotins', one comes upon this remarkable thought:

> "Very clearly, vividly understood (strange to say) for the first time, that either there is no God, or there is nothing except God."

On 3 July L. N. set out back to Yasnaya, and on the way from Kochety to the railway station turned aside to the farmstead of his friend Khrisanf Nikolaevich Abrikosov.

In L. N.'s diary there is a brief entry about this journey:

> "Set out on the 3rd, as I had decided. I was at dear Abrikosov's. Tanya saw me off as far as Mtsensk. I traveled third class, and very pleasantly: a gendarme and resettlers. Those people who are treated like cattle, and who alone make life and history (if it is of interest to anyone). I corrected *The Inevitable Revolution*."

Upon his return to Yasnaya L. N. had again to live through a hard trial. The chairman of the international peace congress, appointed this year for August in Stockholm, sent L. N. an invitation to come to the congress.

> "I shall go," — L. N. said to Gusev, having read this invitation, — "I want there to express clearly this incompatibility of Christianity with military service."

And on that same day L. N. dictated to Gusev a letter addressed to the chairman of the congress, in which he says that if only he has the strength, he will try to be at the congress himself; but if not, then he will send that which he would wish to say.

This great undertaking was not fated to be realized.

For a reason difficult to explain, Sofya Andreyevna set herself against this journey. This resistance of hers created in the house of Yasnaya Polyana a heavy atmosphere, from which Lev Nikolaevich had much to suffer.

In Gusev's diary there is this entry:

> "21 July. S. A. does not wish L. N. to go to Stockholm to the peace congress.
>
> ...Today L. N. neither ate nor drank anything the whole day; only, as he was going off to sleep, he took for himself half a glass of tea."

What must have been the sufferings of L. N. that brought him into this distressing state.

> "Yielding to S. A.," — Gusev continues, — "L. N. resolved not to go to the peace congress. This morning he dictated to me an article which he intends to send to the congress. Having finished the dictation, he went up to the table, admired a bouquet of flowers which Ilya Vasilyevich had brought today.
>
> — That, surely, Hans (the gardener) sent. And these here are peasant ones, — he pointed to a bouquet of field flowers standing in another vase. — What is this — you do not know? — he asked me, pointing to some little whitened leaf that had got in among the flowers.
>
> I did not know.
>
> — It is an oak leaf, — said L. N., having peered at it, — for some reason it has turned white. And suddenly, unexpectedly for me, L. N. added:
>
> — I feel so well today."

To the artist N. K. Parkhomenko, who was at this time painting his portrait, L. N. also spoke of how he had resolved not to go to the congress, and of how it was joyful in his soul.

— But it seems you very much wanted to go, — asked Parkhomenko.

— Because I so much wanted to go, — said L. N., — and I was asked not to do it, and I at last yielded, it is now joyful to me that I was able to yield.

But this joy came to him after a hard struggle. This struggle was reflected in the entries of his diary:

> "21 July. Since yesterday evening S. A. has been weak and irritated. I could not fall asleep until 3 o'clock and longer. I woke weak; they roused me. S. A. did not sleep the whole night. I went to her. It was something insane. Dušan had poisoned her, and so forth. The letter from Stakhovich, about which I had to speak because she thought that something was being hidden from her, called forth a still worse state. I am tired and can do no more, I feel myself quite ill. I feel the impossibility of behaving reasonably and lovingly, the complete impossibility. For the present I want only to withdraw and to take no part in anything. I can do nothing else, or else I have already been seriously thinking of running away. Come now, show your Christianity. C'est le moment ou jamais (*). And I dreadfully want to go away. There is hardly anything in my presence here that is of use to anyone. A heavy sacrifice, and to the harm of all. Help, my God, teach me. One thing I want — to do not my will, but Thine."
>
> (* Now or never. *)

> "22 July. Yesterday I neither ate nor slept. As usual, it was very hard. It is hard now too, but with a tender well-being. Yes — to love those who do us evil, you say; come now, put it to the test. I try, but badly. More and more I think of going away and of making a disposition about my property...
>
> ...I do not know what I shall do. Help, help, help. This 'help' means that I am weak, that I am bad. It is well that there is at least this consciousness..."

> "26 July. After dinner I began to speak of the journey to Sweden; there arose a dreadful, hysterical irritability. She wanted to poison herself with morphine; I snatched it away and threw it under the staircase. I struggled. But when I had lain down in bed and calmly thought it over, I resolved to refuse the journey. I went and told her. She is pitiful, I truly pity her. But how instructive. I undertook nothing except the inward work upon myself. And the moment I took myself in hand, everything was resolved."

The congress was at first postponed because of a strike in Sweden, but afterward took place nevertheless. From Russia the representative at this congress was Prince Dolgoruky, the well-known public figure.

L. N.'s article, which he sent to the congress, was received, but it was not read at the congress. The moderate and well-intentioned milieu of pacifists gathered at the congress was scandalized by the "outburst" of L. N., who held that, in order for people not to make war, there must be no army. This seemed to them such naïveté that, smiling condescendingly and rendering their due to the great genius, they who had invited him to the congress did not venture to announce his opinion aloud.

But these events in L. N.'s personal life did not deliver him from the influx of visitors. For all others he was still that same beacon, toward which from all ends of the earth people strove, both as solitary individuals and in groups. Here is one of such touching visits:

On 2 August Yasnaya Polyana was visited by the members of the XI All-Russian Forestry Congress, which was being held at that time in Tula.

On that day the members of the congress went to inspect the foothill forestry district — Kozlova Zaseka.

Someone put forward the idea of visiting Lev Nikolaevich. It was in an instant taken up and approved.

There and then they composed a letter to Countess Sofya Andreyevna with a request to inform them whether they might visit Lev Nikolaevich, and sent the letter by a special messenger.

The desire to see the great old man was so strong that the excursionists, to the number of about 150 persons, could not contain themselves and set off on foot to Yasnaya Polyana, without awaiting the return of the one sent.

The excursionists were invited into the garden.

In a few minutes Lev Nikolaevich appeared amid the professors in their black frock coats and the foresters in their uniforms and jackets with gold piping and gleaming shoulder-straps.

In the name of the guests, Professor N. S. Nesterov (from Moscow) came forward with a speech.

— Dear Lev Nikolaevich, — he said. — The members of the XI All-Russian Forestry Congress, having gathered in Tula, in the depth of the forests, from various ends of European Russia and of Siberia, could not restrain themselves from the passionate desire to see you and to bring our deepest greeting and obeisance to the great thinker. We are happy to see you and express the warm, heartfelt wish that your living word may yet long, long resound for the good of humanity.

Lev Nikolaevich, touched by this heartfelt speech, in his turn thanked the excursionists in the simplest and warmest terms.

In conversation with the guests Lev Nikolaevich took an interest in whether there would be at the congress, besides the specially forestry matter, anything of a universally human kind.

Having taken leave amiably of the excursionists, L. N. presented to each a copy of his pamphlet *An Appeal to Russian People*.

And just as in every picture the patches of light are intermingled with shadow, so too in the picture of L. N.'s life the joyful minutes alternated with heavy manifestations of the violence of authority.

On 4 August his friend and secretary N. N. Gusev was arrested and exiled. Here is what L. N. recorded about this in his diary:

> "Yesterday evening the brigands came for Gusev and carried him off. The send-off was very fine: the relations of all toward him and of him toward us. It was very good. About this I have today written a declaration."

This declaration was printed and reprinted by the majority of the Russian newspapers. Here is its text, from which we reproduce the most essential, autobiographical part:

> "Yesterday at 10 o'clock in the evening several men in uniform drove up to our house and demanded my assistant in my labors, Nikolai Nikolaevich Gusev.
>
> Nikolai Nikolaevich went down to the men who were demanding him and, returning from them, informed us that those who had come were the police captain and the district officer, and that they had come in order to take him at once and convey him to the Krapivna prison, and from there to dispatch him to the Cherdyn district, of Perm Province.
>
> This news was so strange that, in order to understand what the matter was, I went down to the men who had come and asked them to explain to me the causes of this their appearance and demand.
>
> One of them, the police captain, in answer to my question took out of his pocket a small paper and with solemn reverence read out to me the decision contained in the paper, of the Minister of Internal Affairs, to the effect that for the good of the Russian people entrusted to his care, by article 384 or some other (although it would seem that in order to do what they were doing there was no need to refer to any articles whatever), N. N. Gusev, for the dissemination of revolutionary editions, was to be taken into custody and exiled, by certain considerations known and comprehensible to the Minister of Internal Affairs, precisely to the Cherdyn district, of Perm Province, and by those same considerations — precisely for two years.
>
> Considering, after the contents of this paper had been heard out, that further conversation with the executors of it was useless, I went to my room in order to take leave of Nikolai Nikolaevich and to receive from him all those affairs with which he had been occupied in helping me in my works. Here I found all our household and guests in a particularly excited state because of the fact that so unexpectedly there had come crashing down upon Nikolai Nikolaevich Gusev, beloved and respected by all.
>
> The one author of this excitement alone, N. N. himself, was joyful and calm, and with the kindness and care for others, and not for himself, characteristic of him, was hastily putting my affairs in order, since the term allowed him to prepare for departure was no more than half an hour.
>
> We have all heard and read of thousands and thousands of such dispositions and executions; but when they are performed upon people near to us and before our eyes, then they are particularly striking. And therefore that which happened with Gusev particularly struck me: I was struck both by the incongruity with Gusev's personality of that cruel and crude measure which was taken against him, struck too by the evident injustice of the causes put forward for its application and, above all, by the inexpediency of this measure both with respect to Gusev and, still more, with respect to me, against whom this measure was, properly speaking, directed.
>
> The incongruity of suddenly, by night, seizing a man and at once carrying him off and casting him into prison (and everyone knows what the Russian prisons are now, with their overcrowding), and then dispatching him by stages under the guard of a man with a loaded rifle for more than 2,000 versts into a backwoods place standing 400 versts from the town — the incongruity of such a measure with respect to Gusev was particularly striking.
>
> One had to see how Gusev was seen off both by all our household and by all the acquaintances who had chanced to gather that evening in our house, who knew Gusev. One thing in all, from old to young, down to the children and the servants, was one feeling of respect and love toward this man and a more or less restrained feeling of indignation against the authors of that which was being done with him.
>
> Taking leave of Gusev, I burst into tears, but not from pity for that which had befallen Gusev. Pity him I could not, because I knew that he lives that spiritual life in which no external actions can deprive a man of his true good; but I burst into tears from tender emotion at the sight of that firmness, reaching even to gaiety, with which he received all that had happened with him.
>
> And this very man — kind, gentle, truthful, an enemy of all violence, desiring to serve everyone and demanding nothing for himself — this man is seized by night, locked up in a typhus-ridden prison, and exiled to some place known to those exiling him only for the reason that it is considered by them the most unpleasant for life.
>
> Still more striking was the pretext on which Gusev was seized and put into prison and is to be exiled. The pretext put forward is that Gusev disseminates revolutionary books. But during all that time — two years — that Gusev lived with me, not only did he disseminate no revolutionary books, but he never had nor read them, and always regarded such books negatively. If, in fulfilling my commissions, he sent by post and handed over any books, then these were not revolutionary, but my books. My books, however, may seem both bad and unpleasant to people, but in no case can they be called revolutionary, since in them in the most definite manner all revolutionary activity is denied, in consequence of which these books are always both condemned and derided by all the revolutionary organs. So that the accusation of Gusev of disseminating revolutionary books is not only incorrect, but has not the semblance of any kind of foundation."

Speaking then of the inexpediency of this measure both with respect to Gusev, whom it will neither reform nor render harmless, and with respect to himself, Lev Nikolaevich, he concludes thus:

> "And therefore — and in this consists the chief aim of this declaration of mine — I would again ask those people to whom the dissemination of my thoughts and my activity is unpleasant, if they can in no way remain calm and at all costs wish to employ violent measures against someone, then to employ them by no means against my friends, but against me — the sole and chief author of the appearance and dissemination of these thoughts displeasing to them.
>
> All this I have expressed with respect to Gusev and to me. But the affair which has called forth this declaration of mine has yet another, more important significance, relating not to me and Gusev, but to that spiritual state in which those people find themselves who perform such deeds as that which has been performed upon Gusev.
>
> We all know about what was being done these last years and continues to be done now in Russia. About all this it is terrible, and one does not wish, to speak. One pities all those people perished and perishing and embittered in places of exile, in prisons, dying with malice and hatred on the gallows; but one cannot but pity also those unfortunate ones who perform such deeds, and chiefly — who prescribe them.
>
> For however much these people may assure themselves that they do this for the common good, however much such people as they may approve and extol them for these deeds, however much they themselves may strive to stupefy themselves with all sorts of cares and amusements, they are people, and for the most part kind people, and they feel and know in the depth of their soul that they are acting badly, that, in doing such deeds, they are ruining that which is dearer than anything in the world — their own souls, are slamming shut for themselves the door against all the true and best joys of life.
>
> And to these very people, on the occasion of an event insignificant for Gusev and for me, I wished to say: think of yourselves and of your life, of what you are spending on the spiritual powers given you by God. Look into your soul, take pity on yourselves."

Soon after Gusev's banishment L. N. made ready to visit his friend Chertkov, also banished, who was living at Kryokshino, the estate of his uncle Pashkov, not far from Moscow, on the Bryansk road.

For this it was necessary to come to Moscow, where L. N. had not been for 8 years now. Before setting out for Chertkov's, L. N. spent a night in Moscow and on the following day, having been to Zimmermann's music shop and having listened there to the playing of the new mechanical musical apparatus the "Mignon," set out for Chertkov's from the Bryansk station.

At Chertkov's L. N. stayed two weeks, from 4 to 18 September.

From the 18th to the 19th L. N. spent again in Moscow, and in the evening visited a cinematograph, which afforded him no satisfaction.

On the daytime of the 19th L. N. had again to set out for Yasnaya. The Moscow public, having learned of this, arranged a noisy ovation for L. N. We borrow the account of this from the description of an eyewitness, a contributor to *Russkoye Slovo* (Russian Word):

> "At the Kursk station, even more than an hour before the departure of the train, long before the arrival of Lev Nikolaevich, an enormous crowd began to gather.
>
> As the time of the train's departure drew nearer, the crowd kept growing and growing.
>
> Almost the whole square before the Kursk station was packed.
>
> Here were representatives of all strata of the population of Moscow. Artists, performers, journalists, tradesmen, workmen...
>
> And among them, in predominant number, various student youth.
>
> A mass of male students and female students. In the group of journalists there stand two deputies — V. A. Maklakov and the former secretary of the second Duma, M. V. Chelnokov. The crowd kept swelling and reached several thousand persons.
>
> Diligently the photographers and cinematographers were at work, taking shots of the separate groups of those waiting.
>
> — He's coming, he's coming... — was suddenly heard in the crowd.
>
> In the distance, from around the corner, a carriage appeared.
>
> Part of the crowd surged to meet it, in a moment surrounded the carriage, barring its further way.
>
> The carriage halted some 200 sazhens from the approach to the station — all around was full of people.
>
> In an instant all, as one man, took off their hats.
>
> A thunderous 'hurrah' rang out. Somehow they began little by little to clear a passage, and the carriage at a slow pace began to move toward the station.
>
> Lev Nikolaevich, having taken off his hat, sitting bareheaded in the carriage, bowed amiably to all. Slowly, at last, the carriage drove up to the steps of the station.
>
> Accompanied on one side by Sofya Andreyevna, on the other by V. G. Chertkov, L. N. began to mount toward the doors of the station.
>
> It is impossible to describe the inconceivable crush which kept growing around L. N. and the persons accompanying him.
>
> Something incredible was taking place, and it seemed unthinkable to make one's way inside the station.
>
> The loud, joyful 'hurrah' did not cease for a single minute.
>
> Somehow, with great difficulty, L. N. Tolstoy makes his way inside the station and, accompanied by the thousand-strong crowd that does not fall behind, comes out onto the platform.
>
> Streaming from all the doors and windows, the public in one instant fills to overflowing the whole platform of the Kursk station, with all its tracks. Not only that — they station themselves in the carriages standing near the train, on the locomotives, on the barriers, and so forth.
>
> The train in which Lev Nikolaevich was to travel stood at the third platform. It begins to seem that it will be impossible for Lev Nikolaevich, surrounded on all sides, to make his way there.
>
> — A chain, a chain, gentlemen... Form a chain, — voices are heard. The students and many of the crowd take up positions in files and, joining hands, form a chain.
>
> At last Lev Nikolaevich with his near ones approaches the train and goes to his carriage between the improvised files.
>
> The 'hurrah' does not cease and keeps growing.
>
> Somehow they reach the second-class carriage.
>
> They enter the carriage.
>
> — Sit down, sit down quickly in the compartment and lock it, — someone says.
>
> Into the compartment sit Lev Nikolaevich, Sofya Andreyevna, and V. G. Chertkov. The whole platform and the neighboring tracks are packed. From the windows of the carriage there is to be seen a sea of heads.
>
> Closer and closer it grows at the open window of the carriage into which L. N. had gone.
>
> A minute, and Lev Nikolaevich comes out of his compartment and goes up to the window.
>
> The greetings and shouts take on grandiose dimensions.
>
> The enthusiasm and exaltation grow.
>
> In a few seconds shouts are heard:
>
> — Quiet, quiet, gentlemen... Lev Nikolaevich is going to speak...
>
> With difficulty they manage to restrain the shouts and exclamations.
>
> Silence falls at last.
>
> Turning to all, Lev Nikolaevich says:
>
> — I never expected such a joy, such a manifestation of sympathy on the part of people... Thank you.
>
> Tears hinder him from speaking.
>
> From all sides it rings out:
>
> — To you, to you, thank you...
>
> The third bell. The train begins to move.
>
> — Thank you, friends, thank you... — says Lev Nikolaevich from the window.
>
> In answer shouts ring out:
>
> — Live yet a hundred years! Work for our benefit... Till we meet again... till we meet again...
>
> Lev Nikolaevich answers:
>
> — Till we meet again, if God grant...
>
> A general 'hurrah' sees off the disappearing train."

These tempestuous send-offs were both agreeable and hard for L. N. In his diary he recorded:

> "An enormous crowd all but crushed me. Chertkov rescued me. I was afraid for Sonya and Sasha. The feeling is again the same, and the unpleasant is the stronger, because it is plain that this is already the feeling of a crowd."

In a letter to a friend he wrote: "These send-offs have stirred up in me the old wound of vanity."

All these agitations, the fatigue of the journey, brought L. N. to a state of illness. Already in the railway carriage, on the approach to Shchekino, those around him began to notice in him certain strange nervous symptoms, and when he had taken his seat in the conveyance and set off for Yasnaya, delirium began, and then a deep faint. On the next day L. N. recovered, and soon his powers were once more restored.

On 27 September I had the good fortune to come to Yasnaya and to spend a whole week with L. N.

I then set down, according to my own impression, that which I had occasion to live through and that which was going on around L. N.; I reproduce here the essential part of the account, printed at that same time in *Russkiye Vedomosti*:

> "A golden autumn. Mild, warm, crystal-pure air. Amid this wondrous nature, this year somehow especially long caressing people with bright sunny days, there flows in the Yasnaya Polyana house the pure, bright, mild autumn of the life of Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy.
>
> Many agitations he has had to live through of late. And these agitations have not passed without effect and have left their imprint of fatigue upon his still strong nature. The last joyful and at the same time disquieting agitation of the passage through Moscow has not passed without a trace.
>
> L. N. feels himself even now still somewhat fatigued, listless, notices a weakening of memory and sometimes of sight. All this is signs which ought to compel the people who love him to guard his repose with especial tenderness and care. And this, unfortunately, is not observed by all. True, there are not many visitors now; but the correspondence flows in in abundance: from 20 to 30 letters daily, besides newspapers, journals, manuscripts, books, and other wrappered packets and parcels.
>
> Officious protectors have taken away from L. N. the helper most needful to him. True, he is replaced with love by L. N.'s daughter with her assistant, but they barely manage to keep up with the extraordinarily productive literary work of Lev Nikolaevich, copying out for him the drafts of several new works of his which he is carrying on in parallel; and the enormous correspondence gives still greater work in the packing, copying, and registering of L. N.'s answers. All this work is performed joyfully, by loving hands, but at times there is felt the need of the help of an additional person. The question of replacing Gusev is very difficult to resolve. The nearness of such a man to Lev Nikolaevich makes him almost a member of the family, and such people are not made to order.
>
> The correspondence received by L. N., however, does not always bring him joy and satisfaction. With each post half, and sometimes more, of the letters bear a petitioning character. To Lev Nikolaevich people address requests of every kind, sometimes of the most original sort. Many requests for the sending of money, requests for the reading of the manuscripts of beginning authors, requests for the finding of posts, for placement in the service, for intercessions before the court in civil, criminal, and political cases, requests for the admission of cinematographic shots, and so forth. The requests are often quite physically unrealizable. But the greater part of them are written in the tragic, despairing tone of people resorting to a sole means of salvation, and therefore they cannot but agitate L. N. Especially hard for him are the requests for money — hard by that distrust, that unwillingness to understand him, which is expressed in them.
>
> — Strange people, — L. N. said to me with bitterness, — why, I have already many times declared, both orally and in writing and in print, that I have no money and cannot help with money, and yet they do not cease. For if I am deceiving, then there is no point in turning for help to such a deceiver. And if I am not deceiving, then there is still less ground for turning to me.
>
> Several times L. N. was on the point of printing again in the newspapers such a declaration. But this is hard for him to do, and perhaps my note will deliver him from it.
>
> Of a different character are the letters which, though they too cause L. N. to be agitated and to suffer, yet often draw him out to an answer.
>
> This is a large section of letters, indicating the spiritual rout of the so-called intelligent people of our time.
>
> The contemporary currents of thought during the last 20–30 years of the life of Russian society have cluttered up the souls of people with complex theories, unrealizable hopes, castles in the air, and all sorts of presumptuous illusions, and all this, like houses of cards, has collapsed at the first breath of life.
>
> And in their place there has remained such a frightful desert of dissatisfaction, of repentance for the squandering of one's powers, and of the consciousness of the aimlessness of existence, that cries for help are heard more and more often from the wounded lying upon this field of life's battle.
>
> I think that L. N. can help these people; but I would give the young people who turn to him one piece of advice, which, I think, their own reasonable consciousness too will dictate to them, if they will with care and love think about that man to whom they are turning for help.
>
> Before writing to him, or going to him, or in another way disturbing him, taking from him his precious time and powers, which he spends for the good of us all, one ought to read attentively all that he has written, to think over, both alone and in common, what one has read, to take counsel with people who have already made certain steps upon this path, and only in the last extremity, in case of insoluble contradictions, having made use of all that he has already given us, only then to turn to him...
>
> Hard for L. N. are the letters of beginning writers, who send him their works in great number, with a request to read them and to express his opinion. Such letters and manuscripts come almost every day. Picture to yourself L. N., having abandoned all his works and occupied in reading through the folios sent him, of the most impossible, naïve content and careless in form.
>
> True, all these burdens are sometimes redeemed by the letters of sensitive, simple people, immediately perceiving the truth, childishly rejoicing in the bright ray they have glimpsed and ready to go to all lengths, if only this shining ray will light up the path of their life. But again, in counterweight to these expressions of sympathy flying from all ends of the world, there appears a whole series of abusive letters, filled with every kind of foul language addressed to Lev Nikolaevich and bearing witness either to the madness or to the unscrupulousness of their authors. L. N. receives these letters with meekness, humility, and, of course, with regret for the spiritual darkness of those who wrote them.
>
> There come also letters of general interest and worthy of publication.
>
> Interesting too is L. N.'s correspondence with Hindus. His writings have for them great significance. Recently one of them informed L. N. of the existence in the Transvaal of a Hindu society of non-resisters, admirers of L. N., for whom this Hindu translates and prints in the Hindu language the writings of Tolstoy. This was the now world-famous Gandhi.
>
> Exiles in Siberia ask him to send books of a religious-philosophical direction.
>
> Such is the real worldwide influence of L. N. Tolstoy. Such is the unceasing work of his still vigorous spirit. How precious to us, who love L. N., ought to be the days and hours of his life, and how needful it is for us to guard his repose."

On 22 October, walking about the village and having come home, L. N. recorded his impressions in his diary. Subsequently he worked this entry up into an artistic story. But this diary entry is precious for its immediate freshness and gives a vivid picture of L. N.'s spiritual experiences:

> "I went to the village and experienced one of the strongest impressions. I had a cry. There was a send-off of the lads being led off to be soldiers. The sounds of a large accordion dashingly strike up 'The Lady,' and the crowd accompanies them, and the wailing of the women — mothers, sisters, aunts. They go to the carts at the end of the village and turn into the houses where their comrades are. All six of them. One married. The wife — a town woman, smartly dressed, with large gold earrings, with a drawn-in waist, in a fashionable dress with lace.
>
> The crowd — for the most part women, and, as always, the bustling, lively, dear lads and little girls. The peasant men go alongside or stand at the gates, with a stern, serious expression of face; laments are heard — one cannot make out what, but sobbing and hysterical laughter. Many weep silently.
>
> I got into talk with Vasily Matveyev, the father of the departing married son: I spoke about vodka. He drinks and smokes. 'From boredom.' Anikanychev came up, the village elder, and a little old man. I did not recognize him. It was red-haired Prokofy. I began to point at the lads, to ask who this was. The accordion, without ceasing, kept pouring forth. We all walk along; as we walk I ask the little old man about a tall fine fellow, well dressed, stepping smartly, gallantly. — 'And whose is that one?' — 'Mine,' — and the little old man burst out sobbing, and so did I. The accordion, without ceasing, kept working. We turned in at Vasily's. He offered vodka. The woman cut bread. The lads barely sipped. We went out beyond the village, stood a while, took leave, the lads conferred about something, then came up to me to say good-bye. They pressed my hand, and again I wept. Then I sat with Vasily in the cart. On the way he flattered me. 'Die here; we shall carry you on our heads.' We drove to Yemelyan's. There is no one but the Yasenki folk. I went home. I met my horse and rode home."

A few days later he writes a touching letter to his exiled friend Gusev. This whole letter is a cry of living love:

> "Dear, dear, beloved friend Nikolai Nikolaevich, however near you have been to me before that trial to which you have been subjected, you are now still nearer and dearer to me, not only because I feel my own guilt, that all which you are experiencing by all justice ought to have been experienced by me, but simply because you are bearing, and bearing so well, the trial sent you.
>
> I cannot but feel myself guilty before you, since those words which are imputed to you in accusation are my words, and it is I who must answer for them. I know that you do not reproach me, but still I cannot but ask you to forgive me and not to change toward me your kind feeling, dear to me. May God help you to bear your trial without changing the most precious thing for you, your loving attitude toward people who, for whatever motives, do or try to do evil to their brother who loves them.
>
> May God help you.
>
> Always one who loved you, and now, as one conscious of his guilt before you, with especial tenderness loving you, your friend and brother Lev Tolstoy.
>
> I think it is needless to write to you that to fulfill any commission, any wish of yours, would be for me a great consolation and joy."

In November L. N. records a remarkable thought, indicating his broad understanding of religion:

> "I do not wish to be a Christian, just as I should not have advised, nor should wish, that there should be Brahmanists, Buddhists, Confucianists, Taoists, Mohammedans, and others. We must all find, each in his own faith, that which is common to all, and, having renounced the exclusive, one's own, hold to that which is common."

In December L. N. once more underwent a severe indisposition, the fever reaching 42°, and once more his mighty organism bore it and brought him out to the work of the last year of his life.
