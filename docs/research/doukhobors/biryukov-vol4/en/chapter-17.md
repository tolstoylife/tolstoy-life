# Volume IV, Chapter 17. 1909–1910. The Will

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-17.md`](../ru/chapter-17.md).

---

In carrying his world-view into life, L. N. naturally came to the denial of property, which is always upheld by violence. One of the most unlawful kinds of property is literary property. Of course, L. N. had to renounce it. But, as in many other applications of his understanding of life, he met in some members of his family with obstacles to the carrying out of his intention, and the struggle for this carrying out continued to the end of his life. In this struggle we can mark three moments. The first is L. N.'s striving to free himself from literary property. The second moment is the striving of some members of his family to obstruct this and to transfer the right of property to the family. The third moment is the striving of L. N.'s friends, with Vl. Gr. Chertkov at their head, to help in the carrying out of this liberation, for the speediest transfer of it to common use.

It is of the combinations and conflicts among these three strivings that those relations consist, the sharpening of which brought so much suffering to L. N., especially in the last years of his life.

The first act of renunciation, although an incomplete one, of literary property was accomplished in 1891.

L. N., courageously overcoming family obstacles, declared in print that he renounced all rights and remuneration for everything written by him and appearing in print after 1881. Thus, by this renunciation he still left at the disposal of the family all his great artistic works, which brought in a large income.

In this same year there took place the division of his landed property among his children. Thus, L. N. little by little freed himself from his property even during his lifetime.

A few years later, in 1895, he wrote down in his diary how he would wish his manuscripts to be disposed of after his death.

We have placed this important document in its proper place. Here we shall only recall its special character, wholly corresponding to that Christian creed which L. N. professed: in point 4 he says: "The right of publication of my earlier works — the ten volumes and the *ABC Book* — I beg my heirs to hand over to the public, that is, to renounce the author's rights. But I only beg it, in no wise bequeath it. To do so will be good. It will be good for you too; if you do not do so — that is your affair. It means you are not yet ready to do it. The fact that my works have been sold these last ten years has been for me the most grievous matter of my life."

Here L. N. is speaking of the works of the first period, considering that of the works of the second period he had already declared publicly in 1891. Thus, this declaration supplemented the preceding one.

Characteristic here is the phrase "I beg, but do not bequeath." Indeed, how can a Christian bequeath, that is, require of his relatives, and moreover lean upon authority, that they perform such-and-such acts when he will be no more, when he will not know the circumstances in which these acts will have to be performed! The most beneficent intention may prove to be an evil deed under new, altered conditions.

So L. N. thought and acted when these thoughts and actions flowed freely from his soul.

Maria Lvovna made copies of this testament. One copy was given for safekeeping to V. G. Chertkov, another to Sergei Lvovich Tolstoy, and a third was kept by Maria Lvovna.

In the autumn, before his departure for Gaspra, L. N. signed the copy which was kept by Maria Lvovna. In this way there now appeared a signed expression of his will. It was kept by Maria Lvovna. Of this Ilya Lvovich learned and told his mother. Sofya Andreyevna became greatly agitated and demanded it for herself. But M. L. was at that time at Pirogovo. Then there took place the move to the Crimea, and the matter was for a time forgotten.

Upon their return from the Crimea, S. A. once more put forward her claims to this testament, and Maria Lvovna, with L. N.'s consent, in order to avoid painful scenes before the sick L. N., had to give it up to her mother, who, in all probability, destroyed it.

Thus, the first attempt of L. N. to express his will was, so to speak, beaten back. At least S. A. so thought, not knowing or having forgotten that this will was recorded in the diary and in two other copies as well — at Chertkov's and at Sergei Lvovich's.

Let us hear what Sofya Andreyevna herself says about this event, about the violence done by her to Lev Nikolaevich's will. Here is an entry from her diary that touches upon this matter:

> "10 October 1902. When there took place the division of property in our family at the wish and disposition of Lev Nikolaevich, my daughter Masha, who was then already of age, renounced any share in her parents' inheritance, both in the present and in the future. Knowing her untruthful and broken nature (*) I did not believe her, took her share in my own name, and made out upon this capital a testament in her favor. But my death did not come about, and Masha married a pauper, Obolensky, and took her share, in order to support herself and him. Having no rights for the future, she for some reason copied out, secretly from me, from her father's diary of 1895 a whole series of his wishes after his death.
>
> (* In conveying this entry accurately, I cannot but be indignant at these words, which referred to the pure, ideal nature of Maria Lvovna Tolstaya. (P. B.) *)
>
> There, among other things, it is written that he had suffered from the sale of his works and would wish that the family should not sell them after his death either. When Lev Nikolaevich was dangerously ill in July of last year, 1901, Masha quietly from everyone gave her father this paper, copied out by her from the diary — to sign with his name, which he, being ill, did.
>
> This was extremely unpleasant to me, when I learned of it by chance. To give Lev Nikolaevich's works over into common property I consider both bad and senseless. I love my family and wish for it the best welfare, but in giving the works over into public domain, we should be rewarding rich publishing firms, like Marx, Tsetlin (Jews) and others. I told Lev Nikolaevich that if he died before me, I should not fulfil his wish and should not renounce the rights to his works, and that if I considered this good or just, I would in his lifetime have given him this joy of renouncing the rights, but that after death this no longer has any sense.
>
> And now, having undertaken the publication of Lev Nikolaevich's works, by his own wish having left the right of publication with myself and having sold it to no one, in spite of offers of large sums for the right of publication, it became unpleasant to me, as indeed it always had been, that in Masha's hands there was a paper, signed by Lev Nikolaevich, that he would not wish to sell his works after his death. I did not know the exact content and asked Lev Nikolaevich to give me this paper, having taken it from Masha.
>
> He very willingly did so and handed it to me. There happened that which I had by no means expected: Masha flew into a fury, her husband shouted yesterday God knows what, saying that he and Masha had been intending to make this paper public after Lev Nikolaevich's death, to make it known to the greatest number of people, so that all should know that Lev Nikolaevich had never wished to sell his works, but that his wife was selling them."

This entry gives us a clear picture of those passions which raged over Lev Nikolaevich's head and weighed down with intolerable heaviness his peace-loving soul.

Sofya Andreyevna's account does not quite coincide with what I conveyed above and what I had heard from the lips of Maria Lvovna and her husband. But this difference in details has no significance; it is very possible that I did not quite accurately remember the end of this story, and therefore I am ready to accept the factual sequence given by Sofya Andreyevna.

In one way or another, Lev Nikolaevich's numerous occupations, his constant labors, and his relations with people to whose needs he always readily responded, as it were postponed for a time the question of a new manifestation of his will. Moreover, V. G. Chertkov, who could have raised this question again, was still abroad in exile.

In all probability, on learning of the destruction of the important document, V. G. Chertkov in his correspondence with Lev Nikolaevich raised the question of the restoration of this document. It is very possible that L. N. did this also on his own initiative or at Maria Lvovna's reminder. In one way or another, in 1904 Lev Nikolaevich wrote Chertkov the following letter:

> "Dear friend Vladimir Grigoryevich!
>
> In 1895 I wrote something in the nature of a testament — that is, I expressed to people near to me my wishes as to how to dispose of what should remain after me. In this note I write that I beg my wife, Strakhov, and yourself to sort out all my papers. I beg this of you because I know your great love for me and your moral sensitiveness, which will indicate to you what to throw out, what to keep, and when and where and in what form to publish. I might add also that I trust you particularly because I know your thoroughness and conscientiousness in such work, and chiefly, our complete agreement in the religious understanding of life.
>
> At that time I wrote nothing to you about this; but now, after nine years, when Strakhov is no more and my death is in any case not far off, I consider it necessary to make good what was omitted and to express to you in person that I beg you to take upon yourself the labor of looking over and sorting out the papers that shall remain after me, and together with my wife to dispose of them as you shall find necessary.
>
> Besides those papers which are with you, I am sure that my wife or (in the event of her death before yours) my children will not refuse, in fulfillment of my wish, to communicate to you those papers also which are not with you, and to decide together with you how to dispose of them.
>
> To all these papers, except the diaries of the last years, I, frankly speaking, ascribe no significance and consider any use whatever of them to be wholly a matter of indifference. The diaries, on the other hand, if I should not succeed in expressing more accurately and clearly that which I write down in them, may have a certain significance, if only in those fragmentary thoughts which are set forth there. And therefore the publication of them, if there be omitted from them all that is incidental, personal, and superfluous, may be useful to people, and I hope that you will do this as well as you have hitherto done the extracts from my unpublished writings, and I beg this of you. I thank you for all your past labors over my writings and in advance for what you shall do with the papers remaining after me. Union with you has been one of the great joys of the last years of my life.
> Lev Tolstoy."

One would think that this document was wholly sufficient for all who respected the will of Lev Nikolaevich; but it did not bear the seal of authority, and it was held to be invalid.

The revolution of 1905 freed the exiles, V. G. returned from abroad and settled near Yasnaya Polyana. Evidently the question agitated Lev Nikolaevich how his will would be carried out after his death, for in the diary of 1908 he again repeats briefly his will, expressed by him in the diary of 1905.

The solemn time of the jubilee had passed, having of course frightfully increased the demand for L. N.'s works. And Sofya Andreyevna, reckoning with the moment, conceived the idea of issuing a new complete collected works of L. N. Tolstoy in 20 volumes; making use of certain censorship indulgences and of her connections, she resolved to include in it a large part of L. N.'s writings of a religious-philosophical character. A plan was drawn up for 20 volumes. Of course, this edition required large outlays, and S. A. wished to obtain guarantees that her outlays should not be wasted in vain — that is, that she should be free to issue this edition and to sell it off even in the event of L. N.'s death. As always, the question of a new edition, undertaken with a commercial aim, greatly agitated her, and these agitations and considerations weighed heavily upon L. N., upon his spiritual and physical health.

By some of the family the question was raised of selling all the works of Lev Nikolaevich to some one publisher on advantageous terms.

L. N. had as far back as the 1880s given Sofya Andreyevna a power of attorney for the conduct of the publishing affairs. This power of attorney gave her the possibility of concluding contracts with printing houses and paper suppliers, but in no way of selling the rights of publication. She consulted experienced jurists, and they convinced her that she possessed no such rights.

Seeing no other way out, S. A. resolved to publish herself. A part of this edition was issued during L. N.'s lifetime, and a part already after his death.

Interesting in this connection is the account of L. N.'s kinsman, Ivan Vasilyevich Denisenko, a jurist, who was staying at this time at Yasnaya. Both sides, trusting him as a person close to themselves, turned to him for advice. We take from this account the most essential part:

> "In July, when I was at Yasnaya Polyana," Iv. Vasilyevich relates, "S. A. called me into her bedroom and, having shown me a general power of attorney for the management of affairs, given her long since by Lev Nikolaevich, asked me whether under this power of attorney she could sell the right of publication of Lev Nikolaevich's works, and chiefly, institute proceedings against Sergeenko and some schoolmaster of a military gymnasium for having compiled from Lev Nikolaevich's works collections and chrestomathies, in view of the fact that these collections might cause great material loss to her new edition of the works.
>
> I was extremely surprised that Lev Nikolaevich's works up to '81 do not constitute her property, which I told her; to which she replied that no one knows that she publishes Lev Nikolaevich's works only by power of attorney, and asked me not to divulge this. I answered her that, in my opinion, she has no right to sell the right of publication of the works by the power of attorney she possesses; and to institute proceedings against the compilers of the collections, it would be necessary for her to have a special power of attorney from Lev Nikolaevich, which he, of course, would not give her.
>
> As far as I recall, S. A. said: 'Well, perhaps he will give it, I shall try.' Evidently, S. A. did 'try,' for L. N. records in his diary of 12 July:
>
> '…Yesterday evening it was hard from S. A.'s talk of publication and judicial proceedings. If she knew and understood how she alone poisons my last hours, days, months of life. But I cannot say it, and I do not hope for any effect on her of any words whatever…'"

Soon after this, Iv. Vas. Denisenko was approached by Lev Nikolaevich himself.

Iv. Vas. Denisenko relates this thus:

> "It seems, on the day after this, in the daytime, I went along the avenue passing between the flower-beds, and there quite unexpectedly met Lev Nikolaevich. His appearance struck me.
>
> He was stooped, his face exhausted, his eyes faded; he seemed weak, as I had never seen him.
>
> At our meeting he quickly seized me by the hands and said with tears in his eyes:
>
> — Dear man, Ivan Vasilyevich, what is she doing with me, what is she doing with me! She demands of me a power of attorney for the institution of proceedings. Why, I cannot do this… This would be against my convictions.
>
> Then, having walked a few paces with me, he said to me:
>
> — I have a great request to make of you; only let it for the present remain between us, do not speak of it to anyone, not even to Sasha. Please draw up for me a paper in which I might announce for general information that all my works, whenever written by me, I hand over for general use. Besides this, I should wish to hand over all the land to the peasants.
>
> Lev Nikolaevich, in saying this, was terribly upset and nervously agitated. I said that I could not fulfil his request at once, since I should need to consult the laws and to learn the opinion of certain jurists, and that then I would draft for him what was desired and send it from Novocherkassk. Lev Nikolaevich expressed his agreement to this.
>
> On the day after this conversation, when he was going out for his morning walk, Lev Nikolaevich met me and, drawing me aside, said:
>
> — Ah, ah, what I told you yesterday! I was so upset that I had forgotten that I had long since given the land to the children and to my wife; but as for my works, do for me, all the same, what I asked you about."

Iv. Vas. Denisenko on returning home wrote of this to L. N., but this letter for some reason did not reach him.

These questions greatly agitated L. N.: in his diary of that time he records:

> "25 July. Yesterday I spoke with Ivan Vasilyevich. How hard it is to be rid of this filthy, sinful property. Help, help, help…"

In September 1909 L. N. was visiting Chertkov at Krekshino, near Moscow, on the estate of his kinsman Pashkov. In L. N.'s diary on 17 September there is recorded:

> "Spoke with Chertkov about the children's intention to appropriate the works given over to all. I do not wish to believe it."

At that same time, at Krekshino, the first formal testament was written, signed by three witnesses. Alex. Boris. Goldenweiser, one of the signers, relates this event thus in his reminiscences:

> "I found everyone very upset. Anna Konstantinovna said to me:
>
> — How good that you have come. L. N. has resolved to make a testament and wished to ask you to be a witness.
>
> This news greatly agitated me and very much touched me as a token of L. N.'s trust in me.
>
> We in the course of the day conferred several times about the form in which the testament was to be written so that it should have juridical significance, since L. N. had resolved to make a testament having not merely moral significance, having full ground to think that otherwise his will would remain unfulfilled.
>
> …Returning from the walk, Chertkov handed us the text of the testament, corrected and supplemented by L. N.'s hand on the sheet with the outline drawn up by us the day before. Al. Lvovna copied out this text, and Lev Nikolaevich went to his room to work.
>
> L. N. worked at his place rather long, and we began to be uneasy that Lev Nikolaevich could not bring himself to sign the testament copied out by Al. Lvovna. But there, L. N. came into the little room in which we were all awaiting him. He had a very solemn air, he was, evidently, agitated. He sat down at the table, glanced briefly at the copied text, took a pen and signed. After him the witnesses signed: I, Kalachev, and Sergeenko (the son). L. N. rose and thanked us, pressing our hands."

Here is the text of the testament written at that time:

## The Will

"I declare that I wish that all my works, literary productions and writings of every kind, both those already anywhere reprinted, and those not yet published, written or first printed since 1 January 1881, as also everything written by me before that date, but not yet printed, should not after my death constitute anyone's private property, but might be issued and reprinted gratuitously by all who shall wish it. I wish that all the manuscripts and papers which shall remain after me be handed over to Vladimir Grigoryevich Chertkov, in order that after my death too he should dispose of them as he disposes of them now, in order that all my writings shall be gratuitously accessible to all who desire to acquaint themselves with them. I beg Vladimir Grigoryevich Chertkov also to choose such a person or persons to whom he should hand over this authorisation in the event of his death.
Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy."

Krekshino, 18 September 1909.

At the signing of the present testament there were present and do hereby certify that Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy at the drawing up of the present testament was of sound mind and firm memory.
Free Artist Alexander Borisovich Goldenweiser.
Townsman Alexei Petrovich Sergeenko.
Alexander Vasilyevich Kalachev, townsman.
The present testament was copied out by Alexandra Tolstaya.

Al. Bor. Goldenweiser continues his account:

> "Al. Lvovna was at the office of the attorney-at-law Muravyev, showed him the testament, and he said to her that as a juridical document it is no good at all for many reasons, among them because the law does not provide for the possibility of 'leaving an inheritance to no one.' One must without fail leave it to someone, who should then dispose of it according to the will of Lev Nikolaevich. Muravyev promised to think it over and to send an approximate text of a testament to Yasnaya; Al. Lvovna would hand it over to L. N., who would decide how to proceed.
>
> For this purpose," Alex. Boris. relates further, "2 or 3 times conferences took place at his (Muravyev's), at which were present Chertkov, F. A. Strakhov, and myself. When the draft text of the testament was more or less established in several versions, it was necessary to take these drafts to L. N. so that he might read them and settle on one of them or reject them all, if he should find them not in accord with his suppositions.
>
> It was to be done on 26 October. I was on that day occupied and could not go, so that this errand F. A. Strakhov, a friend of Lev Nikolaevich's, took upon himself."

Fyodor Alexeyevich related his journey in a feuilleton of the *Peterburgskaya Gazeta*, and we take from his account the essential part. When Strakhov had set forth before Lev Nikolaevich the substance of the matter and had proposed to him that he confirm the testament, Lev Nikolaevich uttered remarkable words, vividly expressing his inward attitude toward this affair:

— Hard is all this affair to me. Nor is this needed — to safeguard the dissemination of one's thoughts by means of measures of one kind or another. Why, Christ — though it is strange that I as it were compare myself with him — did not trouble himself that someone might not appropriate his thoughts to his own private property, nor did he himself record his thoughts, but uttered them boldly and went for them to the cross. And these thoughts have not perished. Nor can the word perish without a trace, if it expresses the truth, and if the man uttering this word deeply believes in its truth. But all these external measures of security are only from our unbelief in that which we have uttered.

Further Fyod. Alex. Strakhov relates thus:

> "Having said this, Lev Nikolaevich went out of the study, and I, remaining alone, in meditation moved over to the window and, looking at the lawn-grass strewn with yellow leaves, began to consider what I should do further: whether to make some objection to his declaration, or to depart from Yasnaya Polyana with nothing.
>
> When Lev Nikolaevich returned to his study, there had ripened in me a firm resolve not to leave this matter so, and, plucking up courage, I addressed Lev Nikolaevich with the following words:
>
> — Will you permit me, Lev Nikolaevich, to express my opinion on this matter?
>
> — Please, I beg you to do so, — he hastened to answer me, and, having seated himself in his armchair in the corner, prepared to listen to me.
>
> — I understand, Lev Nikolaevich, — I began, — and fully value that height, standing on which, you have judged this matter. But to understand and to judge anything in the light of the truth that has been disclosed to us — this is one thing, in this sphere we are fully free, but to act — this is quite another, because our action one must always bring into accord with the given conditions of time and place. There, you mentioned Christ. He, indeed, did not need to trouble himself about the unhindered dissemination of his word. But why? Because he did not write, and by the conditions of the time received no fee for his thoughts. The conditions of our time, however, are such that if you undertake nothing for the safeguarding of the universal use of your writings, then by this you will indirectly contribute to the establishment of the rights of private property in them on the part of your family. But if you take care to hand them on by inheritance, even into private property, but to a person for whom your will, expressed by you in '95, shall be sacred, then it is just by this that you will give them over for universal use.
>
> — A weighty argument, — Lev Nikolaevich answered me to this, and added that he was just then going out to ride on horseback and that, having well considered this matter during his walk, he would give me his final answer on his return home.
>
> …A little later than this Lev Nikolaevich rode off on horseback. On his return he lay down to sleep. After his sleep we dined together in the hall… and after dinner he at once went to his study and led there with him Alexandra Lvovna and myself.
>
> — I shall surprise you with my extreme decision, — he addressed us both with a kindly smile on his face. — I wish to be *plus royaliste que le roi*. I wish, Sasha, to give you everything — you understand, everything, not excluding that about which a reservation had been made in that newspaper declaration of mine.
>
> We stood before him, struck as by lightning by these his words: 'one' and 'everything.' He uttered them with such simplicity, as though he were communicating to us the most insignificant adventure that had befallen him during his walk.
>
> — Better and simpler it will be if I make it all out to you alone, — Lev Nikolaevich again addressed Alexandra Lvovna, — and this is wholly natural, because you are the last of all my children, you live with me, you sympathise with me, you help me so much in all my affairs.
>
> — Well, as you yourself know best, papa, — Alexandra Lvovna said through her teeth.
>
> — A little heavy it will be for you, eh?
>
> — What is one to do? I look upon this as my duty…
>
> — But how, Lev Nikolaevich? What then is your will concerning all those writings, the income from which Sofya Andreyevna has hitherto enjoyed and which she has grown accustomed to consider your gift and then her own property, — I involuntarily intervened with my question, not yet being able to come to myself after L. N.'s unexpected decision.
>
> — All this Sasha can grant her for life, in accordance with my will; in a word, do so that my testament shall introduce in regard to her no changes. Well, but all these trifles and details you will think over together with Vladimir Grigoryevich, — he addressed Alexandra Lvovna. — Only it will be hard for you!
>
> With these words Lev Nikolaevich brought to an end the conversation about the inheritance, which was, evidently, beginning to weigh upon him. Having noticed this, Alexandra Lvovna and I went out of his study."

When F. A. Strakhov returned from his journey and conveyed to V. G. Chertkov its results, V. G. wrote to Lev Nikolaevich:

> "Concerning the dispositions about your writings after death, I cannot express to you how glad I am that you have resolved to act decisively. To cut off a leg in gangrene, after a certain degree of the spread of the disease, is sometimes much better in every respect than any other means. In bequeathing everything to A. L., you will put an end to that horror which has been going on around you for so many years now, and you become master of the situation in the sense that it will now depend on you to improve the situation, which hitherto you have not been in a position to do. When Strakhov related to me that you, riding on horseback, before your conscience had decided the matter in the most extreme sense, I at first rejoiced over your decision, but at the same time felt certain doubts as to whether your family would not be in the right to consider themselves deceived by you in regard to the writings of the first period, which you had many years ago made over to them. But at first I gave no scope to my inclination to express to you my doubts. I thought to myself: in many a long day you have resolved to set up a rebuff to that horror which was ever growing around you, and the degree to which it has reached you do not even now know in full. He has decided this, I said to myself, alone before his God. Another man should not intrude into this region. But afterward I more and more began to feel that one ought to remind you of what you had already earlier made over to others, 'as if you had died.' And, transposing myself into the position of your family, I understood, as it seemed to me, the soundness of their forthcoming surprise and indignation in regard to that part of the 'literary heritage' which you by your direction had permitted them to consider their own in the future. And I then felt that it was necessary to remind you and to point out to you this side of the question, in order that your final decision should be undertaken in one direction or the other without losing sight of this side.
>
> The rest Alex. Borisovich will relate to you."

According to Lev Nikolaevich's wish, the testament was again drawn up by Muravyev in juridical form and delivered to L. N.

This time the testament was brought to him by Alex. Borisovich Goldenweiser. It was early in the morning of 1 November, when all in the house were still asleep except L. N. Here is what Alex. Borisovich relates of this.

> "Lev Nikolaevich willingly began to write out the text of the testament himself, taking great care not to make any blots, in which he wholly succeeded. After him I signed the testament as a witness. Strakhov was not yet there. A few minutes later he arrived. I went with him to Lev Nikolaevich.
>
> L. N. was very anxious that it was late and that Sofya Andreyevna might at any minute come in, and he closed all the doors of his room. Strakhov signed. I hid the testament in the portfolio and carried it downstairs to my place."

Subsequently L. N. decided that to Alexandra Lvovna one must add also Tatyana Lvovna, and the testament had to be recopied again, which L. N. did at Chertkov's house, at Telyatinki, on 17 June 1910.

Into this third testament there crept some formal error, and it had to be recopied for the fourth time. Here is how A. P. Sergeenko relates this final act:

> "On 22 July 1910, in the daytime, about three o'clock, into the yard of the Telyatinki farmstead Alexander Borisovich Goldenweiser rode in swiftly on horseback. He told us that he had ridden with Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy out from Yasnaya Polyana for a walk, and when they had ridden a fair way off, L. N., having resolved on that day to write the testament, sent him to Telyatinki, to bring with him to the place where he had appointed a meeting witnesses for being present at the drawing-up of his testament. Alexander Borisovich very much hurried us to get ready more quickly. The horses were at once saddled, and he, Radynsky, and I, the three of us, galloped off to L. N. The place where he was to await us was about two versts from Yasnaya Polyana, near the little village of Grumond. We chose the shortest direction, and therefore rode without a road along a brook flowing through a birch wood. Riding out of the wood within sight of Grumond, we began to look about for L. N. Before us and on the sides was elevated ground, but nowhere was he to be seen. We began to be uneasy, but, riding farther, saw him on a hillock that had earlier been hidden from us. L. N. was on his horse, which was turned toward us and shifting from foot to foot. The figure of L. N., in a white hat and a white shirt and with his white beard, on the handsome Delir with his arched neck, stood out picturesquely on the top of the hillock, behind which only the sky was to be seen.
>
> Rejoicing to see him, we rode up to him more quickly. Having greeted us, at a quiet pace he rode in the direction of the village, and we after him. We rode through the village, descended the hill, and L. N. directed his horse onto another hill.
>
> — What conspirators we are, — he remarked jestingly.
>
> We rode in single file. Having ridden up the hill, L. N. set off at a light trot across a large mown rye-field, with shocks standing about everywhere, toward the enormous state forest of the Zasek. Riding up to it, he for a moment paused his horse in hesitation, which way to go. But at once he directed it straight into the wood, at first along a narrow road which there turned up, and then, leaving the road, began to take the most unexpected, winding direction, as if he wished to lead us into the depths. His Delir, accustomed for several years to carrying him through woods and along impassable roads, obeyed the slightest movement of his hand, went boldly, as along a well-known road. But our horses were at a loss. We had every moment to bend our heads under sagging branches or to push branches aside. L. N. did this lightly and from habit. In the depth of the wood he halted at a great stump and began to dismount. We also dismounted and tied the horses to the trees. L. N. sat down on the stump and, having taken out the English reservoir pen pinned to his blouse, asked us to give him all that was needful for writing. I gave him paper and the cardboard, prepared by me for this purpose, on which to write. And Alexander Borisovich held before him the draft of the testament. Throwing one leg over the other and placing the cardboard with the paper on his knee, L. N. began to write: 'one thousand nine hundred ten, July twenty-second day.' He at once noticed a slip he had made in writing 'twenty' with the letter 't,' and wished either to correct it or to take a fresh sheet, but he thought better of it, remarking, smiling:
>
> — Well, let them think that I was illiterate.
>
> Then he added:
>
> — I shall set it down also in figures, so that there be no doubt — and after the word 'July' inserted in parentheses '22' in figures.
>
> It was hard for him, sitting on the stump, to follow the draft, and he asked Alexander Borisovich to read it to him. Alexander Borisovich began distinctly to read the draft, and L. N. carefully traced out the words, making double hyphens at the end and at the beginning of the lines, as it seems was done in old times, and as L. N. sometimes did in his letters, when he tried to write especially clearly and legibly. He at first wrote the lines too closely, and when he saw that there still remained much space, he said:
>
> — One must write more spreadingly, so as to go over to the other page, — and increased the distance between the lines.
>
> When at the end of the testament he had to sign his name, he asked:
>
> — Should I write 'count'?
>
> We said that one need not write it, and he did not.
>
> Then we also signed, the witnesses. L. N. said to us:
>
> — Well, thank you.
>
> After this I gave Lev Nikolaevich a paper in which, at his commission, his supplementary instructions had been expressed. He attentively read it through and said that one must alter two places. One place, where it was written that to Countess Sofya Andreyevna Tolstaya was granted life-long use of the works published before 1881, he said that one must wholly omit. In another place, where it was said that V. G. Chertkov, as before, should publish his works, he said that one must add the words 'on the former bases' — that is, not pursuing any material personal aims.
>
> — Lest it should be thought, — Lev Nikolaevich remarked, — that Vladimir Grigoryevich shall derive from this matter any personal advantage.
>
> L. N. returned me this paper, and a few days later reminded Vladimir Grigoryevich of it, asking that it be sent to him in final form, in order to sign it.
>
> L. N. rose from the stump and went to his horse.
>
> — How heavy are all these juridical cavils, — he said to me in meditation, evidently recalling all the formalities of the testament.
>
> With ease unusual for an 82-year-old man he sprang upon the horse.
>
> — Well, farewell, — he said, holding out his hand to me.
>
> — Farewell, Lev Nikolaevich. Thank you, — I said to him.
>
> And I said to him 'thank you' because, strictly speaking, through my fault it had come about that he was again writing the testament that day. The matter was that in the preceding testament, written by him a few days before, through my oversight something had been omitted in the words of the witnesses, without which the testament lost its juridical significance, and for this reason L. N. had to write it again, and I felt my fault before L. N.
>
> — But for what are you thanking me? — said L. N. — A great thank-you to you for having helped me in this matter.
>
> And I clearly saw by the expression of L. N.'s face that, although all this matter was hard for him, he did it with a firm consciousness of moral necessity. In L. N. one saw no wavering. In the course of this half-hour spent with him I saw how clearly, calmly, and deliberately he did everything."

Of this event L. N. on the same day briefly entered in his diary: "Wrote in the wood."

Thus, there appeared the definitive formal testament, subsequently confirmed by the district court. We give here its text:

> "One thousand nine hundred ten, July (22) twenty-second day, I, the undersigned, being of sound mind and firm memory, in the event of my death make the following disposition: all my literary productions, ever written up to this time and such as shall be written by me up to my death, both those already published and those unpublished, both artistic and of every other kind, finished and unfinished, dramatic and in every other form, translations, recastings, diaries, private letters, rough drafts, separate thoughts and notes — in a word, all without exception that has been written by me up to the day of my death, wherever it may be found and with whomever it may be kept, both in manuscript and equally also in print, and the right of literary property in all my works without exception, as also the manuscripts themselves and all the papers remaining after my death, I bequeath in full ownership to my daughter Alexandra Lvovna Tolstaya. In the event that my daughter Alexandra Lvovna Tolstaya should die before me, I bequeath all the above in full ownership to my daughter Tatyana Lvovna Sukhotina.
> Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy.
>
> Hereby I certify that the present testament has indeed been drawn up, written in his own hand, and signed by Count Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy, being of sound mind and firm memory.
> Free Artist Alexander Borisovich Goldenweiser.
> The same I certify, townsman Alexei Petrovich Sergeenko.
> The same I certify, son of a lieutenant-colonel Anatoly Dionisievich Radynsky."

The testament, as is seen, is drawn up in the name of Alexandra Lvovna, and in the event of her death — of Tatyana Lvovna. Since L. N. desired that V. G. Chertkov should dispose of the manuscripts, and since, on the other hand, the very fact of Lev Nikolaevich's drawing up a juridical testament might call forth in many people perplexity as to how L. N. could perform such an act, directly contrary to his convictions, an explanatory note was required, drawn up at L. N.'s commission by V. G. Chertkov and confirmed by L. N. Here is its content:

> "Since L. N. Tolstoy has written a testament, by which he leaves after his death all his writings 'in the ownership' of his daughter Alexandra Lvovna Tolstaya, and in the event of her death before his death — of Tatyana Lvovna Sukhotina, it is necessary to explain, in the first place, why, himself not acknowledging property, he has drawn up such a testament, and, in the second place, how he wishes that his writings should be dealt with after his death.
>
> To a 'formal' testament, having juridical force, Lev Nikolaevich resorted not for the sake of confirming to anyone whatever the property in his writings, but, on the contrary, in order to prevent the possibility of their being turned after his death into anyone's private property.
>
> In order to safeguard those whom he has commissioned to dispose of his writings according to his directions from the possibility of these writings being taken from them on the basis of the laws of inheritance, only one path lay open to Lev Nikolaevich: to write a testament, fitted out with all the formalities required by the law, in the name of such persons as he is sure will accurately fulfil his directions as to how to deal with his writings. The sole, therefore, aim of the 'formal' testament he has written consists in this — to prevent the presentation by any one of his family of their juridical rights in these writings in the event that these family members, disregarding L. N.'s will concerning his writings, should wish to turn them into their personal property.
>
> Lev Nikolaevich's will concerning his writings is this — he wishes that:
>
> 1) All his works, literary productions and writings of every kind, both those already anywhere printed, and those not yet published, should not after his death constitute anyone's private property, but might be issued and reprinted by all who shall wish it.
>
> 2) That all manuscripts and papers (among them diaries, drafts, letters, etc.), which shall remain after him, be handed over to V. G. Chertkov in order that the latter, after L. N.'s death, occupy himself with the looking-over of them and the publication of what he shall find in them desirable for publication, whereby in the material respect L. N. asks V. G. Chertkov to conduct the matter on the same bases on which he had published L. N.'s writings during the latter's lifetime.
>
> 3) That V. G. Chertkov should choose such a person or persons, to whom he should hand over this authorisation in the event of his, Chertkov's, death, in order that this person or persons should likewise act in the event of their own death, and so on, until the need for this be at an end.
>
> 4) That those persons to whom L. N. has bequeathed 'formal' ownership in all his writings should bequeath this ownership to further persons, chosen by agreement with V. G. Chertkov or with those to whom shall pass the above-mentioned authorisation of Chertkov, and so on, until the need for this be at an end.
>
> I am completely in agreement with the content of this declaration, drawn up at my request and accurately expressing my wish.
> Lev Tolstoy."

From the correspondence of V. Gr. Chertkov and Al. Lvovna it is seen that V. Gr. proposed to her to announce the testament before the family, but Al. Lv. declined this proposal.

It would seem that all was going well, but L. N.'s soul was not at peace. Five days after the writing of the testament, on 27 July, V. G. Chertkov writes to L. N.:

> "Dear friend, I have just seen Al. Lvovna, who told me of what is going on around you. She sees much more than you do, because they do not stand on ceremony with her, and she for her part sees that which they do not show you…
>
> …The hard truth which it is necessary to convey to you consists in this — that all the scenes which have taken place these last weeks, the arrival of Lev Lvovich, and now of Andrei Lvovich, have had and have one definite practical aim. And though there were in this some really morbid phenomena, as there could not but be in so prolonged, intense, and wearisome a dissembling, yet these morbid phenomena too were skilfully exploited all for that one and the same aim.
>
> The aim has consisted in this — to remove me from you, and if possible Sasha too, by means of unrelenting joint pressure to extract from you whether you have written any testament depriving your family of your literary inheritance: if not, then by means of unrelenting watch over you up to your death to prevent you from doing so; and if you have, then to let you go nowhere until they shall have managed to invite Black-Hundred doctors, who should declare you fallen into senile dementia, in order to deprive your testament of significance…
>
> To forestall this sin and in general to break off this evil business, which is in preparation and with which your family is now intensely occupied at Yasnaya, is possible to us only and moreover by a very simple path: this is, without delay to leave Yasnaya for Kochety, where in surroundings preventing them from carrying out their evil business, we should be able calmly to consider how you are to proceed."

All these communications and counsels agitated Lev Nikolaevich, and there, soon, he records in his diary that the struggle into which the people close to him had drawn him is hard and odious to him. And that his task is to conduct this struggle in love, which, of course, was even for him in the extreme degree difficult.

Soon it became apparent that the decision adopted by L. N. was felt by himself too to be far from satisfactory.

At the end of July I arrived with my family to visit Lev Nikolaevich. I have already spoken in the preceding chapter of the morbid state in which we found Sofya Andreyevna.

I here adduce what was recorded by me from fresh memory in my diary concerning the question of the testament:

> "I remember that on one of my visits to Yasnaya Polyana I once went into Alexandra Lvovna's room, and she with a mysterious air told me, though in general terms, of the testament being prepared or already made. I told her then that I was very glad and sympathised greatly with L. N.'s expressed will about the passing of all his works into common ownership, but that I did not like that mystery, that conspiracy with which this important affair was surrounded. And, as I remember, Alexandra Lvovna also sympathised with me in this and expressed her dissatisfaction with the way in which this important affair was being carried out.
>
> Since then, I do not remember how long, I have heard little of this affair, and have heard only hints, knowing that something was being done, but taking no part in it. And I remember that this non-participation was pleasant to me. I feared, as a close friend of L. N., to be drawn into a matter from which it would be hard to refuse and which did not wholly coincide with my views.
>
> When then I came to L. N. at the end of July 1910, I saw that the matter had already been done, that it was kept in deep secrecy, but that S. A. already suspected the existence of the testament, was seeking it, eavesdropping on conversations, and in general was scenting a conspiracy contrary to her interests and to the interests of her family. This suspiciousness, this scenting, of course, intensified in her her enmity toward Chertkov, which, in conjunction with the aforementioned pathological fits, made the atmosphere at Yasnaya unbearable even for outsiders. What then was it to L. N. himself to endure it!
>
> It seemed to me that L. N. considered me also a participant in this conspiracy, and, not wishing to mislead him, I resolved in a frank talk to express to him my attitude toward it.
>
> Having chosen a convenient moment, after dinner, when L. N. usually sat in his study in the corner armchair and read or laid out a game of patience, giving himself up to meditation and rest, I went in to him and asked permission to speak with him.
>
> He, of course, with a kindly, joyful smile, agreed to my request, laid down the book he was reading. I sat down opposite him and said to him approximately the following:
>
> — L. N., I wanted to express to you my attitude toward your testament and toward the manner in which it has been carried out. I do not know all the details of this matter, since I took no immediate part in it. While ardently sympathising with its basic idea, that is, the handing over of all your works for common use, I am not satisfied with the manner in which it has been done, and I very much wished that you should know this attitude of mine, and, if it is mistaken, that you should point out to me my error, and in any case should not think that I am in agreement when I am not, should not think of me better than I am. I have no pretension to alter or to undertake anything in this matter; I simply wish to clear my conscience before you, to say what I think, whatever the consequences of this may be. I want to tell you that the conspiratorial secrecy of this matter weighs upon me. I feel that there is here something not quite right, if this has to be hidden from the family around you.
>
> L. N. listened attentively, and when I had stopped, he, as if recalling something, with a serious, thoughtful air said:
>
> — Yes, yes, you are right, of course, but how was it to be done otherwise?
>
> — L. N., — I answered, — it is very hard for me to give you advice, to teach you, but if you ask my opinion, then I think that you ought to have called together your whole family and even some of your friends as witnesses and declared to them your will.
>
> L. N. with an agitated voice said:
>
> — Yes, yes, of course, but I think that this is beyond my strength.
>
> — L. N., then it is better not to do it at all.
>
> — But how shall I lead my children into temptation? They will receive much money; Andryusha — what will become of him?
>
> — L. N., I do not think that the thousands of rubles which they will receive could change anything in their life. And in 50 years all the same all your works will become common property, or perhaps even earlier they will fall into the hands of some new publisher, who shall distribute them in enormous quantity. But all this is not so important in comparison with the evil that this conspiracy produces, and yet more, what will come later, when your children shall see that their expectations have been deceived.
>
> — Yes, yes, you are right…, — L. N. said with a kindly smile, with the expression of a certain regret over a mistake committed.
>
> I do not remember now how this conversation of ours ended. It seems that it soon passed over to something else, in all probability to some intimate theme from my or L. N.'s family life, since our souls at that minute were open to each other.
>
> On the next day in the morning I caught a glimpse of L. N. before his morning occupations, and when he was already sitting at his place and was occupied, and I was sitting in the dining room, I was called to her by Alexandra Lvovna, who was in her office, or in the 'remingtonnaya,' as it was called; she was there together with Varvara Mikhailovna Feokritova, her friend. On entering, I noticed that they were both very agitated. Alexandra Lvovna addressed me and with a stern face said: 'P. I., what have you done? Well, you have stirred up a mess; all our labors are lost; all that I had hoped to devote my whole life to after papa's death has now flown apart into dust.' I understood nothing and looked at her with surprise. Then she, taking one of L. N.'s letters which he had written and which she was to copy and send off, read it to me aloud; here is this letter, addressed to V. G. Chertkov:
>
> '2 August 1910. Yesterday I spoke with Posha, and he very rightly told me that I was at fault in that I had made the testament secretly. I ought to have done this openly, by declaring it to those whom it concerns, or to have left all as it was — and to have done nothing. And he is entirely right, I acted ill and am now paying for it. The ill is in this — that I did it secretly, presupposing evil in the heirs, and did it, chiefly, undoubtedly ill in that I availed myself of an institution of a government denied by me, by drawing up the testament in form. Now I clearly see that for all that is now taking place I alone am to blame. One ought to have left everything as it was, and to have done nothing. And it is hardly likely that the spreading abroad of my writings will redeem that distrust toward them which the inconsistency in my acts should call forth.
>
> It is easier for me to know that the ill comes to me only from myself. But I think that, for the present, the best thing is, after all, to undertake nothing. Though it is hard.
>
> Here is what I wrote down for myself today, 2 August in the morning, and communicate to you, dear Vl. Gr., knowing that all that is important for me is important for you.
> L. T.'
>
> At the same time L. N. was writing to Anna Konstantinovna Chertkova:
>
> 'I write to you, and not to Dima, because to him I should have to say too much and I shall not now be able to. I hope that our faithful friend Goldenweiser shall convey to him my feelings and thoughts…
>
> Let what I have written to him not trouble nor grieve him. In the present hard conditions I, more than ever, feel the wisdom and beneficence of non-doing, and undertake nothing and shall undertake nothing, not only in deed, but in word. I speak and listen as little as possible, and feel how good this is. I kiss you both, my friends, and beg you not to let your love for me diminish. It is to me very dear, very necessary…
> L. T.'
>
> Having heard out this letter, I hastened to explain to Alexandra Lvovna the degree of my participation in this matter and expressed my satisfaction in that I had served as an involuntary occasion for such a clear definition by Lev Nikolaevich himself of this act, and that I was sure that all that L. N. should now do with a clear consciousness would be useful to people. But Alexandra Lvovna and Varvara Mikhailovna, not listening to me, continued to be agitated, and I went out.
>
> After some time, half an hour or an hour, I do not remember, I came again to Alex. Lvovna. She was already in a softer mood, and I could talk with her. I told her that I should like to talk with her from the heart, to explain to her the motives of my act in greater detail, especially since I am personally in no way interested in this, stand to one side, and that if I had now intervened in it, the motive for this had been, in the first place, the personal question of conscience, the desire to disclose to Lev Nikolaevich my attitude toward his act, and, in the second place, my conviction that out of such a manner of testament-making much evil and embitterment will come, and all this will fall upon L. N.'s head and memory. Alexandra Lvovna proposed to me to walk along the avenue leading to the cottage, in order, alone, the two of us, calmly to discuss this question. She was then in that gracious mood which makes her beautiful.
>
> When we were walking with her along the avenue, she told me that after the first letter to Chertkov he had written again, I do not remember whether in the form of a letter to Chertkov, or simply in the form of a will-wish expressed by him, or had expressed in words, that the testament should be made in the following manner: 'I shall call my whole family and shall express to them my will in the presence of witnesses not belonging to my family, and in order not to call forth superfluous irritation, I shall invite not Chertkov, but someone else, for instance Sukhotin and Biryukov, and to them shall I commit the fulfillment of this will of mine.'
>
> On hearing this, I said to Alexandra Lvovna: 'For me the will of Lev Nikolaevich is sacred, and if it cannot be otherwise, I submit to it and shall fulfil all that he bids me, but let this cup pass from me. There shall be found unkind people who shall interpret this thus: "out of envy that it had not gone to him, he upset the matter and so arranged it that L. N. appointed him executor." And it will be very hard for me.'
>
> But this was only Lev Nikolaevich's wish, which it was not given to be realised.
>
> On the day after the talk with me Lev Nikolaevich recorded in his diary:
>
> 'Very, very greatly understood my error. One ought to have gathered all the heirs and declared one's intention, and not secretly. I have written this to Chertkov, he was much grieved.'
>
> At L. N.'s request Chertkov wrote L. N. a detailed history of the testament. In this note V. G. reminds L. N. of the whole history of the testament, the attempts of the family to appropriate to themselves the rights of publication, speaks of the difficulties which L. N.'s friends would experience if the family had appropriated to themselves the publication, since they, the friends, would in that case be deprived of the matter dear to them — the dissemination of his works in a form accessible to the masses, and, finally, convinces him that my objections were founded upon my ignorance of the position of affairs.
>
> For me personally this note is little convincing; but I had firmly resolved to avoid any kind of polemics, and therefore I leave it without objection. As for Lev Nikolaevich, this note again convinced him of the necessity of the testament. He records on reading it through in his diary:
>
> '11 August… A long letter from Ch., describing all that has gone before. It was very sad. Hard to read and to recall. He is wholly right, and I feel myself guilty before him. Posha was not right. I shall write to both the one and the other.'
>
> And in a letter to V. G. Chertkov on the next day he writes, among other things:
>
> 'I write on little sheets, because I write in the wood, on a walk. And since yesterday evening and since this morning I have been thinking of your letter of yesterday. Two chief feelings this letter called forth in me: aversion to those manifestations of coarse self-interest and unfeelingness which I either did not see, or saw and forgot; and grief and repentance that I had given you pain by my letter, in which I expressed regret for what had been done. The conclusion, however, which I drew from the letter is this — that Pavel Ivanovich was not right, and that I too was not right in agreeing with him, and that I wholly approve of your activity, but with my own activity am still dissatisfied: I feel that one could have acted better, although I do not know how. But now I do not repent of what I have done — that is, of having written the testament that has been written, and can only be grateful to you for the part you have taken in this matter.
>
> Today I shall tell all to Tanya, and this will be very pleasant to me.
> Lev Tolstoy.'
>
> 12 August 1910."

L. N. then wrote nothing to me. I, however, was living far off, in service in the Kostroma provincial zemstvo, and did not try further to intervene in this matter.

But for Lev Nikolaevich it caused much suffering.

By his diary one sees the vicissitudes of these sufferings:

On 24 September he records:

> "At breakfast there began a talk about *Children's Wisdom*, which Ch., the collector, had collected. Where will he put the manuscripts after my death? I rather hotly asked to be left in peace. It seemed, nothing. But after dinner reproaches began that I had shouted at her, that I should have pitied her. I was silent. She went off to her room… It turns out, she had been asleep and came out calm…"

On 12 October again the same question:

> "Again from the morning a talk and a scene. Something somebody said to her about some testament of mine of the diaries to Chertkov. I was silent."

On 14 October: "It turns out, she has found and carried off my little diary… She knows about some testament to someone about something — evidently, concerning my works. What a torment because of their monetary value — she fears that I shall hinder her edition. And she fears everything, unhappy woman."

On 16 October: "Very, very hard. God help. I said that I shall give no promises, and do not give any, but shall do all that I can not to grieve her."

Thus, Lev Nikolaevich had to keep silence so as not to lie in answer to the directly put question whether there was a testament. It is hard, of course, to condemn L. N., but it is painful for him when one sees that by this conspiracy his straightforwardness was violated. To the question of the testament he answers evasively: "I do not give and shall not give any promises." S. A. directly accused him of lying, telling me afterward that, when the testament had already been written, he denied it.

One can easily picture to oneself the spiritual torment lived through by Lev Nikolaevich during this time, and we remain in that conviction that the drawing up by him, in secret from the family, of a juridical testament did not bring him peace.
