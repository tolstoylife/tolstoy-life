## Part II. 1902–1905. The 75th Birthday. War. The Beginning of the Revolution.

Having arrived at Yasnaya Polyana, L. N. records in his diary:

> "Three days since I came from Gaspra. The journey was physically hard. I was recovering, but yesterday there was fever and weakness again. I do not take offence. I am preparing, or rather, trying to live out the last days and hours the better. I have been correcting *To the Working People* all the while. It is beginning to take shape, and, it seems, I have finished it."

At this time I had begun to write the biography of L. N. And my correspondence with him during this period often points to our dealings on this account. I have described them in detail in the preface to Volume I. Here I give in full a letter from L. N. to me, corresponding to this period and containing several interesting thoughts.

> "Dear friend Posha!
>
> I have not written to you in a hundred years, and this greatly grieves me: it is as though our bond with you were lengthening or sinking out of sight. And this pains me, because you are one of the first and best of friends, who have given me much joy and support. So let us not allow our bond to be broken. Not on any account. I fear that I raised your hopes in vain with the promise to write my reminiscences. I tried to think about it and saw what a fearful difficulty it is to avoid the Charybdis of self-praise (by keeping silent about everything bad) and the Scylla of cynical frankness about all the vileness of one's life. To write down all one's nastiness, stupidity, depravity, baseness, quite truthfully — more truthfully even than Rousseau — that would be a seductive book or article. People would say: here is a man whom many set high, and look what a scoundrel he was; so for us, then, simple folk, it is surely permitted by God.
>
> Seriously, when I began to recall my whole life thoroughly and saw the stupidity (precisely the stupidity) and the vileness of it, I thought: what of other people, then, if I, praised by many, am such a stupid reptile? And yet, this is explained still further by the fact that I am only cleverer than others. I am saying all this to you not for fineness of style, but quite sincerely. I have lived through all this. One thing I can say, that my illness helped me much. Much foolishness fell away when I set myself in earnest before the face of God, or of all that of which I am a changing part. I saw much that was wretched in myself, which I had not seen before. And it became a little easier. In general one ought to say to those one loves, not 'I wish you to be well,' but 'I wish you to be ill.'
>
> You live, thank God, well, as I hear, with your dear Pasha and the children. Change nothing, but only improve, and do not grow weary. They say only that you have too many superfluous people about you. That is hard. One must struggle lovingly.
>
> I write that I fear a false promise of reminiscences. I do fear, but this does not mean that I refuse. I will try, when there is more strength and time.
>
> I have a plan for avoiding the difficulties of which I spoke, by merely hinting at the good and bad periods. Farewell, I kiss you."

L. N.'s diary of this time discloses to us his spiritual mood.

> "8 August 1902, Y. P., at night. A very hard day — my liver aches, and I cannot conquer evil feelings. I wish ill. I am writing to X. M. and am ashamed of it all. The priest's pamphlet — it pains me. Why do they hate me? I must write to them lovingly. Help me. Mashenka, Liza are here, the Glebovs were here. A letter from Seryozha."

Evidently, in the family there were again being raised those questions of property which always so poisoned for L. N. his stay at Yasnaya Polyana, dear to him for its memories. But here he is carried away by artistic work to such a degree that he abandons his diary and returns to it only in September, writing thus:

> "20 September 1902, Y. P. I have not written for a month and a half. All the while I have been writing *Hadji Murat*. My health is mending. With my spiritual state I may be content. There are no unkind feelings toward anyone. I have thought much. There is much to write down.
>
> 23 September 1902, Yasn. Pol. I have been correcting *Hadji Murat* all the while. This morning I wrote a great deal 'to the clergy.' I thought well. What is written in No. 15 is not good. I wrote a letter about this to Khalileyev, which I shall copy out here. This morning I began to think over an article on the misunderstanding of Christianity and of religiousness, which is to precede the article 'to the clergy.' 'The chief cause of the evil or calamity of our time.' Such ought to be the title. I thought about this."

In the same diary L. N. records also a remarkable thought about the strength of Christianity:

> "They say that Christianity is a teaching of weakness. A fine teaching of weakness, whose founder perished a martyr upon the cross, not betraying himself, and which numbers millions of martyrs, the only men who looked evil boldly in the eye and rose up against it. Both the Jews who executed Christ, and the present-day champions of the state, know what kind of teaching of weakness this is, and fear it alone more than all the revolutionaries. By instinct they see that this is a teaching that undermines at the root, and surely destroys, all that order on which they rest. To reproach Christianity with weakness is just the same as, in war, to reproach with weakness that army which does not go at the enemy with its fists, but, under the enemy's fire, without answering it, builds batteries and sets upon them the cannon that will surely shatter the enemy."

L. N.'s new young friend, Khrisanf Nikolaevich Abrikosov, who visited him at Yasnaya Polyana, relates:

> "L. N. is now occupied with the working-over of his artistic tale *Hadji Murat*, is carried away by this work and speaks of it with pleasure. He told us how today he had read through the whole tale, all that had been written by him earlier and all that he has written now. 'I led it on and on and got tangled up,' he said, 'and I do not know which is better: what was written earlier or what I have written now.' Earlier the tale had been written as a kind of autobiography, narrated by Hadji Murat himself. Now, however, it is written objectively. Both the one and the other version have their advantages, and L. N. began to speak in detail about the advantages of the one and the other version.
>
> We came out of the orchard, and before us there opened a glade, beautifully lit by the slanting rays of the sun. 'How fine! How beautiful! To feel this beauty especially, one had to be ill,' broke from L. N.
>
> The talk turned to the article just finished, *To the Working People*. One felt that L. N. was not fully satisfied with this article of his. The language is not simple, not clear. For the people it must be translated into Russian.
>
> Having rounded the park, we approached the stone pillars and went along the avenue. The road runs rising slightly uphill, it was hard for L. N. to walk, and he asked us to push him slightly in the back. Of course, the talk could not avoid the attempt on the life of Governor Obolensky, and then we began to speak about the *Thoughts on Education*. L. N. expressed the wish that this pamphlet should be printed in Russia, since there is nothing uncensorable in it, and was sorry that there had not gone into it his last letter on education to S. N. Tolstaya. 'How can we speak of brotherhood, when in a moment we shall come to dine, and a footman will serve us,' said L. N. apropos of this letter.
>
> Of late L. N. has received several letters to the effect that there is nothing to say of Christianity and nothing to await from it. Christianity has existed now for 2,000 years, and has not only brought no benefit at all, but, on the contrary, has done harm, in that it has hindered supermen from being formed. Something of this kind is said also by Zola in the *Revue Blanche* apropos of L. N.'s thoughts on the sexual question.
>
> L. N. feels the necessity of answering all these letters and of writing in defence of Christianity.
>
> 'They will say to me: but you are speaking of some Christianity of your own, professed by some ten men, whereas we are speaking of the Christianity professed by millions of people. And I shall answer: if one is to speak of Christianity, then it is all the same how many men profess it, but one must speak objectively of true Christianity.'
>
> In the evening, after dinner, L. N. was enraptured by the letter on revolution from the *Stundist* Ivanov, which he had cited in his article *To the Working People*, and said that now he could not picture to himself any other revolution than only in the form of the refusal of soldiers to shoot."

Toward the autumn it fell to L. N.'s family to decide the important question of where L. N. should spend the winter, so as to protect his health as far as possible. S. A. wrote to me on this account:

> "...Doctors came from Moscow, and there was a careful examination of the whole organism of Lev Nikolaevich, which they found in a satisfactory condition, and they decided unanimously to leave him to live at Yasnaya Polyana, at which all of us, it seems, are glad. I personally was dreadfully unwilling to go anywhere: I saw enough last year in the Crimea of how hard it is, in certain years, to adapt one's organism to new conditions of life. L. N. has mended very much in these two months at Yasnaya Polyana, and *'le mieux est l'ennemi du bien,'* as the French proverb says.
>
> Only his digestion is all bad, and this is irremediable under any conditions of life, as the doctors say. With Lev Nikolaevich there now lives permanently a physician, and a very good one has chanced to come our way. He will stay with us until February, and then — as God grants."

The admirers of L. N., who follow the outward development of his literary activity, recalled that in September 1902 there fell the 50th anniversary of his first tale, *The History of My Childhood*, which had appeared in 1852 in the September number of *The Contemporary*. We have related this event in detail in the first volume of the biography.

This jubilee did not pass unnoticed, and in many journals there appeared articles devoted to L. N. We give here extracts from the most substantial.

> "Contemporaries reveled for many years," says the author of an article in *Obrazovanie*, "in the highly artistic enjoyment afforded by Tolstoy's works; all-destroying time seemed to have lost its power over the inexhaustible spring of the giant-writer's creation, over the active might of his extraordinary genius; the greatest writers of the world gazed with amazement upon the dazzling phenomenon and then stood aside, having unanimously indicated the literary throne as the only place worthy of Tolstoy among the artists of the word... One writer alone was dissatisfied with the author of *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina*: that was Lev Tolstoy... The 'accursed questions' all but drove Tolstoy to suicide at the very time when everything seemed to have combined to bestow upon this man that which men call happiness, in the name of which they struggle, suffer, commit grievous crimes... A picture astounding in its profoundest content, worthy of the brush of the greatest artist! But Tolstoy came out victorious from this trial too, renewed himself and showed himself to the world from yet a new side. And however anyone may regard Tolstoy's preaching, hardly will there be found many people who would dare to suspect one of the fundamental properties of this preaching: its sincerity, issuing from the profoundest depths of the preacher's soul."

In the journal *Mir Bozhy* for September we find such thoughts, confirming from another side that same characterization of the "timelessness," that is, the eternity, of L. N.'s creations.

> "In the course of half a century we behold a truly extraordinary phenomenon, having no example in world literature — the constant and unchanging growth of a writer over whom time seems to have lost its influence. Even after fifty years Tolstoy, already an old man, is as fresh and mighty a writer as he was at the beginning of his work. There widens only the scope of his genius, which, never halting, continues its search for truth and unchangingly moves forward. One image alone involuntarily suggests itself for comparison — the great old man Goethe, at his decline finishing his great work, over which he labored all his life, and with youthful vivacity taking an interest in the movement of scientific thought. But from the excessive Olympian calm of Goethe there blows upon us a chill, as from the summit of a gigantic mountain covered with eternal snow amid unattainable clouds. Tolstoy, yielding nothing to Goethe in the vitality of his creation and the untiring vigor of his spirit, is nearer to us, the poor and little children of the earth, with whom he stands in constant communion, tormented by the common doubts and the thirst for truth.
>
> From his first entrance upon the literary field there does not abandon him that 'holy discontent' with himself, which is then felt so vividly in each new work, all the while growing stronger, until it bursts into a whole storm at the moment of the turning-point at the beginning of the 80s. Discontent with himself and the search for truth give an extraordinary wholeness to Tolstoy's creation and to his gigantic personality, which as it were fills with itself half a century of the life of Russian thought. In his astonishing personality there is, indeed, something symbolic. Everything, it would seem, had combined in his life to give him the happiness and satisfaction possible to a man. A mighty talent, a powerful organism, personal happiness with full material security, the general veneration before the genius-artist who knew no rivals — and at the same time the ceaselessly gnawing worm of doubt and discontent, granting not a minute's peace. No one in the nineteenth century expressed with greater force that restless thirst for eternal truth which torments mankind from the first day of its conscious existence.
>
> And his significance now is, beyond dispute, immeasurably higher in comparison with the former time. Then he was the property only of a small circle of the intelligentsia; now he is, undoubtedly, a writer of the people, whose name is as popular among the masses as among the intelligentsia. To speak of the circulation of his works is impossible, since there are no figures for this, but one thing is beyond dispute: that the total sum of his works circulated among the masses many times exceeds the total figure of the works of all Russian literature. *Resurrection* alone went out in nearly a million copies, and his little stories circulate in the hundreds of thousands. This wide circulation of Tolstoy makes his significance as a writer of the people incapable of any comparison or reckoning. Before us is a literary phenomenon which overshadows all the rest by the enormity of its social significance."

Notwithstanding this glorification of Tolstoy in Russia and abroad, L. N.'s views met with resistance not only in the reactionary policy of the Russian government, but also in such an enlightened country as Germany; we borrow a description of the trial of Lev Nikolaevich in Saxony from one Russian émigré journal of the day:

> "Tolstoy was tried, and not in Russia, but in Germany; he was tried for his reply to the Synod, which had offended the religious feelings of a certain *Justizrat* Pelizaeus. Pelizaeus had read the German translation of this famous reply, published by the Leipzig firm of Diederichs, and called the attention of the Saxon procuracy to the fact that the translation and publication of such a work was a crime against article 166 of the German penal code, which punishes 'with imprisonment of up to 3 years for the public reviling of the institutions and rites' of existing Christian churches enjoying 'corporate rights' in Germany. The Saxon assessor Witthorn drew up a lengthy bill of indictment, directed, in essence, against the greatest of our contemporaries, L. N. Tolstoy; but formally there were called to account the publisher Diederichs and the translator Loewenfeld.
>
> The trial took place on 9 July; the direct defendants, of course, retreated into the background, and the case turned chiefly upon the personality of Lev Nikolaevich.
>
> To the honor of the second judicial chamber of the city of Leipzig, L. N. Tolstoy, in the persons of the translator and the publisher, was acquitted in the most decisive fashion, the court costs were taken upon the public account, and the confiscated copies of the *Reply to the Synod* were released with the right of free distribution in Saxony. The procurator remained dissatisfied and lodged an appeal against the verdict, but at the demand of the general procuracy it was withdrawn. Enough, they said, of disgracing ourselves! The verdict of acquittal came into force, and now there have already been published the 'motives' that prompted the court not to agree with the procurator's accusation."

We borrow from them a few curious passages.

> "Born into the Greco-Catholic church, which calls itself Orthodox, he (Tolstoy), simultaneously with the beginning of his inner work in the moral-philosophical sphere, became a diligent investigator of its teaching and a strict fulfiller of its precepts; this study of the dogmas of his church and the strict fulfilment of its precepts led him, after a strong inner struggle, to the renunciation of 'the Orthodox faith which established the universe' (the words of the resolution of the Most Holy Synod).
>
> This conviction he courageously professed in all his religious-philosophical writings of the latest period; in this he did not stop at a simple negation of the teaching embodied in the dogmas of the Greek church. He created for himself his own, very simplified and, as one must admit, profound Christian faith. The fundamental key to which it is tuned is undoubtedly the same as that which sounds in the essence of the teaching of Christ Himself: love and the fulfilment of the will of God."

Having skilfully conveyed, by extracts from Tolstoy's writings, the essence of his faith, the "motives" mention the resolution of the Synod and its "directions" in case of the death of the "false teacher."

> "It is understandable," the "motives" continue, "that this curse against so profound and serious a man as Tolstoy, whose strivings, even if they be considered erroneous and utopian, are directed precisely at the restoration of the purity of the teaching of Christ, could not but touch him."

This autumn the spectators of Yasnaya Polyana were alarmed by an occurrence which, happily, had no evil consequences. S. A. records in her diary:

> "On the night of 10 to 11 September there was a fire in our attic. Four beams burned, and had I not noticed this fire, by some happy chance having looked into the attic, the house would have burned down, and, above all, the ceiling might have collapsed onto the head of L. N., who sleeps in just that room over which the fire was burning in the attic."

And then S. A. adds:

> "L. N. was well all the while, rode much on horseback, wrote *Hadji Murat*, which he finished, and began to write *An Appeal to the Clergy*. Yesterday he said: 'How hard it is, one must denounce, and yet I do not wish to do anything unkind, so as not to call forth evil feelings.'"

At last L. N.'s health begins really to recover, and he writes of this with joy to his eldest daughter, T. L. Sukhotina:

> "All is well with us. I am mending, I bear the frost — today it is 2 degrees — lightly. I walk with pleasure. And I write, I have finished *To the Clergy* and, it seems, have finished a fantasy, or legend — I do not know what to call it — about the descent of Christ into hell and the restoration of the kingdom of the devil. You know, I think, what it is about. It is, as it were, an illustration to the appeal to the clergy. It seems to me that this may be of use. But perhaps I am mistaken."

This same autumn there took place at Yasnaya Polyana an interesting meeting of L. N. with Peter Verigin, the leader of the Doukhobor community. He had long languished in exile and had now been released in order to emigrate to Canada and join his fellow believers.

L. N. reports of this meeting to his daughter thus:

> "Verigin, by a strange play of fate the leader of the most religious society of people, is himself a man not yet religiously born, although a very intelligent and moral and, above all, a calm man. Yesterday I received from Mavor in Canada detailed information about the religious upsurge of one third of the Doukhobors. The Canadian government is horrified, but acts gently and solicitously. P. Verigin, I think, will help toward the cessation of the movement and the returning home. Today there is news of the slaughter of workers in Rostov. The situation in Russia, looked at not only from the side, but for us as well, appears ever more and more strained."

Khrisanf Nikolaevich Abrikosov, who was present at this meeting, relates of it in a letter to his friend:

> "Interesting was the talk of Verigin with L. N. Among other things, Verigin expressed the opinion that it is equally bad to kill a man or an animal, and equally bad and abominable to feed upon the flesh of an animal as upon human flesh. But L. N. said that he did not agree with this, and that in all acts and in life one must know the order of succession, what to do earlier and what later. Thus, toward a man and his sufferings one feels more pity than toward the sufferings of a horse, and toward the sufferings of a horse more pity than toward the sufferings of a rat or a mouse, while toward a gnat one feels no pity. Now, the instinct for this order of succession is the true wisdom. One cannot pity a gnat and at the same time treat a man cruelly. 'I do not condemn the Doukhobors for their acts,' said L. N., 'but I fear that they did not observe the order of succession. There were still many Christian acts they could and ought to have done before they did what they did.'"

Another opinion we cite from a letter of M. A. Schmidt; her naive but kindly judgment adds several interesting touches:

> "We were lately visited by P. V. Verigin, a very dear and warm-hearted man; he was with me twice, but L. N. said of him: 'he is very good and can strongly influence people, but is a man not yet born in spirit,' and I quite agree with L. N. P. V. believes that man, thousands of years ago, descended from a worm, protoplasm, and so forth. It grieved me to hear such scientific nonsense precisely from him. He has been in England and has now gone to Canada. In the summer he wanted to come to me, because it is near to L. N. He is only 35 years old, quite young and simply a mighty hero, and yet what self-command he has — one simply envies it — and not a drop of malice, and the absence of irritation over a 15-year exile; he tells of everything with marvelous good nature."

A more detailed characterization of P. V. Verigin may be found in my book about the Doukhobors. There too one may read about that radical, inconsistent — as L. N. called it — upsurge among one part of the Doukhobors, which was the subject of the talk between L. N. and Verigin. This movement did not subside at once even after Verigin's arrival in Canada, and to this day traces of it are still noticeable.

The internal situation of Russia was becoming ever more complex and gloomy, and L. N., who listened keenly to the voice of the people, often, in letters to friends, pointed to the signs of an approaching catastrophe. Thus, in a letter to his eldest daughter he writes, among other things:

> "If those incurably ill with consumption, with cancer, knew their condition and what awaits them, they could not live — so too our government, if it understood the significance of all that is now taking place in Russia, they — the men of government — could not live. And therefore they do well to be occupied with balls, reviews, the receptions of Loubet, and the like."

In December of this year L. N. fell ill again. We borrow from a letter of Abrikosov to his friend Škarvan a brief sketch of this time.

> "At Yasnaya Polyana I spent two days and nights; there was no one besides me of outsiders. Of L. N.'s sons only Serg. Lvovich. L. N. lies upstairs, and the doors of his room are all open right up to the hall, so that one could hear in the hall how he coughed. In the evening he summoned me to him. When I went in, he was sitting on the bed, not even propped up with pillows. He had grown thin, and his voice is weak. After a few questions about the Chertkovs, about Marya Alex., and about my kin, he said to me: 'How good it is that you nursed Arkhangelsky, and do not come to nurse me, because there are without that plenty of people to nurse me.' And then he began to relate that he has now conceived the idea of writing his autobiography, or rather, not an autobiography, but simply the most significant moments in his life; he wishes it for Posha. That night he kept recalling his childhood and dictated to S. A. about 20 points from his reminiscences. And there and then he began to relate to me how his father scattered gold pieces 'out of this very chiffonier,' he pointed, and how they, the children, picked them up, how they could not find one gold piece, and the father said that if they found it, they might take it for themselves. They found it and did not know what to do with it. They thought and thought and decided to buy Pelageya Ilyinishna a censer. Here L. N. could not contain himself and began to laugh and weep at this simple recollection of childhood. He explained what kind of censer it was, and simply broke down and shook all over with laughter. I was even frightened for him.
>
> Afterwards I read aloud to him Kropotkin's autobiography, and when I had finished, I sat at a distance and tried not to talk with him, so as not to tire him. Sometimes he called me over and asked me to straighten his pillows or blanket. Once, when I came up to him, he said: 'I feel unwell,' and I asked, 'and spiritually, are you well?' He answered: 'spiritually I am very well, I have come spiritually to such a state that there is nowhere further to go, my whole spiritual state expresses itself in me thus: "Father mine, into Thy hands I commend my spirit. My Father, into Thy hands I commend my spirit,"' he repeated again. I withdrew, and again there was stillness and the half-darkness of the lamp. Suddenly he calls me over and says: 'How many brothers have you?' — I answered. — 'Tell me about them.' — I told him. Of the youngest he said that he is now passing through the most difficult age, that is, from 13 to 15 years. By 15 or 16 the more spiritual interests begin to engage one. 'And that is a nasty age,' he said, and began to recall these years of his own. 'How important the moral environment is in these years, and how immoral it was when I was growing up,' said L. N."

On the other side, about this time Marya Lvovna wrote to me, and her letter beautifully supplements Abrikosov's account. Here is the essential part of this letter:

> "Papa is still lying down, and his illness has taken on some chronic form. After the influenza there has now remained in him a very great weakness, and at times, owing to the pains in his liver, such a great weakness of the heart that he has to be supported constantly with stimulants, even with injections of camphor and all sorts of cardiac medicines. To leave the old man in such a state is, of course, quite unthinkable, and to expect a speedy recovery is likewise impossible. It is a month now that he has been lying down, and no improvement at all. For instance, today all night and just now he has a fever; from what, owing to what organ — it is incomprehensible. The doctor who lives with us thinks that now the whole matter is in the liver, in a catarrh of the bile ducts and small stones. But even in this there is little that is good. So much for the physical. Now for the moral: when he has no pains, he is very calm, gentle, sometimes even jests; mentally he is fresh all the while — he says that it is good to be ill, that he wishes it for everyone; but when the liver ceases to secrete bile and the heart grows confused and weak — then there comes upon him a purely physical anguish, gloom, thoughts of death, that is, a conviction that this is already she, and then he is all silent, and to see this is somehow hard; one feels that what he is going through is too serious to share with those around, and it somehow grows eerie. But his gentleness and goodness do not fail him, and his great patience. Sometimes at such a time one silently caresses him, kisses his hand, or simply he feels my tenderness — and he always responds to it and always repays beyond deserts. So that to go away yet I shall probably not be able for some time, and sometimes I think — whether I shall be able to at all."

On 13 December there appeared in *Russkie Vedomosti* the following declaration by Lev Nikolaevich:

> "Dear Sir, Mr. Editor.
>
> By my years and by the illnesses I have undergone, which have left their traces, I, obviously, cannot be fully well, and, naturally, there will recur worsenings of my condition. I think that, although detailed information about these worsenings may be of interest to some, and that in two most opposite senses, the printing of this information is disagreeable to me. And therefore I would ask the editorial offices of the newspapers not to print information about my illnesses.
> Lev Tolstoy."

Yasnaya Polyana, 9 December 1902.

When, on the day of the Nativity, Lev Nik. was unwell, he said half in jest to Marya Lvovna: "the angel of death came for me, but God recalled him to other affairs. Now he has got free and has come for me again."

But this time too the angel of death was recalled to other affairs, and L. N. met the new year, 1903, calmly and joyfully.
