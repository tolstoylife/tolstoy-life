# Volume IV, Chapter 9. 1905. The Revolutionary Movement. *The Circle of Reading*

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-09.md`](../ru/chapter-09.md).

In the previous chapter we described the life of Lev Nikolaevich in 1904. In order to relate ourselves rightly to his activity in 1905, we must go back and briefly summarize those events of the social life of Russia which prepared the menacing and bloody phenomena of 1905.

In August 1904 a manifesto was proclaimed on the occasion of the birth of the heir. This manifesto granted amnesty to many political exiles and emigrants. I too was brought under this manifesto, and I received permission to return to Russia. The time of Svyatopolk-Mirsky and of the politics of trust had come. The intelligent circles that guide public opinion grew agitated and were filled with the expectation of new great events. The Russo-Japanese War was drawing to its end. At the end of December, by the old style, I set out for Russia and went straight, without stopping in Moscow, turning off toward Tula, and arrived at Yasnaya Polyana. It is difficult to describe the joy and the agitation that seized me at this meeting with Lev Nikolaevich after a separation of nearly eight years. I lived at Yasnaya Polyana for about two weeks. I give here my brief description of this meeting, which I placed in the form of an article in the then-new free newspaper *Nasha Zhizn* (Our Life).

At Yasnaya Polyana

Cut off from Russia by circumstances beyond my control, for eight years I had not seen Lev Nikolaevich. It was with a certain dread that I travelled to him, knowing of the illnesses he had endured in this time; I expected to see a decrepit, bent old man, and, to my great joy, I found him vigorous, healthy, cheerful, full of vital energy — not that energy which requires for its satisfaction a great deal of bustle, but that which expresses itself in the unceasing labour of thought, in a constant responsiveness to all the serious phenomena of life.

This higher, spiritual, vital energy, expressing itself in tireless activity, serves him at the same time as a criterion for appraising the phenomena of life, out of which he, like a magnet picking iron filings from a heap of sand, picks out that which attracts to itself his spiritual personality, developed in the highest degree.

Avoiding fussy talk about the everyday phenomena of life, he does not read the newspapers.

"The reading of newspapers," says L. N., "is the smoking of tobacco, darkening and stupefying the consciousness, cluttering the brain."

But in the hall of his hospitable house a circle of people gathers; around someone or other who has just arrived a lively exchange of thoughts strikes up; one hears the cries of "Port Arthur," "the zemstvo men," "the constitution," "the land question," and so forth. And L. N. comes up unnoticed, sits down beside the talkers, and they fall silent, the better to hear his weighty word, and he enters into the argument, grows agitated, jumps up, and with the words "again I have got a lungful of other people's cigarettes" quickly goes off into his study, in order once more to give himself up to his meditations, to concentrate upon the fundamental questions of human life.

One of the occasions that compelled him "to light up other people's cigarettes" was the placing in the newspapers of his telegram — the answer to a question of the Philadelphia press — and the various commentaries upon it. He smiled indulgently when there was shown to him a sympathetic citation from the *Moscow Gazette*, showing by this smile that "comments are superfluous." He expressed the liveliest joy on learning of the article in *Nasha Zhizn*, in which it was explained that L. N., being on principle an enemy of all violence, cannot acknowledge any state power whatever to be good; but that when he himself appeals to the power, he demands of it only freedom.

And in all the people close to Lev Nikolaevich this article evoked complete satisfaction.

The thoughts of Lev Nikolaevich, working in his solitude, are directed, as it seems to us, chiefly along three paths: in the first place, he is occupied with an ever greater and greater clarifying to himself of his world-view and with the illumination of the events of life from the point of view of this world-view; besides this, he is constantly concerned to give people at the present moment the best possible spiritual food; thus, only recently he finished a great work — *The Circle of Reading*, a collection of specimens of the philosophical and imaginative literature of all times and peoples; finally, often, perhaps sometimes even against his wish, his thoughts pour themselves out into artistic images, and he sketches them down on paper.

Many begun works lie on his literary workbench... but let us not peep in there, that is a secret.

It is quite natural that Lev Nikolaevich, standing at the root of life, cannot take an active part in the contemporary social movement. In this whirlwind movement, formed by many forces, there rises much turbidity from the bottom, darkening the clear vision.

One must wait until the whirlwind subsides, the turbid matter settles to the bottom, and then in the transparent stillness we shall hear the voice of the prophet.

There is one enormous phenomenon, accomplishing itself in stillness and silence, toward which Lev Nikolaevich is never indifferent — that is the life of the working, preeminently the agricultural, people.

A sincere love for the people was always one of the chief nerves of the life of Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy.

And of the good of the people he always expresses himself very definitely. The spiritual good of the people is conscious religion, and all that fosters the development of it leads it to this spiritual good. The material good of the people is the land. And all that leads to the solution of the once again ripened land question leads to the material good of the people.

The land question was often, and from various sides, discussed by Lev Nikolaevich, and as an illustration of this discussion he recently received information about the rumours going about among the people, to the effect that forty years ago the buying-out of souls had begun. This buying-out had been finished, and now one must await a manifesto about the buying-out of the land...

Made wise by the unceasing labour of thought, by life experience, and possessing a constant store of artistic images, Lev Nikolaevich often speaks in parables. Thus, having once come out of his study into the dining room, where his family and some of the guests were sitting, he said approximately this:

— A few days ago I set down in my diary: in order to build a building, one needs, chiefly, three elements: working energy, that is, the striving toward creation; suitable material; and the cement that binds it together. So too for the building of the edifice of human life. The striving toward creation exists, it is laid into people in the form of social instincts. The material is we ourselves, and in order to be suitable material, hewn stone, self-perfection is necessary. The binding cement is the religious consciousness of the necessity of union.

Struck by the clearness of this comparison, I wished to complete it still further and attempted to object:

— It seems to me, — I said, — that yet a fourth element is needed — that is, the plan of the future building.

After thinking a little, Lev Nikolaevich said: "yes, it is needed, but this plan is unknown to us." And with these words he returned to himself.

Such are the questions, full of life, that agitate the dwellers of the Yasnaya Polyana house. And these questions, and their solutions, without disturbing the quiet, family peace that reigns in this house, are borne by the numerous visitors of Yasnaya Polyana throughout all the world.

One of the events that called forth great disputes and agitations was the news of the surrender of Port Arthur. We have already seen in its place, from the diary entry, that this tidings grieved him and that in this grief he caught himself out in a remnant of the feeling of patriotism.

I was present at a conversation of L. N. with his son-in-law Obolensky on this question. Lev Nikolaevich said: "In our time it would have been considered a disgrace, and would have seemed impossible — to surrender a fortress while having supplies and a forty-thousand-strong army." Obolensky objected to him from the point of view that this surrender preserved many lives. L. N. said that he was speaking not from this point of view, but from the point of view of human dignity in a thing once begun. There ought not to have been the beginning of a war, there ought not to be any army at all. But he who has taken upon himself these duties and this responsibility must be honest and reasonable and carry the matter through to the end.

I too was surprised by this note of military honour that spoke up in L. N. But I must add that nothing more was said than what I have here cited. I convey this from memory after fifteen years; perhaps not quite exactly, but the sense was that.

I bring up this conversation in order to mention how it was exploited by L. N.'s sister-in-law, T. A. Kuzminskaya, who was present at this conversation. Ten years later, already during the world war, there appeared in the Paris newspaper *Le Figaro* an article, "Tolstoy and War," in which it was argued that Tolstoy was a patriot and approved of war. In doing so the article cited several patriotic phrases from the *Sevastopol Sketches*, which, as is known, were spoiled by the censorship, which inserted certain patriotic expressions, and then his opinion about the surrender of Port Arthur, of course in an exaggeratedly patriotic colouring.

While living at Yasnaya Polyana, I greatly supplemented my biographical material for the first volume, since Lev Nikolaevich gave me the diaries of his youth to read and permitted me to make extracts from them. Of course, I also made use of oral conversation with him for the elucidation of the obscure places of his early life.

I met the new year, 1905, at Yasnaya Polyana. I managed, on the first of January, to take a photograph of L. N. in his study.

The deepest thoughts were expressed by L. N. in simple private conversation. Thus, in a conversation with Marya Lvovna on the fifth of January he said:

— Just as in our consciousness a change took place slowly and imperceptibly, and you from a child became a woman, and I — an old man, so too in the people the consciousness changes; and when a change ought to take place in the people, it throws out all sorts of follies: Manchuria, decadence... Now we are gradually coming to the consciousness that the state is not needed, that it is an institution that has outlived its day. For the guidance of the common life there is needed not violence, but religious consciousness. And in the measure that religious consciousness develops, the state will melt away.

Thus my personal intercourse with L. N. was re-established and was no longer interrupted up to his very death.

In the first days of January I left for Moscow and from there for Petersburg, where I arrived on the sixth of January. At the station I learned the strange tidings that, during the salute on the occasion of the immersion in the water of the cross at the palace Jordan, one shot had been fired with a charged shell, which flew over the head of the tsar and his suite and wounded a policeman standing on the embankment by the Winter Palace. This shot was ascribed to carelessness, but it turned out to be the beginning of the bloody events. It fell to my lot to be a witness also of the Gapon slaughter of the ninth of January.

Having obtained a foreign passport, I again went to Moscow and Yasnaya, in order, having taken leave of L. N., to return to Switzerland, having resolved at the first opportunity to move with my family once more to live in Russia. At Yasnaya, of course, there was agitation too, and to L. N. there had come rumours, letters, and personal testimonies about all that had been taking place in Petersburg.

There is no need to speak of how indignant L. N. was at everything that was being done in Petersburg in the name of some good or other, so badly understood on both sides.

But all the same L. N. did not overvalue this event. He knew that at this time there was taking place another, still more frightful slaughter, the Russo-Japanese War. I found Lev Nikolaevich at the writing of an article in which he spoke more at length of what he had said briefly in that telegram to the American newspapers which we cited in the preceding chapter.

L. N. tries to show in this article that the political, liberal-revolutionary agitation, which was making itself felt at this time in Russia, is not a popular movement and therefore does not deserve the respect that is rendered to it.

L. N. considers that revolutionary movement to be true which sets as its foundation the freeing of the land and non-submission to any government whatever.

At the end of the article he cited the letter of a woman unknown to him about the Petersburg events, full of deep indignation on account of all that had been taking place there, the author of the letter trying to penetrate into the cause of these frightful phenomena and speaking thus:

> "I cannot determine what is the most frightful thing here; it seems it is that they do not understand and that they have ordinary faces, in spite of the fact that within an hour there will be killed people, and everywhere blood on the stones. It seems the most frightful thing is to feel that between people there is no bond of any kind, it seems — this is the most terrible thing. From the very same village, only some are in a grey greatcoat and others in a black overcoat, and there is no way you can understand why the grey ones joke about the frost and peaceably glance at the black people passing by them, when they not only know that each of them has cartridges enough for ten shots, but know also that within an hour or two all these cartridges will be spent. And the black people look at them as though it must indeed be so. About this thing that sunders people one reads in books, one talks, and does not feel how frightful it is; but when all this is around you, and, as in these days, for a time everything else has ceased to exist, and there is only this one thing: grey greatcoats, black overcoats, and elegant fur coats, and all of them are occupied with one thing, but all in different ways, though none of them knows why some shoot, others fall, others look on."

And L. N. ends the article with these words: "Yes, the whole matter is that there is something which sunders people, and that there is no bond between people. The whole matter is to remove that which sunders people, and to put in its place that which unites them. And it is every external, violent form of government that sunders people; that which unites them is one thing alone — the relation to God and the striving toward Him, because God is one for all and the relation of all people to God is one and the same. Whether or not people wish to acknowledge this, before all of us stands one and the same ideal of the highest perfection, and only the striving toward it destroys the sundering and brings us nearer to one another."

To Lev Nikolaevich there flowed to Yasnaya friends and correspondents, Russian and foreign, wishing to hear from him a weighty, authoritative word about the events that were taking place.

Thus, on the nineteenth of January, two Irishmen, Devitt and McConna, came to him; they presented themselves to Lev Nikolaevich as representatives of ten American newspapers. Both of them related themselves sympathetically to Lev Nikolaevich and attentively wrote down all that he said to them. On returning to Tula, they sent off to the American newspapers a telegram each, of which each cost six hundred roubles.

After them came Williams, by origin a New Zealander, the correspondent of the English newspaper *The Manchester Guardian*. This remarkable man knew twenty-four languages, ancient and modern, eastern and western, and spoke with Lev Nikolaevich in Russian.

And life went on and developed among the people in a way of its own. And L. N. sets down in his diary the manifestations of this new life, which reach him from various ends of the world, as to a single spiritual centre; here is what he writes in January of this year:

> "This morning there came, through Lederle, a letter from two sailors who had refused service: they are at Kronstadt, in prison. I want to write to them at once, and to their commander. I looked in the calendar for the commander's name — did not find it. Thought better of writing. In the morning there was, from Nakashidze, the dear man Kipiani, who related marvels about what is being done in the Caucasus, in Guria, Imeretia, Mingrelia, Kakhetia. The people have resolved to be free of the government and to organize themselves. Dušan wrote it down. This great matter will have to be set forth."

This movement in Guria expressed itself in the following facts: oppressed by the landowners, who took from them two-thirds of the harvest, the peasants refused to rent the landowners' lands, so that these lands lay empty for about two years. Besides this, the Gurians resolved to lead a moral mode of life — not to steal, not to rob, and not to lie. In case of the arising of any disputes and misunderstandings — not to turn to the authorities, but to decide matters among themselves. To decide all communal affairs by general assemblies (at these as many as 5,000 people gathered). To bring up the children in an abhorrence of killing. To take care for their education. To pay the taxes punctually. To lead a simple life according to one's conscience. At first there had been added to this "and according to Christ," but in order not to close the path of joining this movement to the Mohammedans, of whom there are many among the Gurians, they did away with this addition "according to Christ" and left only the formula "according to conscience."

It was just of this that Kipiani, who had come to Lev Nikolaevich, sent by L. N.'s friend Ilya Petrovich Nakashidze, told him. Lev Nikolaevich answered him with the following letter:

> "...The information that Kipiani has communicated is, in my opinion, of enormous importance, and it is absolutely necessary to acquaint people with that event of enormous importance which is taking place in Guria.
>
> Although I know that the Gurians have no notion of my existence, I nevertheless very much wish to convey to them the expression of those feelings and thoughts which their wonderful activity calls forth in me. If you can, and find it convenient, convey to them that here is such an old man, who for twenty years has thought and written of nothing but this — that all human woes come from this, that people wait for help and the ordering of their life from others, from the authorities, and when they see that from the authorities there is for them no help and no order, then they begin to condemn the rulers, to struggle against them. Whereas there is need of neither the one nor the other: neither to wait for help and order from the authorities, nor to be angry with them and make war on them. But there is need of one thing: the very thing they do, the Gurians, namely: to order their life so as not to have need of the authorities; one must do again the same thing they do: live according to conscience, according to Christ — in short — according to God. If it is possible, then convey to them what great joy this old man experienced when he learned that the very thing of which he had thought and written for so many years, and which the learned and those who consider themselves wise do not and did not understand — that this very thing thousands of people decided for themselves, by their own mind and their own conscience, and not only decided, but also carried into deed and conduct this matter so firmly and so well that the neighbouring people join them.
>
> Tell them that this matter is so important and so good that one must use all one's powers (spiritual powers: meekness, judiciousness, patience) in order to carry it through to the end, in order to be an example to people near and far and to serve the establishment of the Kingdom of God, not by force and deceit, but by reason and love.
>
> Tell them that not I alone, but many and many people rejoice over them, are ready in every way, if possible and needful, to serve them, and that we are all convinced that, having begun such a great matter and having already done so much for it, they will not abandon it and will lead it on just the same, showing an example to people.
>
> Tell them that the old man, this man, thinks that their chief powers ought to be directed toward this — that, as they themselves say, they live according to Christ, according to conscience, fulfilling one and the same law both for Christians and for Mohammedans and for all the people of the world. This law consists in this: to love every man and to do to another that which you wish to be done to you. If they will live thus, according to God, then no one will do anything to them. If they will be with God, God will be with them, and no one will be able to hinder them."

The political conversations, very widespread at this time, wearied L. N., and he sets down in his diary this characteristic thought:

> "I listened to political reasonings, disputes, condemnations, and went out into another room, where, with a guitar, they were singing and laughing. And I clearly felt the holiness of merriment. Merriment, joy — this is one of the fulfilments of the will of God."

At this time new thoughts of a philosophical direction arise in Lev Nikolaevich, and he sketched them down in his diary:

> It is set down: "I do that which is." This means that life — not only my whole life from birth to death, that is, I, such as I have become through my whole life, but the life of the whole world, all this *is*, only it is not visible to me by reason of my limitedness; it is revealed to me in the measure of my movement in life. It is, and I do it; in this is life."

This same thought, more developed, is set down by him several days later:

> "All that moves presents itself to me as moving; but in essence that already is, and always was and will be, toward which, in the direction of which, anything is moving. My whole life from birth to death, in spite of the fact that I may find myself at the beginning or in the middle of it, already is; and that which will be, just as undoubtedly is as that which has been. Likewise there is also all that which will be with human society, with the planet Earth, with the solar system; only I cannot see the whole, because I am separated from the All. I see only that which is revealed to me in the measure of my powers; I live and, passing from one state into another, see (so to speak) the inwardness of life. And besides this, the chief thing — I have the joy of the creation of life. That all that constitutes my life already is, and that at the same time I create this life — does not contain in itself a contradiction. All this *is* for the highest reason, but for me this is not, and I have a great joy — to create life within limits out of which I cannot pass. If one admits God (which is wholly necessary for reasonings in this domain), then God creates life through us, that is, through separated parts of His essence."

This thought of the existence in the present of all that unfolds in time occupied not Lev Nikolaevich alone; in Western Europe, in this direction, there were already at work Einstein, Lenormand, and others.

The continuing military operations agitated Lev Nikolaevich, and with strained attention he followed the struggle of these two worlds. And in his diary we find interesting thoughts on this occasion:

> "Yesterday there came the news of the rout of the Russian fleet. This news for some reason struck me especially strongly. It became clear to me that this could not and cannot be otherwise. Bad Christians though we be, it is impossible to conceal the incompatibility of the Christian profession with war. In recent times (I mean about thirty years ago) this contradiction has come to be more and more recognized. And therefore in a war with a non-Christian people, for whom the highest ideal is the fatherland and the heroism of war, Christian peoples must be conquered. If up to now Christian peoples have conquered uncultured peoples, this came about only from the advantages of the technical, military improvements of the Christian peoples (China, India, the African peoples, the Khivans and the Central Asians); but with equal technique the Christian peoples must inevitably be conquered by the non-Christian ones, as happened in the war of Russia with Japan. Japan in a few decades not only equalled the European and Asiatic peoples, but surpassed them in technical improvements. This success of the Japanese in the technique not only of war, but of all the material improvements, clearly showed how cheap these technical improvements are — that which is called culture. To adopt them and even to think out further ones — costs nothing. What is dear, important, and difficult is the good life, purity, brotherhood, love — the very thing that Christianity teaches and that we have neglected. This is a lesson to us. I do not say this in order to console myself for the fact that the Japanese have conquered us. The shame and disgrace remain the same. But only they lie not in our having been beaten by the Japanese, but in our having undertaken to do a thing which we do not know how to do well and which in itself is bad."

This question occupied L. N. strongly; he returns to it several times and conceives the idea of writing an article under the title *The Tower of Siloam*, applying to the contemporary events the well-known parable of Christ about the Tower of Siloam, at the falling of which many people perished. This parable ends with the words: "unless ye repent, ye shall all likewise perish."

And so, alluding to this parable, he set down in his diary:

> "18 June. (For *The Tower of Siloam*.) This is the rout not of the Russian army and fleet, not of the Russian state, but the rout of the whole false-Christian civilization. I feel, am conscious of, and understand this with the greatest clearness. How good it would be to be able to express this clearly and forcefully.
>
> This rout began long ago: in the struggle of the success of so-called scientific and artistic activity, in which the Jews, the non-Christians, beat all the Christians in all the states and called forth toward themselves universal envy and hatred. Now this same thing has been done in the military business, in the business of crude force, by the Japanese, having shown in the most evident manner that toward which Christians ought not to strive, that in which they will never succeed, in which they will always be conquered by the non-Christians: in idle knowledge, in that which is called science, in pleasure-giving amusements, *pflichtloser Genuss*, and in the means of violence. History accomplishes the instruction of Christians by a negative path: it shows them what they ought not to do, on what they ought not to direct their powers."

And again, on one of the following days, he sets down thus:

> "(For *The Tower of Siloam*.) A change of the state order can take place only when a new central power becomes established, or when people in places shape themselves into such unions in which the governmental power will be unnecessary. And outside these two situations there may be revolts, but in no way a change of the order."

And so, having condemned Japan as a state, L. N. enters into living intercourse with the Japanese, who greet him as a teacher of life. One of them, the editor of a socialist journal, wrote L. N. a sympathetic letter and received this answer:

> "Dear friend Iso-Abe. The receipt of your letter and of the newspaper with the English note gave me the greatest joy. I thank you from my soul for them. Although I have never doubted that in Japan there are not a few reasonable, moral, and religious people who feel an abhorrence of the present war, I was nevertheless very glad to receive the confirmation of this opinion. It gives me the greatest pleasure that in Japan I have comrades and fellow workers with whom I can enter into friendly intercourse."

Further, L. N. points out to the Japanese that the socialist teaching, which is spreading in Japan, does not satisfy him, and he recommends the religious-moral foundation of life, which determines the perfecting of each separate personality, of which all humanity is composed.

The denial of power from the moral point of view, as is known, constituted an essential part of L. N.'s world-view. And he joyfully greets those authors who try to ground this denial on new — for example, historical — arguments. Such joy was given him by the pamphlet of Khomyakov, the son of the well-known Slavophile writer. In this pamphlet there was expressed with especial vividness the thought which he comments upon in his diary:

> "30 March. How right the Slavophiles are in saying that the Russian people avoids power, withdraws from it. It is ready to surrender it sooner to bad people than to soil itself with it. I think that, if this is so, it is right. Anything is better than being compelled to use violence. The situation of a man under the power of a tyrant is far more conducive to the moral life than the situation of an elector, a participant in power. This consciousness is proper not only to the Slavs, but to all people. I think that the possibility of despotism is founded on this. I think too that to their participation in the government one must ascribe the immorality, the indifference of the Europeans and the Americans in constitutional states."

In this same direction L. N. answers one of his correspondents of that time.

In March L. N. received an interesting letter from a peasant who was serving as a lackey in Petersburg. From the first lines of L. N.'s answering letter it is evident what the peasant had asked him, and therefore we cite here the beginning of this letter. L. N. wrote thus:

> "You ask: how much longer will the many-millioned grey homespun coats drag the overturned cart? You write: the twentieth century is going on and a grievous time has come, the blood and sweat of the destitute, exhausted Russian people is flowing. There will be no fathers, brothers, husbands, but there will be a multitude of cripples, and the overturned cart stands on one spot.
>
> You write: how much longer must we drag it and sing 'Dubinushka': ah, it goes, of itself it will go, oo-oo.
>
> You ask my advice, how the long-suffering and long-enduring homespun coats are to drag the overturned cart to the appointed place, and how the people is to be delivered from useless labours."

L. N. points out to the author of the letter that he has answered these questions in a whole series of articles: *The Sole Means*, *Is It Really So Necessary?*, *Where Is the Way Out?*, *To the Working People*, *Bethink Yourselves!*

And again he sets forth to him the essence of his answer to the questions put. The fundamental answer of L. N. consists in this — that one ought not to think about setting the cart to rights (the process of external reforms), but to try to unharness oneself from it, not to draw it. And for this there is one means — to live according to God.

And Lev Nikolaevich sets forth the five commandments of the Sermon on the Mount: "be not angry, do not commit adultery, do not swear, do not avenge, and do not make war."

> "If only people would remember," he continues his reasonings, "the chief law of Christ: do unto others as you wish that they should do unto you, and all would be well.
>
> People complain that life is bad for them because of the rich and the authorities, that they ruin and kill them. But who is it that bids them live badly?
>
> Just as the louse and every kind of vermin attacks an ailing body, so all manner of rich men and authorities breed upon the bad life of the working people. Live well — and all this vermin will of itself disappear."

And he sets up as an example the sectarians who refuse to obey the authorities, points to the Gurian movement.

And concludes thus:

> "And therefore my advice is — not to think about the cart; let those who have need of it overturn it and draw it; but as for you, with all your strength, let each one strive for his own good life, himself to live as it is said in the Gospel. And if people will live according to the Gospel, their life too will be good."

At the end of the letter L. N. adds that, in view of the fact that many turn to him with similar questions, he is sending this letter to the press.

The Russian revolutionary movement of that time also gives him occasion to express a whole series of original thoughts:

> "Tocqueville says," sets down L. N., "that the great revolution took place precisely in France, and not in another place, precisely because everywhere the situation of the people was worse, more downtrodden, than in France: 'en détruisant en partie les institutions du moyen âge, on avait rendu cent fois plus odieux ce qui en restait' (*).
>
> (* In destroying in part the medieval institutions, they brought it about that what remained of them became a hundred times worse. *)
>
> This is true. And for the same reason the new, following revolution of the freeing of the land must take place in Russia, since everywhere the situation of the people in relation to the land is worse than in Russia."

As the French were called in 1790 to renew the world, so to the same are the Russians called in 1905."

> "30 July. The intelligentsia has brought into the life of the people a hundred times more evil than good.
>
> The Russian revolution must destroy the existing order, but not by violence — by passive disobedience.
>
> The misunderstanding of the workers of the present Russian revolution is that they wish to institute for the Russian people a new form of government; whereas the Russian people has come to the consciousness that it needs none whatever."

> "19 Sept. All revolutions are only the visible manifestations (leaps, ascents up the steps) of the realization of the highest law, one for all."

> "23 Oct. The revolution is in full swing. They kill on both sides. There has come forward a new, unexpected element, absent in the former European revolutions, of the 'Black Hundred,' the 'patriots'; in essence, people crudely, incorrectly, contradictorily representing the people, its demand not to use violence.
>
> The contradiction is in this, as always, that people wish by violence to put a stop to violence, to curb it. In general the levity of the people who are making this revolution is astonishing and revolting: childishness without childish innocence. To myself and to all I say that the chief business now of every man is to watch over himself, to relate himself strictly to each act, not to take part in the struggle. And this is possible only to a man who relates himself religiously to his life. Only from the religious point of view is it possible to be free from participation in, even from sympathy with, the one or the other side and to further the one thing — the pacification of both.
>
> Now, during the revolution, three sorts of people, with their qualities and defects, have clearly outlined themselves: 1) the conservatives, people who desire tranquillity and the continuation of the life agreeable to them and who do not wish for any changes. The defect of these people is egoism; their quality — modesty, humility. The second — the revolutionaries — wish for change and take upon themselves the audacity of deciding what change is needed, and who do not fear violence for the bringing of their intentions into execution, as well as their own deprivations and sufferings. The defect of these people is audacity and cruelty; their quality — energy and readiness to suffer for the attainment of the goal which presents itself to them as a good. The third — the liberals — have neither the humility of the conservatives nor the readiness for sacrifice of the revolutionaries, but have the egoism, the desire for tranquillity of the first and the self-assurance of the second."

Of course, such a view of the revolutionary movement was possible in a man who does not acknowledge the state, and consequently the fatherland either. And so, continuing the thread of his thoughts, L. N. sets down thus:

> "And what use, spiritual or bodily, is there to anyone from the fact that there exist Russia, Britain, France... The greatest material calamities: taxes, wars, slavery; in the spiritual respect: pride, vainglory, cruelty, disunion, and solidarity with violence.
>
> There have been many cruel and ruinous superstitions: both human sacrifices, and the Inquisition, and the stakes, but there has been none more cruel and ruinous than the superstition of the fatherland — the state. There is the bond of one language, of one set of customs, as for example the bond of Russians with Russians, wherever they may be, in America, Turkey, Galicia, and of the Anglo-Saxons with the Anglo-Saxons in America, in England, Australia; and there is the bond uniting people who live on a common land: the rural commune, or even an assembly of communes, governed by freely established rules of the inhabitants; but neither the one nor the other bond has anything in common with the violent bond of the state, which demands at the birth of a man his obedience to the laws of the state.
>
> In this is the frightful superstition. The superstition is in this — that people are assured, and people assure themselves, that a union artificially composed and held together by violence is a necessary condition of the existence of people, whereas this union is only violence, advantageous to those who carry it out."

But here too there turn up in the diary thoughts of a purely personal character, no less interesting:

> "6 June. A multitude of people, all elegant, eating, drinking, demanding. The servants run about, fulfil. And to me it is ever more and more tormenting, harder and harder, to take part and not to condemn.
>
> I am compared with Rousseau. I owe much to Rousseau and love him, but there is a great difference. The difference is that Rousseau denies every civilization, whereas I deny the false-Christian one. That which is called civilization is the growth of humanity. Growth is necessary, one cannot say of it whether it is good or bad. It is — in it is life — like the growth of a tree. But the bough, or the forces of life growing in the bough, are not right, are harmful, if they absorb the whole force of growth. So it is with our false civilization."

> "21 September. In me are all the vices, and in the highest degree; both envy, and self-interest, and avarice, and voluptuousness, and vainglory, and ambition, and pride and malice. No, malice there is not, but there is embitterment, mendacity, hypocrisy. All, all is there, and in a far greater degree than in the majority of people. My one salvation is in this — that I know it and struggle, all my life I struggle. Because of this they call me a psychologist.
>
> How good it is that I am at times malicious, and avaricious, and vile, and know this about myself. Only thanks to this can I (to my misfortune, only sometimes) meekly forgive, endure the malice, the stupidity, the vileness of others."

And amid these self-accusations a philosophical thought:

> "You suffer from the fact that people are not religious, do not understand the religious demands, and you are vexed at them — you are grieved. One must understand that the capacity for a religious relation to life (the highest human capacity now) cannot be transmitted by reasoning or by any spiritual influence whatever to people who do not have it. Just as one cannot teach a dog to shut the door, or horses — not to trample the grass, or savage people — to prepare for themselves dwelling and food, so long as their reason is not developed, so one cannot teach people (now) to live understanding the whole significance of their life, that is, to live guided by religious consciousness. Such people are only beginning to be formed, appear one in a thousand, and appear quite independently of the mode of life, of material competency, of education, just as much and even more among the poor and uneducated. Their number gradually increases, and the change of the social order depends only on the increase of their number."

Remarkable at this time is L. N.'s correspondence with the Grand Duke Nikolai Mikhailovich. L. N.'s sincerity was put to the test, and he came out of this test the victor. Here is what he wrote to the Grand Duke:

> "Just before receiving your good letter, dear Nikolai Mikhailovich, I was thinking of you, of my relations with you, and wished to write to you about there being something unnatural in our relations, and whether it would not be better for us to break them off.
>
> You are a Grand Duke, a rich man, a close relative of the sovereign; I am a man who denies and condemns the whole existing order and power and who directly declares this. And there is something for me in my relations with you that is awkward from this contradiction, which we as it were deliberately go round.
>
> I hasten to add that you have always been especially kind to me, and that I can only be grateful to you. But all the same there is something unnatural, and to me in my old age it is especially hard to be other than simple.
>
> And so, allow me to thank you for your goodness to me and at parting to press your hand in friendship."

Nikolai Mikhailovich understood Lev Nikolaevich's difficulty and answered him with a kind letter, in reply to which he received the following from Lev Nikolaevich:

> "I received your letter, dear Nikolai Mikhailovich — 'dear' precisely in the sense that you call forth love toward yourself.
>
> It was very joyful for me to learn from your good letter that you have fully understood me and have retained kind feelings toward me.
>
> I do not forget that *vous avez beau être grand Duc* (*), you are a man, and for me the most important thing of all is to be with all people in kind, loving relations, and it is joyful for me to remain in such with you, even though our intercourse should cease.
>
> (* However much you play the prince. *)
>
> Very, very grateful to you for your kind letter."

The literary work of Lev Nikolaevich in this year went on very intensively. From the entries of his diary one can trace both this work and other small facts of his life.

> "3 April. Was unwell in the heart. Death is ever simpler and simpler, more and more natural. In spite of the ill health, I did something, namely for *The Net of Faith* (and not badly) and selections from *The Net of Faith* and a preface to *The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles*. Worse, no good. And the letter about the overturned cart."

> "4 May. In this time I finished *The Great Sin*. Wrote a story for *Prayer*. It seemed good, and I was moved while writing, but now I almost dislike it."

> "22 November. In this time I have been correcting *The Divine and the Human* and am still dissatisfied. But better. I have begun *Alexander I*. Was distracted by *The Three Untruths*: it did not come off. Health — a uniform extinguishing. Very good. A great event: Tanya has given birth. Masha has arrived with her husband. I very much want to write *Alexander I*. I have been reading *Paul* and *The Decembrists*. I imagine it all very vividly. Every day I ride on horseback. Something else, it seems, ought to be set down, important, but no. I do not know how it will come out. I have skipped these pages and am writing."

> "9 December. In this time I have finished *The Divine and the Human*. I wrote *Liberties and Freedom* as a separate article and now have included it in *The End of the Age* and sent it to Moscow and to England. Probably too late. Let it be in the old way. Yesterday I continued *Alexander I*. I wanted to write *Reminiscences*, but could not manage it. All the strikes and revolts. And I feel more than ever the necessity and the calm of withdrawing into myself. The other day I somehow prayed to God, understanding my position in the world in relation to God, and it was very good. Yes, I forgot: the day before yesterday I wrote *The Green Stick*."

> "17 December. I wrote a little of *Alexander I*. But badly. I tried to write *Reminiscences* — still worse. For two days I wrote nothing at all. Still unwell in the stomach, and was very drowsy mentally and even spiritually. Nothing interests me. Such periods I have not yet grown accustomed to bearing patiently. In Moscow the frightful brutalities continue. There is no news. The trains are not running. Sometimes I think of writing an appropriate appeal 'to the tsar and his helpers' — to the intelligentsia and the people. But there is no strong desire, although I clearly know what to say."

But the chief work, which L. N. especially valued himself, was *The Circle of Reading*, which came out in this year in the first edition of *Posrednik*.

Lev Nikolaevich rejoiced over this book and said that it was very probable that all his writings would in time be forgotten, but that this would remain.

In the course of this summer I lived for about two months with my family in the village at Yasnaya Polyana. Although the illness of my younger son greatly complicated my life, nevertheless, living near L. N., I was able often to make use of his company, and this gave me many joyful, unforgettable minutes.
