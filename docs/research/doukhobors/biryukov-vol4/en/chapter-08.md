# Volume IV, Chapter 8. 1904. The Russo-Japanese War

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-08.md`](../ru/chapter-08.md).

---

In this year L. N.'s diary is uncommonly rich in content. Besides the facts and moods of his personal life, we meet in it a profound and intense work of thought, a penetration to the very end of rational consciousness, and an uncommon clarity and simplicity of the most secret thoughts. One feels that L. N. has entered upon a new stage of consciousness and has established himself in it, in order to go further. We shall give here the most vivid expressions of these thoughts, which illuminate for us the inner life of L. N.

On 2 January L. N. sets down an interesting thought about progress and evolution:

> "The movement which we imagine to ourselves as eternal in the future, in the form of progress, is an obvious illusion, arising from the consciousness of our separateness from the world. Without movement there is no separateness. But in essence we, like God, stand motionless, and it only seems to us that we are breaking through, widening our limits. In this is life. God breathes through us."

The thought here expressed seems to us extraordinarily important. We had been awaiting it, following the development of the idea of eternity in L. N.'s consciousness. Hitherto L. N. had appeared as an evolutionist; he saw movement everywhere. And this evolutionism contradicted his absolute moral principles. Here for the first time he says that movement, progress, is an illusion, since eternity, infinity — outside time and space — cannot contain within itself the idea of movement, of aim, of direction "somewhere"; there can be no concept of speed, because there is no concept of time and space.

Further on we shall see the development of this proposition. On the next day L. N.'s thoughts turn already to the sphere of personal and social life, and just as radically, thought through to the end, he poses the question, foreseeing a social catastrophe.

> "I at first thought that the establishment of a good life among men was possible while retaining those technical contrivances and those forms of life in which humanity now lives, but now I have become convinced that this is impossible, that a good life and the present technical improvements and forms of life are incompatible. Without slaves we shall not only have no theatres, confectioners', carriages, objects of luxury in general, but there will scarcely be any railways or telegraphs at all. And besides, men have now for generations grown so accustomed to artificial life that all the town-dwellers are no longer fit for a just life, do not understand it, do not want it. I remember, Yusha Obolensky, having got into the country during a snowstorm, said that life in the country, where it drifts over with snow so that one has to dig oneself out, is impossible."

On 6 January L. N. records: "I was composing a new calendar." This was the beginning of the *Circle of Reading*, reworked from the *Thoughts of Wise Men*. His denunciations of the existing order do not for a moment darken L. N.'s consciousness of his own shortcomings, and with the same, if not with even greater, sincerity he exposes them. A few days after this he records thus:

> "14 January. I woke today physically healthy, strong, and with an overwhelming consciousness of my own vileness, insignificance, of a life badly lived and being lived. And up to now — the middle of the day — I remain under this beneficent mood. How good, even advantageous, it is to feel oneself, as today, humbled and vile! You demand nothing from anyone, nothing can offend you, you are deserving of everything worst. Only one thing is needful: that this humiliation should not pass into despair, into dejection, should not hinder one from working, from serving, in whatever way one can."

And a few days later again L. N. sketches with bold strokes his thoughts about immortality, and precisely these thoughts are the direct opposite of what is usually ascribed to him.

Thus, he records:

> "25 February. However desirable the immortality of the soul may be, there is not and cannot be any such thing, because there is no soul, there is only the consciousness of the Eternal (of God). Death is the cessation, the change of that aspect (form) of consciousness which expressed itself in my human being. Consciousness ceases — not that which was conscious, which is unchanging, because it is outside time and space. Here it is that faith in God is needed. I believe that I am not only in God, but that I am a manifestation of God and therefore shall not perish.
>
> 29 March. If there is immortality, then it is only in impersonality. The true I is the divine essence, which looks out into the world through the limits bounded by my personality. And therefore the limits can in no way remain, but only that which is within them, the divine essence of the soul. In dying, this essence departs from the personality and remains what it was and is. The divine principle will again manifest itself in a personality, but it will not be that personality. Which? Where? How? That is God's affair."

Besides this, at the beginning of the year L. N. is occupied with a very important and interesting article: a preface to the brief biography of Garrison, the American preacher of non-resistance to evil by violence as far back as the middle of the last century. In the third volume I have already told how this Garrison's son composed his brief biography, and L. N. wrote a preface to it, in which he briefly and vividly expressed the foundations of the teaching of non-resistance.

At the beginning L. N. cites a remarkable quotation from Garrison's writings:

> "The motto of ours," wrote Garrison in the middle of his activity, "from the very beginning of our moral struggle has been: Our country is the world, our countrymen are all mankind. We believe that this will be the motto inscribed even upon our grave. As another of our mottoes we chose: universal emancipation. Hitherto we limited the application of our motto only to those people who are gathered in this country by the southern slaveholders as a market value, as goods, cattle, farm-stock. But from now on we shall make use of our motto in the very widest sense: the emancipation of our whole race from the dominion of man, from the enslavement of self, from the power of brute force, from the enslavement by sin, and — the subjection of men only to the power of God, to the control of their own conscience, and to the governance of the law of love."

Thus Garrison came forward with his spiritual weapon to the struggle with slavery in general, as with a worldwide evil.

> "Garrison," says L. N., "understanding that the slavery of the negroes was only a particular instance of universal violence, set forth a general principle with which it was impossible not to agree — namely, that no man under any pretext whatever has the right to rule, that is, to employ violence over those like himself. Garrison insisted not so much on the right of the slaves to be free as he denied the right of any man whatsoever, or assembly of men, to compel another man to anything by force. For the struggle with slavery he set forth the principle of struggle with all the evil of the world."

But the world did not understand his teaching.

> "The essence of the question," L. N. continues, "remained unresolved, and the same question, only in a new form, stands now before the people of the United States. Then the question was how to free the negroes from the violence of the slaveholders; now the question is how to free the negroes from the violence of all the whites and the whites from the violence of all the blacks."

And L. N.'s preaching, in his words, meets the same — at best condescending — smile that Garrison's preaching also met. He noticed this, as he says, even in regard to the American Bryan, who had visited him, "remarkably intelligent, advanced, and religious," as L. N. expresses himself about him.

The American adduced to him the example known to all of the robber murdering a child, and L. N. once more objects to this customary argument with these words:

> "No one has ever seen the fantastical robber, but the world groaning under violence is before the eyes of all. And yet no one sees, no one wishes to see, that the struggle which can free mankind from violence is not a struggle with a fantastical robber, but with those real robbers who do violence to men. For non-resistance to evil by violence means only this, that the means of interaction of rational beings upon one another must consist not in violence, which can be admitted only in relation to the lower organisms, devoid of reason, but in rational persuasion; and that towards this replacement of violence by rational persuasion all men who wish to serve the good of mankind ought to strive."

Further on Lev Nikolaevich expresses the supposition that men so hardly take in this teaching because they fear to lose their privileged position. And he himself answers this supposition:

> "But there is nothing to fear in the change; the principle of non-resistance is not a principle of violence, but of concord and of love, and therefore cannot be made forcibly obligatory for all men. The principle of non-resistance to evil by violence, consisting in the replacement of brute force by persuasion, can only be freely accepted. And to the degree in which it is freely accepted by men and applied to life, that is, to the degree in which men renounce violence and establish their relations upon rational persuasion — only to that degree is true progress accomplished in the life of mankind."

This thought of the progressive significance of the commandment of non-resistance to evil by violence is here for the first time expressed by L. N. in all its fullness and clarity. Besides this, from this preface there follows yet another important thought. Whoever has attentively studied L. N.'s works, has followed the development of his thought through his diaries and letters, will easily notice that L. N.'s thought, and even his very world-outlook, has undergone a certain evolution. L. N. himself often points to this. But the teaching of non-resistance to evil by violence has remained unshakable. Expressed by him with especial force in 1884 in his work *What Is My Faith?*, it is repeated by him with still greater clarity and depth twenty years later, in 1904. We draw from this the conclusion that this principle has lain at the foundation of that teaching which it is customary to call the teaching of L. N. Tolstoy, and which, of course, represents nothing other than the teaching of Christ in its pure, undistorted form, expounded to us by his disciple with new, living force.

As if laughing with malicious laughter at this teaching, which had again illuminated the world, the devil with a lavish hand poured out the poison of his malice over his unhappy slave — mankind.

In 1904 there arose one of the cruellest of wars, though not a very prolonged one — the war of Japan with Russia. One may understand all the horror and bitterness experienced by L. N. at the arising of this slaughter.

On 28 January he records in his diary:

> "War, and hundreds of reasonings about why it has come, what it signifies, what will come of it, and so forth. All are reasoning people, from the tsar to the lowest army wagoner. And before all of them there stands, besides the reasonings about what will come of the war for the whole world, still the reasoning about how I, I, I am to relate to the war? But no one makes this reasoning. He even considers that he ought not to, that it is unimportant. But seize him by the throat and begin to strangle him, and he will feel that the most important thing of all for him is his life, and that this life is his 'I.' And if this life, his 'I,' is the most important thing of all, then, besides being a journalist, a tsar, an officer, a soldier, he is a man, come into the world for a short term and bound to depart by the will of Him who sent him. What then is more important for him than what he is to do in this world — evidently more important than all the reasonings about whether the war is needed and to what it will lead. And what he is to do in regard to the war is evidently this: not to make war, not to help others to make war, if he cannot indeed restrain them."

The civilized world, which had disgraced itself by permitting this slaughter, knew, of course, what a rebuff it would meet in the views of the great old man. But the press, which traffics in all principles, pretended not to know, and asked L. N. for his opinion.

On 8 February Lev Nikolaevich received a telegram from Philadelphia, from a large American newspaper, with the question: "Was he for the Russians, the Japanese, or for neither?" Tolstoy's answer was the following: "I am neither for Russia nor for Japan, but for the working people of both countries, deceived and forced by the governments to make war against conscience, religion, and their own welfare."

The war with Japan was received by the Russian people and society as a true calamity, and few people could be found who went to the war with eagerness and enthusiasm. On the contrary, in many places in Russia cases of direct resistance were observed. Near Kharkov women lay down on the rails so as not to let through the train which was to carry off their husbands.

The spirit of protest against the war for the first time made itself seriously felt. Of course, no small role in this protest was played by the circulation of L. N. Tolstoy's writings. We have an impartial testimony in this direction. Bishop Innokenty, who lived at Dalny, in his article apropos of the Japanese war directly reproaches the officers with Tolstoyism:

> "Observing," writes Bishop Innokenty, "scenes from the local military life, and very often hearing from the lips of officers Tolstoyan morality concerning the war, one involuntarily has to wonder how an army under such conditions can cope with its great tasks... To wear a military uniform and to be an admirer of the Tolstoyan teaching — this is like a man who, having rigged out a ship and gone out into the open sea, should renounce the purposefulness of his voyage."

Thus the force of L. N.'s influence already in the first days of the war weakened the blow of the enemies who had clashed.

Naturally, many people, sensing the spiritual might of L. N., awaited from him an appraisal of world events. They awaited what he would say apropos of the war of Russia with Japan. Many awaited this, but few had the courage to put this question to Lev Nikolaevich himself. One of the first to venture upon it was the well-known French man of letters and publicist Jules Claretie. He placed in the newspaper *Le Temps* a lengthy open letter to Lev Nikolaevich. The tone of this letter is rather frivolous, betraying in him no great understanding, but the question is put very wittily, with the clarity and precision proper to the French. It is interesting how Jules Claretie reflects in himself the opinion of the French intelligentsia about L. N.

> "You in your way have evangelized the world, you have taught it the morality of compassion and forgiveness, which has not always been acknowledged by the followers of other cults, but which has brought into the hearts of men the true teaching of Christ. And you are truly a Christian, because you apply to life that of which others only speak. You hate hatred. You make war upon war. You dream of brotherhood, of peace, of good among men, who are at last to lead mankind into the promised land, towards which for centuries generation after generation has gone in a long file, strewing its path with bones. In a word, you are one of those prophets who console the unhappy, and when you point us in the sky to a star which you have already descried and we as yet have not, our path seems to us less difficult, the burden of life seems lighter, and we believe in the future."

Continuing further to lavish such epithets, he says at last:

> "It is quite natural that it is precisely of you that we ask what you think — you whose spirit rises above others — what you think about the events now accomplishing themselves, which, unfortunately, now hold men in their possession and overturn all their aspirations."

> "You see, dear and great teacher," Jules Claretie thus ends his article, "man is the plaything of events. The monarch sincerely wishes for peace, and he is made to wage war. The people strives for repose — it is awakened by cannon shots. The great word 'disarmament' has been flung into the world, and armed fleets course over the oceans, and the frontiers bristle with bayonets. Prophet of good, you instruct men in pity, and they answer you by loading their rifles and opening fire! Does not this disturb you, in spite of the firmness of your convictions, and have you not become disillusioned in man the beast? This is just what I should like to hear from you, dear and great teacher!"

And as if in fulfilment of this passionate desire to hear Tolstoy's word, another Frenchman, a contributor to the newspaper *Figaro*, Georges Bourdon, goes to Yasnaya Polyana to ask L. N. his opinion. He holds long conversations with him, the exposition of which made up a whole book; but before this he prints an article in the *Figaro*, where he briefly conveys the substance of his conversation. Lev Nikolaevich was indeed following with strain the military events in the Far East. Georges Bourdon tells thus of his meeting with L. N.

> "He was the first to speak of the war. 'What news?' he asked, and then added: 'How can one not be interested in such a collision! How sad it is to hear of these battles between men!'
>
> I objected to him," says Bourdon, "that in this war there is taking place a struggle of two races, and asked him: what did he think of the consequences of the victory of one or the other race? 'And what concern of mine are the races?' answered Tolstoy. 'I make no distinction whatever between them. I stand for man. What good can come for man out of this war? The trouble is that the war points us to a complete forgetting of human obligations. Above the obligations to the family, to the fatherland, to mankind, there are still obligations to God, if you will permit me to use this word. If it does not please you, then let us say — obligations to the All, with a capital A. This All, which I call God, is not subject to dispute. Whatever I may think, I cannot escape the thought that I belong to some whole, that I make up a part of some general harmony. The consciousness of my relation to this harmony is usually called the religious idea. And men forget these fundamental truths. Not reading the Gospel, that excellent book, they grow torpid in barbarism. And lo, they are drawn into wars, forgetting that the first obligation of a thinking being is to put a stop to killing!'"

After several naïve questions, to which L. N. answered laughing, Bourdon cautiously asked him: "At this minute, when the fate of Russia is being decided, do you, a Russian, whatever you may think about the war, not make now some reservation — I do not speak of your principles, but of their practical application, of their dissemination?"

> "No reservation whatever," answered L. N., "but one must be sincere," he added, smiling. "In the depths of my soul I do not feel myself wholly free from patriotism. As a result of atavism, of upbringing, I feel that, against my will, it still sits within me. I must call reason to my aid, recall the higher obligations, and then without any reservation I set above everything the interests of mankind. Yes, my consciousness tells me that killing, in whatever form it may manifest itself, under whatever pretext it may cloak itself, is always loathsome. That war is a monstrous scourge, and all that prepares it is subject to condemnation."

And Lev Nikolaevich, in an agitated, raised voice, added:

— How can men permit this? Why is the human conscience not outraged? How is it that they do not see all the horror of this bloody tyranny... It is dreadful! If they were to put a knife into your hands and order you to cut the throat of this little girl here, my granddaughter, under the threat of killing you for disobedience — surely you could not do it all the same, because it would be morally impossible for you. If only the Christian consciousness lay at the foundation of the soul of man, it would become just as impossible for him to take a rifle in his hands and go to kill his fellows!

It would seem that L. N.'s answer to the questions put to him was sufficiently clear; but for L. N. himself this was little, and, wishing to speak out with all the force of his word and his spirit, he wrote the article *Bethink Yourselves!*, devoted to the Russo-Japanese war.

L. N. worked long over this article. Already in February there is such an entry in the diary:

> "All this while I am writing about the war. It does not come right yet. My health is not bad. But for some time now my heart is weak. I cannot at all welcome death. There is no fear, but I am full of life and cannot."

The last redaction of the article *Bethink Yourselves!* is signed the 8th of May.

This article *Bethink Yourselves!* consists of two parts; that is, each of its chapters falls (with the exception of the last) into two parts. The first part presents a compendium of the opinions of various thinkers about war. The second part of each chapter presents L. N.'s reasoning on the same theme.

L. N. begins his article with an expression of his indignation at the accomplished fact — the declaration of war:

> "Again war. Again sufferings needless to anyone, called forth by nothing, again falsehood, again the universal stupefaction and brutalization of men.
>
> Men, separated from one another by tens of thousands of versts, hundreds of thousands of such men — on the one side Buddhists, whose law forbids the killing not only of men but of animals, on the other side Christians, professing the law of brotherhood and love — like wild beasts, on land and on sea, seek one another out in order to kill, to torment, to maim in the most cruel manner.
>
> What is this, then? Is it in a dream, or in waking reality? Something is accomplishing itself that ought not to be, that cannot be — one wishes to believe that it is a dream, and to wake.
>
> But no, it is not a dream, but a dreadful reality."

Analyzing the causes of the war, L. N. comes to the conclusion that men have lost their way upon their path to the good.

> "Men of our Christian world and of our time are like a man who, having missed the right road, the further he drives, the more and more he becomes convinced that he is driving not where he should. And the more he doubts the rightness of the way, the more quickly and desperately he drives along it, consoling himself with the thought that he will come out somewhere or other. But a time comes when it becomes completely clear that the road along which he is driving will lead nowhere except to the precipice, which he is already beginning to see before him."

The chief error consists in the denial of religion, that is, of the guiding moral principle.

> "Men deprived of religion," says L. N., "while possessing enormous power over the forces of nature, are like children who should be given gunpowder or detonating gas to play with. Looking at the might which the men of our time make use of, and at how they employ it, one feels that, by the degree of their moral development, men have no right not only to the use of railways, steam, electricity, the telephone, photographs, wireless telegraphs, but even to the simple art of working iron and steel, because all these improvements and arts they employ only for the satisfaction of their lusts, for amusements, for debauchery, and for the extermination of one another."

Especially interesting is the answer to the question which Lev Nikolaevich poses to himself:

> "But how is one to act now, at once," it will be said to me, "with us in Russia, at the minute when the enemies have already fallen upon us, are killing our people, are threatening us; how is the Russian soldier, officer, general, tsar, private person to act? Are we really to leave it to the enemies to ravage our domains, to seize the products of our labours, to take prisoners, to kill our people? What is to be done now, when the matter has been begun?
>
> But surely, before beginning the matter of war, by whomever it may have been begun — every man who has bethought himself must answer — first of all the matter of my life was begun. And the matter of my life has nothing in common with the acknowledgement of the rights to Port Arthur of the Chinese, the Japanese, or the Russians. The matter of my life consists in fulfilling the will of Him who sent me into this life. And this will is known to me. This will is that I should love my neighbour and serve him. Why then, following temporary, accidental demands, unreasonable and cruel, should I depart from the eternal and unchanging law of my whole life, which is known to me?"

> ...To the question of what is to be done now, when the war has been begun, for me, a man understanding his vocation, whatever position I may occupy, there can be no other answer than this: that whatever the circumstances may be — whether the war has been begun or not begun, whether thousands of Japanese or Russians have been killed, whether not only Port Arthur but Petersburg and Moscow have been taken — I cannot act otherwise than as God requires of me, and therefore I, as a man, can neither directly, nor indirectly, neither by orders, nor by help, nor by incitement to it, take part in the war; I cannot, I do not wish to, and I will not."

Not so thought the leaders of this slaughter. And the chief guilt and responsibility lies, of course, not upon those who directly drive men to killing, but upon those who so far pervert the soul of man as to make possible the submission of men to the most absurd demands. And the chief share of the responsibility for the war lies upon those teachers who preach a false faith, perverting the teachings of the great teachers of mankind. In the Russo-Japanese war there was a collision of two religions — the Christian and the Buddhist, equally forbidding killing. We know well how the Christian teachers pervert the commandments of Christ in their catechisms and official sermons and textbooks. And lo, it turns out, exactly the same thing is taking place in Japan, which has already become civilized to such a degree that the servants of its state religion, Buddhism, publish interpretations of the teaching of Buddha, in which it is proved that, although Buddha did indeed teach love towards all beings, yet enemies — the Russians — may be killed.

L. N. had finished his article when he received an interesting letter; he records about this in his diary:

> "8 May. Today I received a letter from a sailor at Port Arthur:
>
> 'Is it pleasing to God or not, that the authorities make us kill?'
>
> This doubt exists, and I write about it, but I know too that there is a great darkness in an enormous number of men. But, as Kant says, as soon as a truth is clearly expressed, it cannot but conquer all. When? — that is another question. We want it soon, but with God a thousand years are as one hour. It seems to me that, in order for wars to come to an end (and with wars the legalized violence), the following historical events are needed: it is needed 1) that England and America should be defeated in wars by states which have introduced universal military conscription; 2) that they in consequence of this should introduce universal military conscription; and 3) that only then will all men come to their senses."

A part of this prophecy has been fulfilled. In the last world war England was temporarily defeated, or at least was threatened with defeat by Germany. And England, and then America too, introduced compulsory military conscription. Of course, this has considerably advanced the cause of peace. All peoples have come to know all the horrors of war. Now it is no longer possible to deceive anyone with patriotism. The revolutionary movement of Russia seems to delay the decision of this question, but perhaps also hastens it, since it exhausts the last justification of war. And we believe that its end is near.

L. N. himself related with great modesty to this work of his. His relation to it is very interestingly expressed by him in a letter to the Grand Duke Nikolai Mikhailovich: he thanks him in the letter for having fulfilled a request for help to the Doukhobors, and then adds:

> "I never thought that this dreadful war would so affect me as it has affected me. I cannot but speak out about it, and I have sent an article abroad, which will appear one of these days and will probably be very much disapproved of in the higher spheres.
>
> In your last letter but one you wrote that perhaps you might some time call in at Yasnaya Polyana. However pleasant it would be to me to see you at our place, I think that I am so unpleasant a person to the government — and shall especially be so now, after my article about the war — that your visit to me might be unpleasant for you, and therefore I consider it necessary to forewarn you of this."

L. N.'s article had a great success and undoubtedly contributed to the enlightenment of mankind.

The reviews of this article, printed in the English newspapers, reached L. N. too, and he records in his diary:

> "Yesterday in *Russkie Vedomosti* a judgement about my article in England. It was very pleasant to me, vainly pleasant, and that is bad."

We give here two of these reviews as the most characteristic:

> "Tolstoy's latest appeal represents one of the most remarkable documents of world history. It is a lengthy and eloquent sermon on the text 'war is murder.' On this theme he preaches with a logical disregard for the most cherished traditions of the world. He lays war bare, tearing from it its ornaments, its pride, its solemnity, and exhibits it in its naked hideousness, to the horror of mankind. 'Courage,' 'patriotism,' 'military glory' — all this for the fearless Russian reformer is but empty words, invented for the support of a system of wholesale slaughter which men call war... For civilized mankind, which has freed itself, or at least partly freed itself, from the savage state, it is shameful that war, with all the cruelties and sufferings bound up with it, is still considered not a calamity to which men are subjected, but a valorous deed, worthy of being extolled."

> "Tolstoy's article is a prophetic word, illuminated with the light of an unearthly origin. It breathes the very spirit of Christ. But however remarkable this article may be from the side of the illumination of the inner conditions of Russian life, our interest concentrates itself in a struggle other than that which now rages between Russia and Japan. Tolstoy embodies the deepest consciousness of contemporary enlightenment. God compels men to choose between His will and that animality which has hitherto reigned among the majority of mankind... There is coming a new dawn in the evolution of a higher mankind... In Tolstoy's words there is a spirit dangerous to all governments. But 'when rulers give themselves over to immoral ambition and kill their brothers, carried away by predatory wars, they ought not to be surprised if the people renounces them.'"

But there were also negative reviews, especially in Russia. Thus, one lady wrote L. N. a letter, reproaching him for the unkindly tone of the article. L. N. humbly answered her:

> "Countess Sofya Dmitrievna, I am very grateful to you for having signed your letter. For I often receive letters of the same kind and, wishing to answer them, cannot do so. One wishes to answer because it is especially painful, at my years, when one stands with one foot in the grave, to know that there are people to whom you wish nothing but good, who hate you. One wishes to justify oneself, to soften them.
>
> You write that I shall not answer this letter, because I answer only those who praise me. This is not quite just; I always read with great interest and attention the letters that condemn me, trying to extract profit from them. And such profit, and very great profit, I have extracted from your letter. You pointed out to me that in my article there is that which ought not to be in Christians — indignation, condemnation. I had felt this even before, but your letter pointed it out to me clearly. It is perfectly just that a man who leans upon Christ ought to try to be, like Him, meek and humble of heart. And I am not at all that. Not in justification of myself, but in penitence of myself, I can say only this, that I am a weak man, far from having attained that ideal towards which I strive. I am to blame that the tone, the spirit of my article is unkindly, but its sense for me is unquestionably true, and I shall repeat the same upon my deathbed. And I am sure of this not because I believe myself, but because I believe Christ and the law of God.
>
> As a circumstance mitigating my guilt there may at least a little serve the fact that, whereas you live in Petersburg amid the solemn preparations and influences of the war, I live amid the unhappy people who, living in extreme need, send off their breadwinners to a slaughter incomprehensible and needless to them, and see only privations, sufferings, and death. But I am afraid of giving myself over again to a bad feeling. Better I will fall silent, since this letter has for its aim not to convince you, but to ask you to forget those unkindly words which you wrote me, and to call forth in yourself feelings towards me that are at least not ill-disposed, if not well-disposed — feelings with which it is proper for all men to relate to one another, and which I experience towards you, especially recalling my meeting with you somewhere in Petersburg, a meeting which left in me the most pleasant memory."

In the month of July L. N. was visited at Yasnaya Polyana by his friend, the peasant M. P. Novikov. Of course, their conversation touched upon the war. And Novikov in his reminiscences adduces interesting and powerful remarks of L. N. about this dreadful affair. When they began to speak of the war, L. N. exclaimed:

— Dreadful, dreadful! And today, and yesterday, I wept over those unhappy men who, having forgotten the wise proverb that a bad peace is better than a good quarrel, perish by tens of thousands from day to day in the name of an idea incomprehensible to them. I do not read the newspapers, knowing that in them the horrors of the killings are described not only not for condemnation, but for evident praise of them... But the household sometimes read to me, and I weep... I cannot but weep...

L. N. showed Novikov a letter he had received and offered him to read it aloud.

In this letter the unknown author described, says Novikov, how well-disposed they had been from the start, from their native town, and how this disposition changed completely as they approached Manchuria. "We rode a day, two, a week, a month," it was said in the letter, "all empty fields and forests. We must have ridden seven thousand, and did not see ten villages. Steppes and steppes. Why, on this land one could yet settle ten more Russias, and even then there would be no fullness, but when we set out across the Chinese land — only mountains and stones. And what the deuce did we need here, what was the blood to be shed for, on account of some mountains and stones? It would have been all very well if we had had no land of our own. So when we saw all this and thought it over, our thoughts went otherwise, and the eagerness was gone."

— What of that? — asked Lev Nikolaevich, when I had finished the reading. — They want to deceive the people, the diplomats assure them that there was no other way at all, but the peasants ride out and decide in their own fashion that there was nothing to fight about.

— Yes, dreadful, dreadful! — continued L. N. — A terrible thing is being accomplished, and no one is conscious of it. The other day on the road a village peasant-woman overtakes me, hurrying to town, three barefoot children with her. I went along together with her, we got to talking. She was going for an allowance, the second payment had come due. "We worried and worried," she says, "we ran and ran, we were at the member's own place three times, hardly waited out the payment." — "Well," I ask, "have you grown used to being without the master? With the payment, I dare say, you'll live well even alone. Before there was more want, I suppose?" And — oh, how the woman burst into sobs, how she dissolved, could not get out a word. "We would," she says, "have given them our last little cow, never mind that we ourselves are in want. What," she says, "do the children need money for? They need their father. They are good and merry only with their father. But now they have become like wet chicks, they never leave their mother's tail. They won't let you take a step, they cling to you everywhere." — "But won't daddy come back, then?" — the little girl asks her in fright, wiping her eyes and looking now at me, now at her mother, and I stand there, weeping, and they all weep. Old fool that I am, I wanted to get into conversation, to console, but it came out that I only led them into sin.

Such was L. N.'s relation to the war of that time. Thanks to him, in this war the question was put point-blank. This was the last "successful" war. The next world war already ended in revolution. The people could not endure this insane and cruel slavery, and rose up.

In this year L. N. lost two persons close to him. On the 1st of April there died his friend of youth and of old age, Countess Alexandra Andreyevna Tolstaya. L. N. records in his diary:

> "Al. Andr. has died. How simple and good this is."

Besides this, all summer L. N.'s brother, Sergei Nikolaevich, lay suffering, dying, ending his days on his estate of Pirogovo.

L. N. drove there several times, visiting the sick man, and always drove away with the heavy feeling that his brother was not submitting to the approaching change, but was struggling and suffering.

Thus, on 15 August he records in his diary:

> "Pirogovo. Three days here. With Seryozha it was very hard. He suffers cruelly, both physically and morally, not submitting. I can do nothing, say nothing good, useful."

At last his strength left him, and L. N. records in his diary:

> "26 August. Pirogovo. Seryozha has died. Quietly, without consciousness, without expressed consciousness, that he was dying. This is a mystery. One cannot say whether it is worse or better. The active religious feeling was inaccessible to him. (Perhaps I am still deceiving myself; it seems that I am not.) But it is well with him too. Something new, better, has opened. The same as with me. The degree of enlightenment is dear, is important; but on what step it stands in the infinite circle is a matter of indifference."

But in L. N. himself life was bubbling up as from a spring, and the intense inner work did not cease. Going back somewhat, we give a little page of his diary, representing the expression of that new stage of consciousness upon which L. N. had entered at this time.

> "30 April. I keep thinking in the same way in the mornings (on waking) about my philosophical delirium. Yesterday and today I thought this:
>
> 1) Is not our constant striving towards the future a sign that life is the expansion of consciousness? Yes, life is the expansion of consciousness.
>
> 2) Movement, all movement in the material world, beginning with the movement of the heart and ending with the movement of Sirius, is only an illusion, proceeding from the expansion of consciousness: more and more I await, learn, live through (*je m'entends*).
>
> 5) In order that there could be an expansion of consciousness (the good), it is needful that it should be limited. And it is limited by space and time.
>
> 4) At first it seems that I am material (I take my limits for myself), then it seems that I am something spiritual, that is, something, as the materialists say, of fine matter, separate. Then one becomes conscious that there is neither the material nor the spiritual, but there is only the passage through the limits of the eternal, the infinite, which is All in itself and nothing (nirvana) in comparison with the personality.
>
> 5) Living by the consciousness of corporeality, man is an egoist, a fighter for his own joys; living with the consciousness of a spiritual being, he is a proud man, a lover of glory; living in the consciousness of his participation in the divinity, he does that which God wishes and which God does; the good of all."

At the beginning of the year L. N. read much of the German philosophers; in his diary in February he records:

> "I read Kant, was delighted; now I am delighted with Lichtenberg. He is very kindred to me."

A similar opinion of Lev Nikolaevich's is adduced with greater detail by the German journalist Ganz, who visited Yasnaya Polyana at this time.

Welcoming the guest, L. N. said:

— At the present time I am under the influence of two Germans. I am reading Kant and Lichtenberg and am enchanted by the clarity and attractiveness of their exposition, and with Lichtenberg also by his wit. I do not understand why the present-day Germans have abandoned both these writers and are carried away by so coquettish a feuilletonist as Nietzsche. For Nietzsche is not a philosopher at all and does not even at all strive to seek and to express the truth... Schopenhauer I consider also a more considerable stylist. Even if one acknowledges in Nietzsche a vivid stylistic brilliance, this too is no more than the knack of a feuilletonist, which does not give him a place beside the great thinkers and teachers of mankind.

But here new literary projects arise in L. N.'s soul, tending towards the expression of that same idea dear to him.

On the 7th of May he records in his diary:

> "It seems to me more and more that there is something to say, and that there is, about the causes of the suppression of the spiritual life of men and about the means of deliverance. It is all the same, the old thing: the cause of everything is violence, violence justified by reason, and the means of deliverance is religion, that is, the consciousness of one's relation to God. The same thing I wish to express in artistic form: Nicholas I and the Decembrists. I am reading much that is good upon this."

The *Decembrists* indeed once more occupy L. N.'s attention.

In the émigré Russian journal *Osvobozhdenie* of that time there appeared the following note by the man of letters Prince Gr. Volkonsky.

> "In *Novoye Vremya* of 3 June 1904 (No. 10148) there was placed a 'Little feuilleton — Something New from the Past of Count L. N. Tolstoy'; this article was signed with the letters W. W., and in it was said:
>
> "It is known that in 1878 Count L. N. Tolstoy conceived the idea of writing the *Decembrists*... With what great enthusiasm Count L. N. Tolstoy related to the work he had conceived, which he was not fated to write (save for fragments). As is known, at least by hearsay, he did not find in the figures of the Decembrists sufficiently characteristic Russian traits, nor indeed sufficient importance in general, for them to be made the centre of a great epic creation.'
>
> I sent this cutting from *Novoye Vremya* to Count Tolstoy with a letter, in which I said: 'You will oblige me if you answer me my question: can this really be true? I suppose that this is simply a vile insinuation of *Novoye Vremya*, which has completely entangled itself in the contemporary questions of Russian life and in the questions of Russian history. It is hard to see how the pressure of the censorship or the desire to please the government deforms Russian thought.' Here are the lines which I received in answer:
>
> 'Thanks to *Novoye Vremya*. Thanks to its inaccurate information I have received word from you.
>
> The Decembrists more than ever occupy me and arouse my wonder and my tender emotion. I read your letter... Very good. What are you doing now?
> Your loving L. Tolstoy.
>
> 1904. 1st of July.
>
> Let Suvorin draw from my communication whatever conclusion he pleases.'"

And for himself L. N. draws strength in the consciousness of his vocation: on the 10th of June he enters in his diary:

> "Remembering that you live only now, in the present, that is, outside time, one can neither grieve, nor still less be angry; one can only rejoice and love. O, help me, Lord, that is, Thou whom I am conscious of, that always — and if not always, then at least as often as possible — I may be conscious of Thee. I apply this to my life now, to my old man's ailments, and the ailments become a good. In old age I have two joys: one — all the joys of this life: communion with the world, with nature, with animals, chiefly with men, the work of thought, active and passive, the reception of others' thoughts; and I have besides the joy of the consciousness of the approaching transition into a new form of life (my ailments)."

Living in Geneva, I was at this time preparing for an appearance at the international philosophical congress. As the theme of my paper I took "The Fundamental Ideas of Tolstoy's World-Outlook." Of course, I did not consider myself entitled to come forward with such a theme without asking the permission of Lev Nikolaevich and without taking counsel with him about it. In answer to my question I received from him a letter, in which he wrote, among other things:

> "I think that you will very well expound my world-outlook, such as it was at the time of my writings.
>
> I say 'at the time,' because in this respect there goes on within me a constant and, especially now, an intensified work, not changing, but clarifying, deepening, grounding my former views. This is *im Werden*, and therefore cannot be expounded."

Afterwards I delivered to L. N. a copy of my paper, and he approved it, but at the congress itself it passed almost unnoticed, although it was printed in the general collection.

The unceasing work of thought led L. N. to original results, in which, by the sincerity proper to him, he went to the very end. Here is an example of such a reasoning in the diary of 7 July:

> "I vividly understood that what results can there be for a man from anything whatever? I do not speak of my personal life — what results can there be in activity amid a world infinite in space and time? To write upon flowing water, to move beads along a cord sewn into a ring? It is all senseless. To satisfy one's passions? Yes, but not to speak of the fact that all this passes, all is insignificant, all this is too little for a man. One wishes to do something real, not to write upon water. Well then — for oneself, for one's passions — it is stupid, but it takes hold of one, just now one wishes it. For the family? for society? for one's own people? for mankind? The further from oneself, the colder; and, strange to say, the worse, the more immoral. For myself I should be ashamed to rob a man, not to speak of killing, but for the family I will rob, for the fatherland I will kill, for mankind there are no longer any limits, everything is permitted.
>
> What then is to be done? Nothing? No, do all that you wish, that is implanted in you, but do it not for the good (there is no good, as there is no evil), but for the sake of this being what God wishes. Do not the good, but the lawful. This alone satisfies. This alone is needful and important and joyful."

At this time V. G. Chertkov, living in England, wrote an article about the revolutionary movement in Russia and sent it to L. N., asking him to write a preface. L. N. read the article, approved it, and set forth his opinion of it in a letter, expressing yet a number of interesting thoughts on the same question. Especially interesting is the new definition of freedom given by L. N. in this letter.

> "To the concept of freedom there is ascribed the property of something positive, whereas freedom is a negative concept. Freedom is the absence of constraint. A man is free only when no one forbids him certain acts under the threat of violence.
>
> And therefore in a society in which the rights of men are in one way or another defined, and certain acts are required and forbidden under pain of punishment, men cannot be free. Truly free men can be only when they are all equally convinced of the uselessness, the unlawfulness of violence, and submit to the established rules not in consequence of violence or the threat of it, but in consequence of rational conviction.
>
> ...And therefore it is evident that a greater and greater freedom of men is attained only by the spreading among men of the consciousness of the unlawfulness, the criminality of violence, of the possibility of replacing it by rational conviction, and by an ever less and less application and use of violence by each separate man.
>
> And therefore it is evident that the struggle for freedom must be transferred into the spiritual sphere.
>
> Spiritual activity is the greatest, the most powerful force. It moves the world. But in order that it should be the force that moves the world, it is needful that men should believe in its might and make use of it alone, not admixing to it the external methods of violence that destroy its force — that they should understand that all the strongholds of violence, even those seemingly the most unshakable, are destroyed not by secret conspiracies, not by parliamentary disputes or newspaper polemics, and still less by riots and murders, but only by each separate man's clarifying for himself the meaning and vocation of his life, and by the firm, compromiseless, fearless fulfilment, under all conditions of life, of the demands of the higher, inner law of life."

Another remarkable letter of that time was written by L. N. to his sick friend Rusanov.

The first part of the letter is devoted to Rusanov's son, who troubles his father by his atheism:

> "You apologize for writing so much about Kolya. This is one of the most interesting themes for me. I have always loved him very much and love him. I like the fact that he is an atheist (that is, something non-existent and incapable of existing). He writes this only because he is not an atheist, that is, he believes in the obligatoriness of truth, in Him, or in That, which obliges. He writes so crudely and lumpishly 'I am an atheist' only because he does not wish, fears untruth, fears to have the appearance of being that which you would wish him to be, the appearance of a man who, out of obligingness even towards the most beloved person, departs from the truth. By this very thing he is good. But that he is in a hurry to say that he is an atheist and fears not God, but the concept of God, and crosses himself off from it — this is a most ordinary phenomenon in our time and a very sad one. You know my opinion that one of the important motives, and of the greatest human activity, is suggestion, hypnosis, and this is very good when this force is employed upon good thoughts and feeling, or upon indifferent acts. Without this capacity men could not live, but it is dreadful, ruinous, when this force is employed upon the evoking of bad feelings, false thoughts, and evil acts, as is always accomplished in state and especially in religious suggestion — that of which I wish to speak: with the concept of God unkindly and erring men have joined so much that is false and evil, that the honest, pure, little-thinking men of our time have worked out in themselves the capacity of a conscious resistance against this suggestion, of the sort whereby I consciously stop myself from yawning when others yawn before me. With the good men (but little-thinking, I repeat) of our time there has happened the same that would happen with travellers who, having several times been lured to a night's lodging and been robbed, and having heard such tales from other travellers, should turn in nowhere for rest, and out of fear of being robbed should not believe those hospitable hosts who should invite them, and, poor men, should keep on wandering until their young legs could carry them. The same too with our poor youth. So that the evil of religious deceivers and suggesters is not only the direct evil which they do to the deceived, but also that which they do to those who refuse to hear and to think about that which alone is needful to men."

In the second part of the letter L. N. initiates his friend into his literary works:

> "I am occupied of late with the composition of, no longer a calendar, but a circle of reading for each day, composed of the best thoughts of our writers. Reading all this time — not to speak of Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Xenophon, Socrates, of the Brahminical, Chinese, Buddhist wisdom — Seneca, Plutarch, Cicero, and the moderns: Montesquieu, Rousseau, Voltaire, Lessing, Kant, Lichtenberg, Schopenhauer, Emerson, Channing, Parker, Ruskin, Amiel, and others (and moreover for two months I read neither newspapers nor journals), I am more and more astonished and horrified at the ignorance and 'cultured' savagery into which our society is plunged. For enlightenment, education, consists in this: to make use of, to assimilate all that spiritual inheritance which our forefathers have left us — yet we know the newspapers, Zola, Maeterlinck, Ibsen, Rozanov, and the like. How I should wish to help, if only in some degree, this dreadful calamity, worse than war, because upon this savagery, the most dreadful 'cultured' and therefore self-satisfied savagery, there grow up all the horrors, war among them."

A similar thought he expressed also in a letter to me about the same time.

> "I am well, I am working very joyfully over the calendar, which is growing into a circle of daily reading. What a wealth of wisdom and of infectious good is scattered through the books of all peoples and times — and is ignored by us, preoccupied with the readings of the Suvorins, Russian and foreign, and of all sorts of *quasi*-artists and thinkers."

When in August L. N. drove to Pirogovo to his dying brother, he set down in his diary that with which he occupied himself there:

> "The first day I translated, yesterday I did nothing, today I unexpectedly found the beginning of an article about religion and wrote a chapter and a half. Suddenly it became clear in my head, and I understood that my ill-health was already preparing itself, and that was the cause of the dullness. The title must be given — 'One Cause of Everything, or The Light Has Become Darkness, or Without God.'"

Further on, that same day, he records this thought: "Men either invent for themselves marks of greatness: tsars, generals, poets — but all this is a lie. Everyone sees through it that there is nothing there, and the king is naked. But the wise men, the prophets? — yes, they seem to us more useful than other men, but all the same they are not only not great, but not a hair's breadth greater than other men. All their wisdom, sanctity, prophecy — is nothing in comparison with perfect wisdom, sanctity. And they are no greater than others. There is no greatness for men, there is only fulfilment, a greater or lesser fulfilment and non-fulfilment of what is due. And this is good. So much the better. Seek not greatness, but what is due."

Remarkable are the thoughts about revolution expressed by L. N. in his diary of 20 August: "I am reading the history of the French Revolution; it becomes unquestionably clear that the foundations of the revolution (upon which Taine so unjustly attacks) are unquestionably true and ought to be proclaimed, and that, as he says, the imaginary man, that is, the ideal of man, is far more real than the Frenchman of a given time and place, and that to be guided by this imaginary man for the ordering of life is far more practical than to be guided by considerations about the qualities of such-and-such a Frenchman. The error was only in this, that the proclaimed principles were proposed to be realized in the same way as the former abuses: by violence. *L'assemblée constituante* would have been entirely right if it had proclaimed those very same principles, namely: that no one may possess another, may possess land, no one may collect taxes, no one may execute, deprive of freedom; if it had declared that henceforth no one, that is, the government, will support these rights, and nothing more. What would have come of this — I do not know; and no one knows what would come even now, if this were declared; but one thing is unquestionable, that there could not have come of it that which came of the French Revolution. Private men will never beat, slaughter, and rob one-thousandth of the number that governments will beat and rob, that is, men who acknowledge in themselves the right to kill and to rob. Perhaps French society was not ready then for such a revolution; perhaps it is not ready even now; but it is unquestionable that this revolution ought to be accomplished, that mankind more and more prepares itself for this revolution, and that a time will come when mankind will be ready for it."

And finally a quite contemporary thought:

> "The great French revolution proclaimed unquestionable truths, but they all became a lie when they began to be introduced by violence."

Further on in the diary there occurs a thought which already betokens a certain stage of consciousness to which his old age has raised him.

Already in 1903 L. N. recorded once that he was ceasing to distinguish one daughter from another, and that they merge for him into one common type of young woman or girl.

In 1904 he records this thought already much more in detail and more definitely.

> "In old age, as in dreams, faces, places, times merge into one: brothers — into sons, friends — into one another; not the faces are remembered, but my relation to them. If the relation is one, then the faces merge. The same with places and times. In death all will merge into one. What will this one be?"

On the 1st of September there is again an entry about the *Circle of Reading*:

> "All this time I have been translating and reading for the *Circle of Reading* and have written a preface. The work is advancing, but there is very much of it."

The governmental oppression was making itself ever more strongly felt, while at the same time there was already being born in Russia a new social movement, known under the name of the "zemstvo agitation."

The American newspapers, having learned of this, wished to know Tolstoy's opinion, and now Lev Nikolaevich receives from Philadelphia the following telegram:

> "Tula. To Lev Tolstoy.
>
> Should greatly appreciate a detailed answer of a hundred words or more, explaining the significance, aim, and probable consequences of the zemstvo agitation. The Americans are deeply interested.
> North American Newspaper."

To this L. N. answers thus:

> "Philadelphia. North American Newspaper. 18 November 1904. The aim of the zemstvo agitation is the limitation of despotism and the establishment of a representative government. Whether the leaders of the agitation attain their aims or only continue to trouble society — in both cases the certain result of all this affair will be the postponement of true social improvement.
>
> True social improvement can be attained only by the religious, moral perfecting of all separate individuals. Political agitation, on the other hand, setting before separate individuals the ruinous illusion of social improvement by means of changing external forms, usually arrests true progress, as may be observed in all constitutional states: France, England, and America.
> Lev Tolstoy."

The life of Lev Nikolaevich, modest in itself, shone out upon the whole world and drew to itself the hearts of men, who expressed their sympathy with him each as best he could.

And now the Parisian intelligentsia stirred:

> "The art journal *Express* is opening a subscription for the erection in Paris of a monument to Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy. The construction of the monument will be entrusted to the well-known Russian sculptor Prince Trubetskoy. The organizers of the subscription intend to arrange a series of great popular festivities with the aim of attracting the popular masses to participation in the subscription and in the honouring of the great Russian writer. The Parisians even expect to see L. N. Tolstoy himself at the festivity being prepared and are proud that Paris is the first to raise a monument to Tolstoy in his lifetime."

What naïveté of the Parisian intelligentsia! They imagined that L. N. would himself go to Paris to unveil a monument to himself.

All these expressions of sympathy called forth, of course, a reaction too. The dark mass, instigated by embittered leaders, gnashed its teeth and concocted its intrigues, carried out with no less naïveté than the Parisian festivities.

A contributor to the newspaper *Nashi Dni* prints a letter received by him from a remote province at approximately this time:

> "Twelve versts from Glukhov there is a monastery, the 'Glinskaya Pustyn,' which for the third year now has been attracting general attention by a topical picture, painted in oils on the monastery wall, depicting Count L. N. Tolstoy surrounded by numerous sinners, among whom, judging by the inscription, one can find Herod Agrippa, Nero, Trajan, and other 'tormentors,' heretics, and sectarians.
>
> The picture is called 'The Church Militant'; in the midst of a sea stands a high cliff, and upon it a church and the righteous; below, the tossing sinful souls; on the right hand there burn in the unquenchable fire the enemies of the church who have already passed into a better world, and on the left — our contemporaries in frock-coats, blouses, and peasant coats hurl stones and fire from rifles at that cliff, on the summit of which stands the temple. Under each acting figure there is a number, and at the side an explanation: the *beguny*, the *molokane*, the *dukhobory*, the *skoptsy*, the *khlysty*, the *netovtsy*, the *perekreshchentsy*, the *pashkovtsy*, the *shtundisty*, and so forth.
>
> In a conspicuous place in the picture there is depicted an old man in a blouse and hat; above him stands No. 34, and at the side a comment: 'the extirpator of religion and of marriage unions.' Formerly on the hat of the 'extirpator of religion and of marriage unions' there was an inscription 'L. Tolstoy'; now this inscription is effaced; near the old man there is the figure of a worldly man, richly dressed, handing a hefty cobblestone to the 'extirpator of marriage unions.'
>
> By the monks' explanation, the man handing the stone is Prince Khilkov.
>
> Beside the topical picture pilgrims are forever crowding, and someone of the brethren with the greatest pathos gives them the corresponding elucidations:
>
> — A heretic he is, and a God-hater! And what are they looking at! Is that the way it should be? I would charge him into a cannon — and bang! Off you fly to the infidels, abroad, you stumpy little count!..
>
> And the preaching has success. From the neighbouring garden of Shalygin a peasant-butcher came to the abbot and asked a blessing for a great exploit:
>
> — I will go up to that old man, that destroyer of marriages," the peasant related his plan, "as if for advice, and there I will whip out a knife from behind my boot-top, and it is finished!..
>
> — Thy zeal is pleasing to God," answered the abbot, "but I will not give my blessing, because one will all the same have to answer for it..."

But L. N. continued his work of destroying the old and building up the new. Reworking in his consciousness the events of the external world, he records in his diary:

> "1 December. The existing order is to such a degree in its foundations contrary to the consciousness of society, that it cannot be set right if its foundations are left, just as one cannot set right the walls of a house in which the foundation is sinking; one must rebuild the whole thing, from the very bottom. One cannot set right the existing order — with the insane wealth and superfluity of some and the poverty and privations of the masses, with the right of landed property, the imposition of state taxes, territorial seizure, state patriotism, militarism, a knowingly false religion forcibly supported. One cannot set all this right by constitutions, universal suffrage, pensions to workers, the separation of state from church, and similar palliatives."

The military actions, developing, led Russia to a well-known catastrophe. Stoessel surrendered Port Arthur.

Lev Nik., with the sincerity proper to him, records in his diary:

> "31 December. The surrender of Port Arthur grieved me, it is painful to me. This is patriotism. I was brought up in it and am not free from it, just as I am not free from personal egoism, from family egoism, even aristocratic, and from patriotism. All these egoisms live in me; but in me there is the consciousness of the divine law, and this consciousness everywhere holds these egoisms in check, so that I am able not to serve them. And little by little these egoisms atrophy."

That this confession by L. N. of his patriotic weakness should not seem one-sided, let us adduce a letter of L. N.'s to the peasant Yakov Chaga, who had refused military service, in which he professes his conscious faith:

> "Dear Yakov Timofeevich, N has informed me of you and of your fate. When I come to know such men as you, and of what has happened to you, I always experience a feeling of shame, of envy, and the reproaches of conscience. I envy because I have lived out my life without having managed, without having known how, to show my faith in deed even once. I am ashamed because, at the time when you sit with so-called criminals in a stinking jail, I live in luxury with so-not-called criminals, making use of all the material comforts of life.
>
> The reproaches of conscience I feel because perhaps by my writings, which I write risking nothing, I was the cause of your act and of its heavy material consequences.
>
> But the strongest feeling which I experience towards such men as you is love and gratitude for the sake of all those millions of men who will make use of your deed.
>
> I know how your affair is complicated and made more difficult by family ties, but I think that if you do your deed not for men, but for God, for your own conscience, then the heaviness of the affair is lightened, you will find a way out and will accomplish the deed.
>
> God help you!"

Let us conclude this chapter with three formulas, summing up the conscious inner work of L. N. for this year 1904, set down in his December diary:

> "God is an x; but although the value of the x is unknown, we cannot, without the x, not only solve, but even compose any equation. And life is the solving of an equation."

> "Conscience is the action of the consciousness of the eternal, divine principle upon the temporal, bodily consciousness. Until this consciousness has awakened, there is no conscience. It is in vain to appeal to it."

> "The life of which I am conscious is the passage of the spiritual and unlimited (divine) essence through matter limited by bounds. This is true."
