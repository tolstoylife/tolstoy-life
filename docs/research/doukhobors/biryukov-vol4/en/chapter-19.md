# Volume IV, Chapter 19. 1910. Illness and Death

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-19.md`](../ru/chapter-19.md).

---

The beginning of L. N.'s illness has been described by Alexandra Lvovna in her reminiscences; I borrow from there the most essential passages. L. N. fell ill while still on the train, to the great alarm of the friends accompanying him.

Alexandra Lvovna writes thus:

> "Father's fever grew stronger and stronger; we brewed some tea and gave it to him to drink with red wine, but even this did not help — the chill continued.
>
> I cannot describe that state of horror which we were experiencing. For the first time in my life I felt that we had no shelter, no home. A smoke-filled second-class carriage, strange and alien people all around, and no home, no corner where we could take refuge with a sick old man.
>
> We passed Dankov and drew up at some large station. It was Astapovo. Dušan Petrovich ran off somewhere and a quarter of an hour later returned with some gentleman dressed in the uniform of a stationmaster. The stationmaster promised to give us a room in his apartment, where we could put the patient to bed, and we resolved to remain here. Father got up, was dressed, and, supported by Dušan Petrovich and the stationmaster, left the carriage; Varya and I remained behind to gather up the things.
>
> When we came to the station, we found father sitting in the ladies' room. He sat on a sofa in the corner in his brown overcoat and held a walking stick in his hand. He was trembling all over from head to foot, and his lips moved feebly. To my proposal that he lie down on the sofa he refused.
>
> The door from the ladies' room into the main hall was shut, and around it stood a crowd of the curious, awaiting the arrival of L. N. Every now and then ladies burst into the room, apologized, adjusted their hair and hats before the mirror, and went out. Dušan Petrovich, Varya, and the stationmaster went off to prepare the room. We sat with father and waited.
>
> But here they came for us. Again father was taken under the arms, on one side by Dušan Petrovich, on the other by the stationmaster. As they passed the public, who were crowded in the hall, all took off their hats; father, touching his own hat, returned everyone's bow. I saw how hard it was for him to walk: every now and then he swayed and almost hung on the arms of those who led him.
>
> When we entered the stationmaster's room, which served him as a sitting-room, a spring bed had already been set against the wall, and V. M. and I at once unpacked the trunks and set about making the bed. Father sat in his fur coat on a chair, and was just as cold as before. When the bed was ready, we proposed that he undress and lie down, but he refused, saying that he could not lie down until everything had been prepared for the night's lodging, as it always was. When he began to speak, I understood that he was beginning a fainting state, that one which had come over him before. At such moments he lost his memory, talked incoherently, uttering some incomprehensible words. He evidently fancied that he was at home, and he was surprised that everything was not in order, not as he was accustomed to it being at home.
>
> — I cannot yet lie down; do it as always. Set the night table by the bed, a chair.
>
> When this had been done, he began to ask that on the table there be set a candle, matches, his little notebook, a lantern, and everything to which he was accustomed at home. When all had been done, we again began to ask him to lie down, but he kept refusing. We understood that the situation was very serious and that, as had happened before, he might at any moment fall into unconsciousness. Dušan Petrovich, Varya, and I began bit by bit to undress him, and, without asking him further, we almost carried him to the bed.
>
> I sat down beside him, and not 15 minutes had passed before I noticed that his left arm and left leg had begun to twitch convulsively. The same thing appeared at times in the left half of his face. We all were terribly frightened. It was clear to us all that the situation was very, very dangerous, and that a bad outcome might come at any minute. We asked the stationmaster to send for the station doctor, who, in case of need, could help Dušan Petrovich. We gave father some strong wine and began to administer an enema. He said nothing, only groaned; his face was pale, and the convulsions, though weak, continued.
>
> Toward 9 o'clock it became better. Father breathed quietly, his breathing was even, calm.
>
> On waking, father was already fully conscious. Calling me to him, he smiled and asked with concern:
>
> — Well, Sasha?
>
> — Yes, well — it is not good.
>
> Tears were in my eyes and in my voice.
>
> — Do not lose heart; what could be better — for we are together.
>
> Toward night it became better still. We took his temperature — the fever began to fall quickly, and L. N. slept well through the night.
>
> Of course, none of us undressed, and we all sat in turn by the patient's bed, watching his every movement; in the middle of the night he called me to him and said:
>
> — What do you think — shall we be able to go on tomorrow?
>
> I said that in my opinion it was not possible, that we should have to wait at least another day.
>
> He sighed heavily and said nothing in reply.
>
> Suffering as he suffered in those minutes, both spiritually and physically, he remembered others all the time:
>
> — Ah, why are you sitting up! You ought to go and sleep, — several times in the course of the night he addressed us.
>
> Sometimes he was delirious in his sleep, and every time his delirium expressed the fear that he should not succeed in getting away.
>
> — To flee... to flee... they will catch up...
>
> He asked us not to inform the newspaper about his illness and in general to inform no one of anything about him. I tried to calm him; his calm was dearer to me than anything and anyone in the world.
>
> On the morning of 1 November, having taken his temperature, we revived: the thermometer showed 36.2. Father's condition is fairly cheerful, he begins to speak of the need to go further. He was, apparently, very disturbed that it might be discovered where he was, and that my mother might come, and he, calling me to him, dictated to me the following telegram to Chertkov:
>
> 'Fell ill yesterday, passengers saw I walked weakened from the train, very much afraid of publicity, today better, going on further, take measures, let us know.'
>
> Taking advantage of father's good state, I resolved to ask him what I needed to know in case his illness should drag on and prove dangerous. I did not close my eyes to the fact that an enormous responsibility lay upon me; but in these few days the situation of our family had changed. My father had himself left his family, had broken with it. I could not and would not any longer reckon with my mother and brothers; but on the other hand I considered myself bound to inform them, since I had promised to let them know in the event that father should fall ill. That is why I asked father whether he wished me to let mother, the brothers, and my sister know in case his illness should prove serious.
>
> He was very alarmed by my words and several times very insistently begged me on no account to let the family know of his whereabouts and illness.
>
> — Chertkov, however, I should like to see, — he added.
>
> I at once sent Chertkov a telegram of the following content:
>
> 'Yesterday got off at Astapovo, severe fever, lapses of memory, in morning normal temperature, now again chill. Going further unthinkable, expressed wish to see you. Frolova' (my pseudonym). To which, several hours later, I received an answer that Chertkov would be in Astapovo the following day, in the morning.
>
> On the same morning father dictated to me the following thoughts into his little notebook:
>
> 'God is the unlimited All, man is only a limited manifestation of Him.'
>
> I wrote it down and waited for him to dictate further.
>
> — Nothing more, — he said.
>
> He chewed for some time, as if pondering something, and then, again calling me to him, said:
>
> — Take the notebook and pen and write. Or, still better, so:
>
> 'God is that unlimited All of which man is conscious of being a limited part.
>
> Truly only God exists. Man is His manifestation in matter, in time, and in space. The more the manifestation of God in man (life) is united with the manifestations (lives) of other beings, the more he exists. This union of his own life with the lives of other beings is accomplished by love.
>
> God is not love, but the more love there is, the more man manifests God, the more truly he exists.
>
> God we recognize only through the consciousness of His manifestation in us. All the conclusions from this consciousness, and the guidance of life founded upon it, always fully satisfy man both in the knowledge of God Himself and in the guidance of his own life founded upon this consciousness.'
>
> After some time he again called me to him and said:
>
> — Now I want to write to Tanya and Seryozha.
>
> What was tormenting him, evidently, was that he had asked me not to summon them by telegram, and he wanted to explain the reason why he did not venture to see them.
>
> I give here in its entirety this letter, in the highest degree touching and heartfelt:
>
> 1 November. 10. Astapovo.
>
> 'My dear children Tanya and Seryozha. I hope and am sure that you will not reproach me for not having summoned you. The summoning of you alone, without mama, would have been a great grief to her, and also to the other brothers. You will both understand that Chertkov, whom I have summoned, stands in an exceptional position toward me. He has devoted his whole life to the service of that cause which I too have served the last 40 years of my life. The cause is dear to me to the extent that I acknowledge — whether I err or not — its importance for all people and for you among them.
>
> I thank you for your good attitude toward me. I do not know whether I am taking leave or not, but I felt the necessity of expressing what I have expressed.
>
> I also wanted to add a piece of advice to you, Seryozha — that you should think about your life, about who you are, what you are, in what lies the meaning of human life, and how every rational man ought to live it through. Those views of Darwinism, evolution, and the struggle for existence which you have assimilated will not explain to you the meaning of your life and will not give you guidance in your actions, and life without an explanation of its significance and meaning, and without the unchanging guidance proceeding from this, is a pitiful existence. Think about this. Loving you, probably on the eve of death, I say this.
>
> Farewell, try to calm mother, toward whom I have the most sincere feeling of compassion and love.
> Your loving father Lev Tolstoy.'
>
> Several times he had to break off dictating because of the tears rising in his throat, and at moments I could barely make out his voice — so quietly, quietly he spoke.
>
> I wrote it down in shorthand, then copied it out and brought it to him to sign.
>
> — Hand it to them after my death, — he said, and wept again, and I too.
>
> At 9 o'clock the chill began, and the fever started to rise. He groaned greatly, tossed about, complained of headache. By 4 o'clock the temperature was already 39.8."

The next morning, 2 November, V. G. Chertkov arrived in Astapovo. He at once went in to Lev Nikolaevich. In his reminiscences V. G. relates this meeting thus:

> "He was very glad to see me, held out his hand, which I carefully took and kissed. He shed tears and at once began to ask how it was at home with me.
>
> During our conversation he began to breathe heavily and to groan and said:
>
> 'A swoon is much better: one feels nothing, and then one wakes and all is well.' Evidently the illness made him suffer physically.
>
> Soon he spoke of what, at that minute, was evidently troubling him most. With especial animation he told me that every measure must be taken so that Sofya Andreyevna should not come to him. He several times asked me with agitation what she intended to undertake. When I informed him that she had declared she would not, against his wish, seek a meeting with him, he felt great relief and on that day did not speak to me again of his fears."

Alexandra Lvovna continues her account thus:

> "At 8 o'clock Seryozha arrived, not having received my second telegram. He was very upset, wished without fail to see father, and at the same time himself realized that such a meeting would agitate and upset him. We hesitated long. Seryozha stood in the next room and looked at father. Then suddenly he said decisively:
>
> — No, I will go in. I will tell him that I had learned by chance at Gorbachevo, from a conductor, that he was here, and had come.
>
> Father was greatly agitated when he saw him, questioned him in detail as to how Seryozha had learned of his whereabouts and illness, and what he knew of mother, where she was and with whom. Seryozha answered that he was from Moscow, but that he knew mother was at Yasnaya and that with her were a doctor, a nurse, and the younger brothers.
>
> — I see that mother cannot be admitted to him, — said Sergei, coming out of the room, — it would agitate him too much.
>
> When Seryozha had gone out from him, father called me to him:
>
> — Seryozha, what a fellow!
>
> — Why, papa?
>
> — How he found me. I am very glad of him, he is pleasant to me... He kissed my hand, — said father, with difficulty, through sobs.
>
> That same evening there arrived the zemstvo physician Semyonovsky, whom we had summoned from Dankov. He examined L. N. together with Dušan Petrovich and the railway doctor, and diagnosed inflammation of the lungs. Father very good-naturedly let the doctors examine and tap him, and when they had finished, he asked Semyonovsky whether he would be able to leave in two days' time. Semyonovsky answered that he could hardly leave even in two weeks. Father, apparently, was very grieved and, without answering anything, turned toward the wall."

In the evening, about 10 o'clock, there arrived in Astapovo an extra train, bringing from Tula the family of L. N. — Sofya Andreyevna, Tatyana Lvovna, and three sons: Ilya, Andrei, and Mikhail Lvovich.

> — Dušan Petrovich, having met the train, — relates the stationmaster Iv. Iv. Ozolin, — went to the Tolstoys' carriage and informed them of the course of the illness and of the state of affairs. Andr. Lvov. declined to occupy the apartments offered to them, pointing out that in the carriage it was more convenient for them all; besides, being all together, they could discuss any situations immediately, in common. The carriage was shunted onto a siding not far from the station. Of those who had arrived, only Tatyana Lvovna went into the apartment to talk with Alexandra Lvovna.
>
> Dušan Petrovich, on returning, reported that for the night none of those who had arrived would come in, and the children of Lev Nikolaevich resolved to take every measure that S. A. should not come in to the patient. In the house, too, it was resolved to watch carefully lest S. A. should somehow accidentally enter. Early in the morning the sons of Lev Nikolaevich came, but did not go in to father. S. A. came to the porch of the house, but they did not open the door to her, fearing she would demand to be let in to the patient.

By the morning train on the fourth day (3 November) there arrived from Moscow Dr. Nikitin and L. N.'s friends — Iv. Iv. Gorbunov-Posadov and Alex. Boris. Goldenweiser. In spite of all his weakness, L. N., on learning of their arrival, wished without fail to see them.

Especially long did he converse with Iv. Ivanovich. We borrow from the reminiscences of Elena Evgenyevna Gorbunova, the wife of Iv. Ivanovich, the description of this last meeting:

> "Lev Nikolaevich lay upon his back. Behind stood a screen, shielding him from the window. In the half-light of the November dusk Iv. Iv. could not clearly see the face of Lev Nikolaevich, but it seemed to him that the face infinitely dear to him shone forth in the gloom. Every sound of the weak voice of Lev Nikolaevich so deeply penetrated the heart.
>
> — We are united not only by the work, but also by love, — said Lev Nikolaevich, and a deep tenderness was in his voice.
>
> — And all the work which we worked with you, Lev Nikolaevich, — said Iv. Iv., — all proceeded from love. God grant, you and I shall yet fight on for her.
>
> — You — yes, — said Lev Nikolaevich. — I — no.
>
> Lev Nikolaevich, with his ever-present affection, asked Iv. Ivanovich about all our family. And then he spoke of the little books being set up at *Posrednik* — chapters from *The Path of Life*, his last great work of the very latest time, the printing of which he especially cherished. Iv. Iv. informed Lev Nikolaevich that he had brought with him the latest proofs of two chapters of *The Path of Life*.
>
> — I can do no more. Do it yourselves, — said Lev Nikolaevich.
>
> Then he asked about the progress of the other little books, the appearance of which he had very much at heart. This was that series of little books about the greatest religious teachings, whose program he had much discussed with Iv. Ivanovich in the latter years, and to the compilation of which he, with especial attention and love, had set his hand. By Lev Nikolaevich himself there had been compiled for this series a little book on the teaching of Lao-Tzu. A whole further series of such little books was contemplated. About each of these little books Lev Nikolaevich was especially solicitous. Now with love he was speaking of them for the last time with my husband.
>
> The conversation of Lev Nikolaevich with Iv. Ivanovich continued some minutes longer. Lev Nikolaevich, in a weakening voice, spoke of the thought that had become, evidently, especially dear to him — that one ought not so much to struggle with evil in people as to strive with all one's strength to make clear to them the truth — to make clear the good to those people doing evil. He spoke this as though it were his last testament to Iv. Ivanovich.
>
> But here the hand of Alex. Lvov. touched my husband's shoulder, saying that we must finish."

The further description of this day we take again from the reminiscences of V. G. Chertkov:

> "L. N. could still, supported on both sides, take two or three steps about the room for his necessities. But when he sat, his head from weakness drooped forward, and I remember how touchingly he thanked me when I supported his head with the palm of my hand. On the way back to the bed it was again necessary to support him and then to lay him in bed, carefully lifting his legs and wrapping them up in the blanket. Once, at the end of these operations, in which two doctors and I took part, L. N., lying on his back and quickly catching his breath after the efforts performed, said in a weak, pitiful voice: 'But the muzhiks — how the muzhiks die,' — and shed tears.
>
> On this day, — V. G. continues, — when we were alone, Lev Nikolaevich informed me in a whisper that on the table lay his pocket notebook, in which from one end there was written his intimate, secret diary, and from the other — separate thoughts, subject to the usual entering into his big diary. The leaves of the intimate diary (the little book was one with detachable pages) he asked me to take out and to put away together with the earlier ones, given over by him to A. L. and to me, such leaves. And the thoughts written from the other end of the little book he charged me to enter, in due time, into his diary. Then he asked that this big diary of his be brought to him and began to write in it. And I went to carry out his commission about the pocket notebook."

On this same day, as V. G. relates, L. N., with his help, looked through the correspondence that had been brought and gave instructions as to how to answer this or that letter.

One letter was from his friend, the peasant Mikhail Petrovich Novikov, who had lately visited him, to whom L. N. had written, asking whether he could temporarily settle in his cottage if it should be necessary to leave Yasnaya Polyana. Novikov very heartily answered that he would be very glad to offer L. N. hospitality at his place.

On this same day L. N. wished to see Tatyana Lvovna. Here is how she describes this meeting in a letter to her husband:

> "He called me, since they had let slip that I had arrived. They brought him his little cushion, and when he asked where it was from — holy Dušan could not lie and said that I had brought it. About mama and the brothers they did not tell him. He began by saying, in a weak, broken voice with pauses for breath: 'how dressed up and *avantageuse* you are.' I said that I knew his bad taste, and laughed. Then he began to question me about mama. This I feared most of all, because I was afraid to tell him that she was here, but to lie outright to him, I felt that I should not have strength enough. Fortunately, he put the questions in such a way that I did not have to tell him a direct lie. 'With whom has she stayed?' — 'With Andrei and Misha.' — 'And Misha?' — 'Yes, they are all very united in not letting her come to you until you wish it...' — 'And Andrei?' — 'Yes, and Andrei. They are very sweet, the younger boys, very worn out, the poor things, trying in every way to calm her.' — 'Well, tell me, what does she do? Does she busy herself?' — 'Papenka, perhaps it is better not to tell you, you grow agitated...' — Then he very energetically interrupted me, but all the same in a tearful, broken voice he said: 'Speak, speak, what can be more important to me than this?' — And he went on questioning: 'Who is with her? Is the doctor good?' I said no, and that we had sent him away, but that there was a very good medical assistant, who had served three and a half years in S. S. Korsakov's clinic. 'So, she is used to such patients... And has she come to love her?' — 'Yes.' — 'Well, further. Does she eat?' — 'Yes, she eats and now tries to keep herself up, because she lives by the hope of seeing you.' — 'Did she receive my letter?' — 'Yes.' — 'And how did she take it?' — 'What chiefly calmed her was the extract from your letter to Chertkov, in which you write that you do not refuse to return to her on condition of her becoming calm.' — 'Did you and Seryozha receive my letter?' — 'Yes, papenka, but I am sorry that you did not address the younger brothers. They have taken everything so well.' — 'Why, I wrote to all, I wrote "children"...' — Then he asked me where I should go from here — to mama again or to my husband. I said that perhaps first to you (that is, to my husband). He said: 'A pity that you cannot summon him. He must, after all, stay with little Tanya.' I asked: 'And would you have liked to see him?' — 'Not to summon him here — to her, to Yasnaya...' — I said that you had telegraphed her, very insistently inviting her to Kochety, to her granddaughter, but that she had only said 'thank him' to this and had not gone, because she is waiting for papa to summon her to him. He fell silent. Then he bade me read in the *Circle of Reading* for 28 October. And he said: 'I read it after...'"

Here are the chief thoughts in the *Circle of Reading* for 28 October:

> "As the sensation of pain is a necessary condition for the preservation of our body, so sufferings are necessary conditions of our life from birth to death.
>
> To every creature is profitable not only all that is sent to it by providence, but also at the very time when it is sent." Marcus Aurelius.

> "Suffering is a stimulus to activity, and only in it do we for the first time feel our life." Kant.

> "Do not grow accustomed to prosperity — it is transient: he who possesses, let him learn to lose; he who is happy, let him learn to suffer." Schiller.

> "Torments, sufferings are experienced only by him who, having separated himself from the life of the world, not seeing those sins of his own by which he brought sufferings into the world, considers himself not guilty and therefore is indignant against those sufferings which he bears for the sins of the world."

This pointing-out by him to his daughter of the thoughts in the *Circle of Reading* on the day of his departure, the meaning of which lies in the humble bearing of sufferings, is very significant. Especially remarkable is the remark that he read them *after*. And if he had read them earlier? Would what came to pass have come to pass?

We continue the account again from the reminiscences of V. G. Chertkov:

> "On the following day, 4 November, what gave L. N. an especially pained expression was the look of his parched and whitened lips. In the subsequent days, however, this was no longer so. But in general with every day his cheeks grew thinner, the lips became thinner and paler, and his whole face took on an ever more and more wearied look, witnessing to the physical sufferings which he had to bear. Especially was this suffering expression noticeable around the lips and mouth, which, owing to the difficulty of breathing, remained for the most part half-open and twisted. Other signs of physical anguish he scarcely manifested. The groans and loud sighs which accompanied for hours together his every breath, his every hiccup, were so even and monotonous that they did not produce the impression of especially acute suffering. When, in connection with this, he was once or twice asked whether he was suffering very much, he answered in the negative. Only several times in the course of the whole illness did there come over him attacks of especially severe sufferings. In these cases he convulsively raised himself into a sitting position, swinging his legs from the bed, tossed wretchedly from side to side, said that it was very hard for him, painful, but soon again sank back upon the pillows and grew quiet with a look of meek reconciliation with the inevitable trial. He, evidently, was conscious that the patient, uncomplaining bearing of the increasing physical anguish represented at that minute his nearest task. And, judging by how he bore himself, to the fulfilling of this task he related himself with that same conscientious and sustained persistence with which all his life he had been accustomed to do that which he considered binding upon himself."

We pass again to the notes of Alex. Lvovna:

> "The morning of 4 November was also very disturbed. Father said something which those around him could in no way understand, groaned loudly, sighed, begging us to understand his thought, to help him...
>
> And it seemed to me that we did not understand his thoughts not because they were senseless — I clearly saw from his serious, spiritualized face that for him they had a profound, important meaning — but that we could not understand them because he was no longer able to convey them in our language, intelligible to us.
>
> But at moments he spoke clearly and firmly. Thus, he said to Vl. Grigoryevich:
>
> — It seems I am dying, but perhaps not.
>
> Then he said something indistinct, and further:
>
> — But still, one must try a little longer.
>
> During the day they aired the bedroom and carried father into another room. When he was again carried into the bedroom, he stared fixedly at the glass door which was opposite his bed, and asked Varya, who was on duty by him, where this glass door led. Varya answered that it led to a little corridor. He asked:
>
> — And what is beyond the corridor?
>
> She said the entry and the porch. I came into the room at that moment.
>
> — And is that door locked? — father asked, turning to me.
>
> I said that it was locked.
>
> — Strange, I clearly saw that from behind this door two women's faces were looking at me.
>
> We said this could not be, since from the corridor to the entry the door was also locked.
>
> But it was clear that he was not calmed and went on with anxiety looking at the glass door. Varya and I took a plaid and covered it.
>
> — Ah, now it is good, — father said with relief, turned to the wall, and for a time grew quiet.
>
> There appeared yet another ominous sign. Father did not cease to pick with his fingers. He took one edge of the blanket in his hands and picked at it with his fingers up to the other edge and back, and so on without end. This terribly alarmed me. I remembered that with my sister Masha, two days before her death, this movement of the fingers had appeared.
>
> At times he lay perfectly motionless, was silent, did not even groan, and looked before him. In this gaze of his there was something for me new, distant, incomprehensible to me. 'The end,' — flashed in my head.
>
> At times he tried to finish saying something, to express some persistent thought of his, which seemed to give him no peace. He would begin to speak, but felt that he was saying the wrong thing, groaned loudly and sighed.
>
> — Don't think, — I said to him.
>
> — Ah, how not to think — one must, one must think.
>
> And he again tried to say something, tossed about, and was, apparently, suffering very much. Toward evening the delirium began again, and father again asked, implored us to understand his thought, to help...
>
> — Sasha, go and see how this will end, — he said to me.
>
> I tried to distract him.
>
> — Perhaps you would like to drink?
>
> — Ah, no, no... How not to understand, it is so simple.
>
> — And again, and again he begged us:
>
> — Come here, what are you afraid of, you do not want to help me, I am asking everyone...
>
> What I would not have given to understand, to help. But however much I strained my mind, I could not understand what he wanted, could not help.
>
> He continued to say something incomprehensible to us:
>
> — To seek, all the time to seek...
>
> Varya came into the room.
>
> Father raised himself in the bed, stretched out his arms, and in a loud joyful voice, looking fixedly at Varya, cried out:
>
> — Masha, Masha!
>
> He seemed to be looking for her, waiting for her.
>
> Varya rushed out of the room, frightened, shaken.
>
> In the evening father became easier and fell asleep.
>
> The night from the 4th to the 5th of November was again very disturbed. I did not leave father's side. He kept tossing, groaning, sighing. Again he asked me to write down; I took a pencil and paper, but there was nothing to write down, and he asked me to read out what had been dictated.
>
> — Read what I have written, read what I have written. Why are you silent? What have I written, — he repeated, growing more and more excited.
>
> All this time we tried to keep watch by twos, but here it happened somehow that I was left alone by father's bed. He seemed to have dozed off. But suddenly with a strong movement he raised himself on the pillows and began to lower his feet from the bed.
>
> — What is it, papa?
>
> — Let me, let me go, — he made a movement to get off the bed.
>
> I knew that if he got up, I should not be able to hold him, he would fall, and I tried in every way to calm him and to keep him on the bed. But he tore away from me with all his strength and said:
>
> — Let go, you have no right to hold me, let go!
>
> Seeing that I could not manage father, since my exhortations and entreaties had no effect, while I had not the heart to hold him by force, I began to call out:
>
> — Doctor, doctor, quick, here!
>
> It seems that at that time Dr. Semyonovsky was on duty. He came in with Varya, and we succeeded in calming father and keeping him on the bed.
>
> It was clear that father was suffering terribly, and I suffered with him, being unable to help him."

We pass again to the reminiscences of V. G. Chertkov.

> "On 5 November, at 2 o'clock in the night, A. L. woke me with the words: 'Papa is not well!' I jumped up and, putting on my jacket and slippers, heard, coming from the third room from me, where L. N. was, his loud and excited voice. Hurrying to him, I found him sitting across the bed. When I went up to him, he said that he wanted to dictate. I took out my notebook. He prepared to set forth his thoughts, but first asked me to read out what had already been dictated by him. I explained to him that I had only just come in and had not yet had time to write anything down. Then L. N. asked me to read out from Dr. Semyonovsky what he had written down. The latter, sitting near the bed, looked at me significantly and turned his notebook in my direction, to show that he had nothing written down. Only then did I understand that L. N. was in delirium. He began insistently to demand that I should read out what was written down with Semyonovsky. Semyonovsky himself at this time rose and carefully went out of the room.
>
> L. N. Well, do read, please.
>
> I. He, L. N., has written nothing down for himself. Tell me what you want to write down.
>
> L. N. (still more insistently). But no, do read. Why do you not want to read?
>
> I. But nothing is written down!
>
> L. N. (reproachfully). Ah, how strange! Here you are, after all, a dear person, and yet you do not want to read.
>
> This painful scene continued for a fairly long time, until A. L. advised me to read out something from the book lying near me on the table. It turned out to be the *Circle of Reading*, which L. N. always kept by him, never omitting to read from it the daily little chapter. I found the part referring to 5 November. As soon as I began to read, L. N. completely grew quiet and all became attention, from time to time asking me to repeat some word he had not fully caught. And during the whole time of the reading he not once tried to interrupt me in order to dictate his own. 'And whose is this?' — he asked several times about thoughts in the *Circle of Reading*. But when after some time, supposing that he was tired, I stopped, he, having waited a little to convince himself that I did not intend to continue the reading, said: 'well, so then...' and was preparing to repeat his dictation. Fearing the repetition of his excitement, I hastened to continue the reading, whereupon he at once again submissively set himself to listen. The same thing was repeated yet once more. And when I, after a long time, began bit by bit to lower my voice and at last entirely ceased the reading, he, must be wearied, uttered now in a satisfied tone: 'well, so' and grew entirely quiet."

We continue the account from the letters of Tatyana Lvovna.

5 November 1910. Astapovo.

> "I sat with him in the morning. Varya read the *Circle of Reading*. He kept living through it all, asked her to read something else, evidently was tormented by something which he could not express. He asked: 'Is it finished. How can you not understand? Why do you not want to do it?' Evidently he was tormented and irritated. On my second visit Dušan and I gave him sweetened water for the hiccup. (Besides Sasha, he now took drink and food from me also, so that Dušan came for me several times to offer him to eat and to drink.) He himself either held or supported the glass, and himself wiped his mustache and lips. The hiccup passed for a time. Then Sasha and I fed him with oatmeal porridge. 'Papenka, dear, open your mouth. So. Wider.' And he submitted very meekly. He recognized me, looked at me and said: 'dear Tanechka.' He hiccupped again, but this time the sugared water did not help. A little later I offered coffee. I said that he should drink it for the hiccup to pass. He pushed my hand away and said: 'le mieux est l'ennemi du bien' (*). Then, when I said to myself: 'what a torment' — he heard and said: 'there is no torment whatever.'
>
> (* "The best is the enemy of the good." *)
>
> At 5 o'clock I sat with Dr. Semyonovsky. Papa looked at me and says: 'A great deal falls upon Sonya.' I heard him, but wished to know more surely that he was speaking of her, and asked again: 'falls upon soda?' — 'Upon Sonya... a great deal falls upon Sonya. We have arranged things badly...' Then he said something indistinct. I asked: 'Do you want to see her? Do you want to see Sonya?' He answered nothing, gave no sign, neither negative nor positive. My hands and feet began to tremble, and I went all cold. To repeat the question I did not dare. It would have been the same as blowing out a candle that is going out.
>
> Once he asked me: 'are you not going to your own people?' I answered: 'No, papenka.' He sadly answered: 'why so?'"

We again turn to the reminiscences of Alexandra Lvovna.

> "6 November. Compared with the previous night, this night passed fairly quietly.
>
> Toward morning the temperature was 37.3; the heart very weak, but better than the day before. All the doctors, except Berkengeim, who all the time looked at the illness very hopelessly, took heart and to our questions answered that, although the situation was very serious, they had not yet lost hope.
>
> At 10 o'clock in the morning there arrived, summoned from Moscow by my family and the doctors, Shchurovsky and Usov.
>
> When they came into father's room, he asked:
>
> — Who has come?
>
> They answered that Shchurovsky and Usov had arrived. He said:
>
> — I remember them. — And then, having been silent a little, in an affectionate voice he added: — Dear people.
>
> When the doctors examined father, he, evidently having taken Usov for Dušan, embraced and kissed him. But then, having convinced himself of his mistake, he said:
>
> — No, not the one, not the one.
>
> Shchurovsky and Usov found the situation serious, almost hopeless.
>
> Yes, I knew this without them. Although since morning all had taken heart, I had almost no hope already. All my spiritual and physical powers at once forsook me. I could scarcely force myself to do what was needed and could no longer hold back from the sobs rising to my throat...
>
> On this day he seemed to be taking leave of us all. Around him the doctors were busy with something. Father affectionately looked at Dušan and with a deep tenderness in his voice said:
>
> — Dear Dušan, dear Dušan.
>
> Another time they were changing the sheets, and I supported father's back. And then I feel that his hand is searching for mine. I thought that he wanted to lean on me, but he firmly, firmly pressed my hand once, then a second time. I pressed his hand and clung to it with my lips, trying to hold back the sobs rising to my throat."

We supplement the description of this day from Tatyana Lvovna's letter to her husband.

> "6 November 1910... Forgive me, but I am not in a state to write. The bulletins you read in the newspapers; what I experience is too long and hard; to speak of my hopes and fears is also senseless, since they change every minute. Today, when I went in to him, he recognized me and said: 'hello, Tanechka.' And once, when I came in, he asked: 'who is it — Tanya or...' I hastened to say that it was I. Today I sat with him alone. Semyonovsky had gone out to prepare a syringe of camphor. He held out his hand to me, took mine, and said: 'Here is the end... and nothing...' — and began to breathe more and more quietly. I thought that the last minutes had come. I wanted to get up, to call the doctor, but he held me back by the hand. Then Semyonovsky came and injected camphor. After a few minutes he raised himself energetically, so that we raised his pillows behind him, almost sat up and in a fully audible voice said: 'one thing only I advise you to remember: there is a multitude of people in the world besides Lev Tolstoy, and you look only at the one Lev...' The last words were spoken now more weakly, and immediately after this he fell into unconsciousness."

On this day there took place in Astapovo one episode which has very little direct relation to L. N., but nevertheless touched him indirectly. The matter is that L. N. was under the excommunication of the Synod and under the prohibition of prayer for him in the event of his death. The Synod had not reckoned with the consequences of its absurd act, but when the death of L. N. began to approach, it bestirred itself and took every measure depending on it, that it might be possible, as though nothing had happened, to perform over L. N. all the commemorative rites and the funeral office. One of such measures was the sending to Astapovo of the monk Varsonofy, the abbot of Optina Pustyn, for the exhortation and reception of L. N. into the bosom of the Church. As early as 4 November there had been received a telegram from Metropolitan Anthony of the following content:

> "From the very first moment of your breach with the Church I have unceasingly prayed and pray that the Lord might return you to the Church. Perhaps He will soon call you to His judgment, and I now beseech you, sick as you are, to be reconciled with the Church and with the Orthodox Russian people."

By the common consent of the relatives, friends, and physicians surrounding L. N., it was decided not to show this telegram to L. N. In the evening of that same 4 November, Varsonofy arrived in Astapovo. On 5 November he did not show activity, but on the 6th, having probably learned from those about him that the situation was worsening, he resolved to come forward with his mission. Here is how Alexandra Lvovna relates this in her reminiscences:

> "In the evening someone told me that Father Varsonofy wished to see me. All my relatives and the doctors had flatly refused him his request to see father, but he still found it necessary to address himself with the same to me as well.
>
> I did not wish to and could not see him, and therefore I wrote him a letter of the following content:
>
> 'Forgive me, Father, that I do not fulfill your request and do not come to converse with you. I am at this time unable to leave my sick father, who at any moment may need me.
>
> To add to what you have heard from all our family, I can add nothing.
>
> We, all the family, have unanimously resolved, before all other considerations, to submit to father's will and wish, whatever they may be.
>
> After his will we submit to the prescriptions of the doctors, who find that at this time to propose anything to him or to constrain his will would be ruinous to his health.
> With sincere respect to you, Alexandra Tolstoy.'
>
> To this letter I received from Father Varsonofy an answer, which I cite in full:
>
> 'Your Excellency, most worthy Countess Alexandra Lvovna. I wish you peace and joy from the Lord Jesus Christ. I respectfully thank Your Excellency for your letter, in which you write that the will of your parent is placed for you and for your whole family in the first plane. But it is known to you, Countess, that the Count expressed to his sister, and your aunt, the nun Mother Maria, the desire to see us and to converse with us, that he might find the desired peace for his soul, and was deeply grieved that his desire was not fulfilled. In view of this, I respectfully ask you, Countess, not to refuse to inform the Count of my arrival in Astapovo, and if he should wish to see me, even for 2-3 minutes, I shall at once come to him. In the event of a negative answer on the Count's part, I shall return to Optina Pustyn, having delivered this matter into the will of God.
> The sinful abbot Varsonofy, your unworthy man of prayer.'
>
> To this letter from Abbot Varsonofy I no longer replied. I had no time for it.
>
> In the evening into the dining room came the brothers, the doctors; Shchurovsky talked much with Vl. Gr. of the state of father's illness, in connection with which he did not despair and said that the patient still had strength.
>
> Then all dispersed to sleep, and only Berkengeim and Usov remained.
>
> I fell asleep. They woke me at 10 o'clock. Father had become worse. He had begun to suffocate. They raised him up on the pillows, and he, supported by us, sat, with his legs hanging from the bed.
>
> — Hard to breathe, — he said hoarsely, with difficulty.
>
> They woke everyone. The doctors gave him oxygen to breathe and proposed making an injection of morphine. Father did not agree:
>
> — No, no need, I do not want it, — he said.
>
> Having consulted between themselves, the doctors resolved to inject camphor, to lift the weakened activity of the heart.
>
> When they wanted to make the injection, father pulled his hand back. He was told that this was not morphine, but camphor, and he agreed.
>
> After the injection father seemed to become better. He called Seryozha: 'Seryozha!' And when Seryozha came up: 'The truth... I love much... how they...'
>
> These were his last words.
>
> But at the time it seemed to us that the danger had passed. All grew calm and again dispersed to sleep, and around father remained only those on duty.
>
> All these days I had hardly undressed and hardly slept, and now I so much wanted to sleep that I could not overcome myself. I lay down on the sofa and at once fell asleep, like one slain.
>
> They woke me about 12. All gathered.
>
> Father had again become worse. At first he groaned, tossed about, the heart was scarcely working. The doctors injected morphine, he fell asleep.
>
> Father slept until 4 o'clock in the morning. The doctors did something else, made some injections. He lay on his back and breathed often and hoarsely. The expression of his face was stern, serious and, as it seemed to me, strange.
>
> He was quietly dying."

The physician D. V. Nikitin relates in his notes that shortly before the end of Lev Nikolaevich the doctors resolved to satisfy themselves whether he still had consciousness. Dušan Petrovich Makovitsky took a glass of water with wine, brought it up to the lips of Lev Nikolaevich, and loudly, solemnly uttered: "Lev Nikolaevich, moisten your lips." Lev Nikolaevich half-opened his eyes, took a swallow. Thus, it may be thought that the last human words of this world to reach the consciousness of Lev Nikolaevich were the good words of his faithful friend and the companion of his last journey, Dušan Petrovich Makovitsky.

Alexandra Lvovna continues her account:

> "They spoke of the necessity of admitting S. A.
>
> I implored them not to admit her while father was in consciousness. I was afraid that her coming might poison his last minutes.
>
> I went up to him, he was scarcely breathing. For the last time I kissed his face, his hands...
>
> They brought my mother in, he was already unconscious. I withdrew and sat down on the sofa. Almost all who were in the room were sobbing dully, my mother was saying something, lamenting. She was asked to be silent. Yet one more last breath. All was finished.
>
> In the room — complete silence.
>
> Suddenly Shchurovsky said something in a loud, sharp voice, my mother answered him, and all began to speak loudly.
>

The last day of L. N.'s life is so significant that we think it necessary to bring forward another description of it, from the account of Sergei Lvovich Tolstoy, at a gathering of friends which took place at his apartment on 28 November 1910.

Sergei Lvovich related among other things the following:

> "Usov and Shchurovsky arrived. They examined father, and when they had gone, I remained with him together with Nikitin. The heart was working badly, breathing was about 50 and the pulse weak. Cyanosis appeared. The nose grew sharp, the ears and lips turned blue, and father picked at things with his hands. Here, for the first time, I lost hope. But then he began bit by bit to improve; they began to give him oxygen to breathe. After the cardiac attack, bit by bit the breathing became less frequent, the hiccup ceased, and the situation became quite another. I saw that Nikitin suddenly began again to hope. In general, by the way, he hoped more than the other doctors, because he had seen Lev Nikolaevich in a more grievous state in the Crimea and at Yasnaya Polyana. At 11 o'clock in the evening father groaned, breathed heavily, but sat up on the bed and said: 'hard, I am afraid I am dying.' He expectorated, swallowed the phlegm, said: 'ah, nasty...' — and began to grow delirious. And then he raised himself and says: 'must flee... must flee...' A few minutes later he, seeing me, called me to him: 'Seryozha.' I went up to him, knelt down beside the bed, in order to hear better, and heard a whole phrase: 'The truth... I love much... how they...'
>
> At 12 o'clock he began to toss about, breathing became frequent, hoarseness and hiccup began. Then Usov said that morphine must be injected. Nikitin supported this, because L. N. had earlier in the Crimea borne it well. Usov said that with the morphine the hiccup would cease, and then the activity of the heart would be better. When father was asked whether he wanted morphine to be injected, he answered: 'do not want morphine.' We left this to the doctors, who did inject the morphine, after which he could not sleep for about 20 minutes. And then, as he was falling asleep, he was saying: 'I shall go somewhere, so that no one shall disturb me... leave me in peace...' Then he fell asleep. For about three hours he was under the action of morphine, but he no longer came to consciousness and kept groaning. They gave him to drink, and once he even took the glass in his hand and drank. Breathing all the time frequent, but no more frequent than it had been the day before. The doctors said that there was no pulse any more. Then there occurred a stoppage of breathing, and Usov said: 'the first stoppage.' Then there was a second stoppage. There was no agony at all. After the first stoppage he sighed two or three times. The doctors said that death had come from paralysis of the breathing, but not of the heart. Dm. Vas. and Dušan Petr. washed the body, dressed it, and bit by bit we all dispersed, and the people began to come to take leave."

Before continuing our narrative, let us adduce a few thoughts on how L. N. himself related to his illness and to the question of the possible approaching death. Here is what V. G. Chertkov says on this account in his reminiscences:

> "Did L. N. during this illness of his think of the possibility of its mortal outcome? And if he did think, then how did he relate to this possibility?
>
> Of the nearness of death L. N. in general constantly thought, and during the whole last period of his life he often said that he felt himself on the eve of death. And at every illness he was inclined to think that he would no longer recover. So that on this one ground alone it could safely be concluded that during this illness of his he both thought of death and admitted the possibility of its coming on. This same is confirmed by certain of his separate words and remarks during the illness. So, for example, at times he distinctly said: 'well, now it is over, all is finished.' Or: 'Here is the end, and nothing.' Or, in a half-delirious, jesting manner: 'well, mother, do not take offense.' And to the thought of the coming of death he, during the whole of this illness, manifested that same calm, that same wise reconciliation with it, which had distinguished him from those very times, some thirty years before, when his religious understanding of life had taken shape. A few days before his death, when we remained alone, he calmly said to me that he might perhaps die of this illness. And he said this in a tone of complete satisfaction and with tears not of suffering or alarm, but of peaceful tenderness in his eyes. When once at night I again sat alone by his bedside, he long, very attentively and affectionately looked at me. I said: 'well, L. N., you are a little better today.' He mumbled in answer something such that I could in no way make it out, but, judging by the touching, childlike expression of his voice and by the tears of tender emotion that came out on his face, I understood that he was speaking not of recovery, but rather of the approach of his own death, and was speaking of it with the very best and brightest feeling. Another time, when he had wakened from a prolonged sleep and our gazes met, he affectionately smiled at me. Then he said: 'Hard is my situation. The fever does not pass.' I said to him: 'Such is the course of the illness. It happens.' He with interest said questioningly: 'Yes?' — And again fell asleep.
>
> How far L. N. in these days was living his inner spiritual life, in spite of those physical sufferings of his which seemed to us so excruciating, was to be seen by certain remarks from time to time uttered by him. So, for example, in the course of the last twenty-four hours of his life he let drop the words: 'well, here is the good of it'; 'all is simple and good'; 'good... yes, yes...' and the like. And he was saying this at such moments when, judging by his difficult breathing, accompanied by hiccup and heavy groans, one might have thought that his body was suffering too much for his consciousness to be able to work freely, and to be experiencing besides satisfaction and good. Evidently, in this time of the dying of his body he was experiencing from his own experience that in which he so unshakably believed and which he never wearied of repeating to others, namely, that man, living by the spirit of God, is capable of drawing for himself good even from the most heavy and excruciating conditions. There is no doubt that the dying sufferings not only did not smother, but, on the contrary, defended and strengthened in L. N. the knowledge of that spiritual principle in which he placed the essence of human life.
>
> The death of L. N. itself came about so quietly, so softly, that it made upon me a pacifying impression.
>
> After uninterrupted hours of heavy breathing, it suddenly was replaced by a shallow and light one. After a few minutes this weak breathing too broke off. An interval of complete silence. No effort, no struggle. Then a scarcely audible deep-deep, long-drawn — last sigh...
>
> Looking upon the husk lying upon the bed of what had been Lev Nikolaevich, I recalled a fragment of the inner work of his soul which I had accidentally overheard the day before. I sat then alone by his bedside. He lay on his back, breathing heavily. Suddenly, evidently continuing aloud the thread of the thoughts occupying him, as though reasoning with himself, he loudly uttered: 'all I... my manifestations... Enough of manifestations... Here it is all...'
>
> I recalled L. N.'s conception of human life as the manifestation of the spirit of God, temporarily enclosed within the bounds of the personality and striving to overcome these bounds in order to merge with the souls of other beings and with God. I felt with especial vividness that life, under such an understanding of it, is a good in nothing to be violated, and that there is no death."

The whole day of 7 November and the whole night into the 8th, the touching scenes of farewell did not cease. The peasants of the neighborhood, the railway employees, and soldiers passing through with troop trains all came in to take leave of the deceased. Through the night the relatives and intimates of the deceased took watch in turn. By one of the railway employees, on the wall, the profile of the deceased was drawn from the shadow cast by the lamp. This profile was afterward carried over to the Astapovo room of the Tolstoy Museum in Moscow and fastened there in its proper place.

On the morning of 8 November there arrived from Moscow two sculptors, and each of them for about an hour took a mask of the deceased. The artist Pasternak made a pastel sketch. The train with the body of L. N. left the station of Astapovo at 1.10 in the afternoon of 8 November.

All the time of the departure, illness, and death of L. N., I was in the town of Kostroma in the zemstvo service. With alarm following the rapidly succeeding events through the newspapers, the letters, and the telegrams of friends, I uneasily hesitated whether to go or to wait calmly for the outcome. But there, on Sunday, 7 November, about midday, there spread through the town the rumor that L. N. had passed away. Then I could not endure and in the evening set out for Moscow. On arriving in Moscow on the morning of the 8th, I asked by telephone where the funeral train could be met, and set out that same day for Tula and Yasenki. From there I went on to Telyatinki to Chertkov's, where I passed the evening and the night, learning all the particulars of the great event. The train with the body was expected the next day early in the morning at Zaseka station, and for the same day the funeral was appointed.

On 9 November, at 6 o'clock in the morning, with several comrades, I set out, still in complete darkness, to the station. It was a frosty morning. The little railway station of Zaseka presented an unprecedented spectacle. The station house was crammed full of people, and around the station a crowd of people had encamped, around bonfires. Everywhere by the fires was heard restrained, quiet talk. From time to time to the bonfires came delegates from the Moscow student body and announced the conditions which it was necessary to observe for the order of the procession. It began to grow light, and then the various groups of delegations began to recognize one another and to come to an understanding about the order of the meeting.

At last, at 8 o'clock in the morning, the train drew up.

They opened the goods-van, and when the coffin became visible, the whole many-thousand crowd bared their heads and sang "Eternal Memory." Then the group of Yasnaya Polyana peasants came up and, taking the coffin upon towels, lifted it, bore it out, and the funeral procession set off down the Yasnaya Polyana road. The girl students and students made a chain, and order was not broken.

Behind the coffin walked the family, the friends, the unbroken crowd of reverent admirers of all ages, classes, nationalities, professions. Far behind the crowd timidly followed police-guards, "just in case"; but no occasion for their intervention presented itself. A special solemnity and a simplicity of grandeur was given by the absence of the clergy and the authorities. Before the coffin Yasnaya Polyana peasants carried, on two poles, a large white ribbon with the inscription "Lev Nikolaevich, the memory of your goodness will not die among us, the orphaned peasants of Yasnaya Polyana." Behind the crowd drove several carts with wreaths. To the sides, on the hillocks, during the procession stood photographers and cinematographers, capturing the chief moments of the funeral. Thus, slowly moving on, the procession about midday reached Yasnaya Polyana, and the coffin was carried into the former working study of L. N. For approximately an hour the doors of the house were closed, to give the relatives the chance to rest from the road and to prepare the place for the public's leave-taking. Then they opened a door on each of the two sides of the house, so that the public, having entered by one door, passed by the open coffin, took leave, and went out through the next door opposite. As soon as the coffin was opened, I stood by the coffin and again saw the face, dear to me, of my great friend, departed into eternity. It is hard to convey in words the agitation I experienced. Some wave of feelings and thoughts caught me up, the tears poured down unrestrainable, and the soul trembled with some solemn alarm, as though from the touch of the great eternity. Having mastered the first agitation, I, standing by the coffin, began to observe those passing, who stopped by the coffin and in one way or another expressed their reverence. There were many touching scenes and tears, and sobs, and religious tender emotion.

For about 3 hours the leave-taking continued. Then the coffin was closed and carried out of the house in the direction of the wood, the Zakaz. Again the heads of the many-thousand crowd were bared, again "Eternal Memory" was drawn out in long tones in the winter forest dusk. The procession stretched out toward the grave, dug at the head of the ravine, in the wood, one verst from the house, just there where, according to legend, had been buried by his brother Nikolai the "Green Stick," the wondrous talisman, the secret of the regeneration of humanity.

When they began to lower the coffin into the grave, complete silence reigned, and the whole crowd sank to its knees. And suddenly amid the wordless silence resounded the sharp blows of frozen earth falling on the lid of the coffin. Again they sang "Eternal Memory," and within half an hour there had grown above the ground a small mound, which hid from us the dust of the dear teacher.

There were no speeches. The moment was too solemn, and no one ventured to break it with the customary funeral word. The dusk thickened, and the crowd quietly dispersed.

The Love and Reason that had illumined this great life had been freed from the husk of personality. And there set in a new epoch of the spreading of the great ideas. Lev Nikolaevich left us an incalculable inheritance.

He who thirsts, let him come and drink.

With these words I conclude my 20-year labor.

My friend, great teacher, accept my humble work and grant me union with you in the spirit of truth! P. Biryukov.

15 December 1922.
