# Volume IV, Chapter 2. 1901. The Excommunication

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-02.md`](../ru/chapter-02.md).

---

The twentieth century began in the life of Russia with a revolutionary movement. The significance of L. N. in this movement is disputed by no one. His criticism of the existing order and his mighty call to the higher truth penetrated the masses and stirred them. The socialists, with their collective organization and discipline, directed this energy into a new state channel, and now, twenty years later, we already see the consolidation in Russia of a revolutionary government.

A new epoch, not only of Russian but of world history, has undoubtedly received a powerful impulse from L. N.'s preaching.

The first signs of these disturbances showed themselves among the students, to whom the workers soon joined themselves.

The winter of this year L. N. spends in Moscow. He often ails, and this hinders him from working. In January there are very few entries in the diary. Let us note one interesting thought, which he records on 12 January:

> "People live by their own thoughts, by others' thoughts, by their own feelings, by others' feelings (that is, by understanding others' feelings and being guided by them). The best man is the one who lives chiefly by his own thoughts and others' feelings; the worst sort is the man who lives by others' thoughts and his own feelings. From the various combinations of these four foundations, these motives of activity, comes all the difference among people. There are people who have almost none — neither their own nor others' thoughts, nor their own feelings — and who live only by others' feelings: these are the self-sacrificing simpletons, the saints. There are people who live only by their own feelings: these are beasts. There are people who live only by their own thoughts: these are the sages, the prophets. There are those who live only by others' thoughts: these are the learned dullards. From the various permutations, according to the strength of these properties, comes all the complex music of characters."

It is interesting to compare this scheme of characters with a similar scheme recorded by him back in 1872. Then L. N. established four elements of character: great intelligence, stupidity, great energy, and apathy, dividing his heroes into characters that combine these properties. If one adds to that scheme the one just cited, then we obtain a whole series of interesting psychological types.

At the beginning of 1901 L. N.'s literary friends were urging him to bring out a special journal, in which he would be the chief contributor and editor. Learning of this, Chertkov wrote him a friendly remonstrance, accusing him of inconsistency.

Nothing came of this attempt. But Chertkov's remonstrance gave L. N. occasion to answer him with an interesting letter of 18 January:

> "I received long ago your long letter with its remonstrance over the attempt at a censored publication.
>
> I so love being remonstrated with that, reading your letter, I entirely agreed with it and felt my own wrongness, and not only did I experience no unpleasant feeling, but, on the contrary, love and gratitude toward you. Then, thinking it over, I agreed with you less: I was very much won over by the fact that it would induce me to write artistic things, which I shall not write without this, and by the fact that the enormous material of an epic character of things, received by me, gathered into a bouquet, might be useful to people. But all the same I am glad that it did not come off, the more so as I seem to have finished, especially for artistic things, and as there would surely have been much that was unpleasant and, as you write, those taking part would involuntarily have been drawn into bad compromises. About life without money I have read and considered everything attentively. I write this to you and to NN, whom I ask to forgive me for not writing separately and in English.
>
> I think that to live without money, for one who feels in himself the strength for this and the possibility (if he is not bound by a family that does not agree), is not only good but obligatory. This is almost the same as the refusal of military service: he who can, let him do it, because it is undoubtedly good; but to demand this, to condemn for the not-doing of it, is impossible. I say almost the same as the refusal of military service, because this can be done by a man who stands yet a step higher in the feeling of his moral consciousness than the one who refuses military service. And that both the one and the other are useful and constitute centres of light from which the rays go far — that is beyond doubt. But I need to repeat that this benefit, these rays, go forth only when the aim is in oneself, in God, and not outside oneself.
>
> I am still ailing and weak. Little by little I am freeing myself from the body. In my soul it is well with me. I am sorry for Lyova, sorry too for Tanya, but I firmly believe that there is no evil, and that what we count as evil is, all the same, good.
> Farewell, dear friends. I kiss you in brotherly love. L. T."

And at this same time L. N. does not abandon his care for the Doukhobors. Those who have resettled in Canada turn to him for various counsels, regarding him as their moral guide. From the very first stages of their life in Canada the Doukhobors had to come up against the demands of the Canadian government, which they were hesitating to carry out. Here is what L. N. answered them:

> 18 January 1901. Moscow.
>
> Dear brothers Vasily Potapov and Ivan Ponomarev. I received your letter of 31 December, but until now I have not answered, because I was unwell. My opinion about those three articles over which you have a disagreement with the Canadian government, my opinion is this.
>
> The first article, about your holding the land in common and not separately, is very important, and, in my view, you must use every effort to obtain an allotment and securing of it not severally for each person, but for the whole community. I think that if there is agreement among you, then in the extreme case one may even accept the land separately, but hold it in common. But if there is no agreement, then even under communal ownership there will be no good of it. You yourselves know, dear brothers, that all the strength lies not in outward affairs but in the inner spiritual state, and therefore most of all, in your new life with all its temptations, you must strive to keep in your community that spirit of Christian life and brotherhood for which you were also driven out of your fatherland, and which is dearer than all the goods of the world.
>
> The remaining two articles, about the registration of the newborn, the dead, and those who marry, seem to me not at all important; one may consent to carry them out, since they are in no way contrary to Christian life.
>
> From Peter Vasilyevich I have had no letters for a very long time now, but I heard of him recently through an acquaintance to whom he wrote. He is alive and well. Having received from Bodyansky a letter about the Yakutsk wives who wish to go to their husbands, I sent this letter to the Sovereign and myself wrote to him, asking him to release the exiles to Canada. To the letter I have so far received no answer at all, and in general I have no hope of success. Write to me what you think about the departure of the wives of the exiles.
> Your brother Lev Tolstoy."

Through L. N. the Canadian Doukhobors maintained their connection with their spiritual leader exiled to Siberia, Peter Verigin. Here is one of those interesting letters, which point to all that significance which fell to L. N.'s lot in the Doukhobor movement.

> 20 January 1901. Moscow.
> To Peter Vasilyevich Verigin.
>
> "Dear brother Peter Vasilyevich. I am forwarding to you, at Bodyansky's wish, his letter to you, concerning you and the brothers living in Canada. I entirely agree with him that, if there does exist among the Doukhobors such a wild superstition by which they ascribe a supernatural significance to your person, then even in view of the benefit that may be drawn from such a superstition, by a beneficial influence upon weak people, one ought not to support it — in which, I am sure beforehand, you too entirely agree — and that, if such a superstition exists, then it exists independently of your will. I disagree with Bodyansky only in this, that he allows an exceptional significance, by the influence they exert, to certain persons. I think that this is not so, and that in a Christian society all are equal and all are taught by one another: the old by the young, the educated by the unlearned, the clever by the dull-witted, and even the virtuous by the dissolute. All are taught by one another, according to the one through whom the spirit of God speaks at the given time. There are no special people: all are sinful, and all can be holy. The information that he (Bodyansky) communicates about the life of the brothers in Canada is, judging by what I hear from those who have come from there, correct, but I think that he is too severe toward them, and that in them the fire of religious service to God by their life does not die out. And if at times it is darkened, then it will surely flare up again with new force. I enclose for you another letter to me, from Ponomarev and Potapov, from which you will see what they are troubled about.
>
> I am very sorry that I have had no news of you for a long time. I wrote, but evidently the route by which I wrote is unreliable.
>
> I and our friends remember you, and I, at least, do not cease trying to persuade the government to release you and the other exiles. The other day I made a new attempt, writing a letter about this to the Sovereign. And I do not know what will come of it. I think nothing. I will go on trying. Farewell; I greet you in brotherly love.
> Lev Tolstoy.
>
> I enclose a letter to the Quakers."

The Russian government, feeling the wavering of its foundations, like a wounded beast, threw itself with malice upon its enemies, often only imagined ones, from which, of course, the innocent suffered. One of such victims was the young man Nakashidze, a student, the brother of Ilya Petrovich Nakashidze, the well-known literary representative of the Tolstoyan movement in the Caucasus. He was then with his brother in Moscow and turned to L. N. for the defence of his brother, who was being subjected to outrageous violence on the part of the Moscow police over some stupid and savage misunderstanding.

L. N. addressed a letter to the then chief of police, afterwards the dictator D. F. Trepov, whom he knew as a friend and comrade of V. G. Chertkov in military service. And notwithstanding the correct tone of this letter, there is felt in it a note indicating that the cup of patience had overflowed even with Lev Nikolaevich:

> "Dmitry Fedorovich.
>
> This letter will be handed to you by Prince Ilya Petrovich Nakashidze, my friend and a man enjoying universal respect and therefore deserving attention to his words. With his brother, a fine youth, pure, moral, never drinking anything, there has happened a terrible, most outrageous affair, the perpetrators of which are police officials.
>
> Judging by what has happened to young Nakashidze, every one of us, inhabitants of Moscow, must constantly feel himself in danger of being disgraced, maimed, and even killed (young Nakashidze is now dangerously ill) by a gang of villains who, under the guise of maintaining order, commit with impunity the most terrible crimes.
>
> I am fully convinced that the crime committed by the police will be taken to heart by you, and that you will deliver us from the necessity of giving this affair the greatest publicity, and will yourself take measures so that all police officials may know that such acts on the part of some of them are not approved of by the higher authorities and must not be repeated.
>
> Please take the trouble to read the description made by the injured man. It bears such a character of truthfulness that, even without knowing the young man personally, one cannot doubt the truth of his testimony.
>
> I wish you all that is good.
> Lev Tolstoy.
>
> Moscow, 24 January 1901."

L. N. continues to ail, works little, but the inner life goes on with especial intensity, and his diary is full of profound and varied thoughts. Now he looks back upon the state structure, and his stern, denunciatory voice is heard:

> "The chief thing is that one must try to destroy the deception, constantly maintained by the government, that everything it does, it does for order, for the welfare of its subjects. Everything it does, it does either for itself (it plunders the conquered), or in order to 'leur donner le change' (*) and assure them that it does this for them."

(* To deceive them. *)

Now he passes over to metaphysics and gives, in a few words, a new and original picture of the relations of the inner and outer life.

> "As the ocean encompasses the terrestrial globe, so our life is encompassed by dreams." More than that — by dreams; it is encompassed by an unconscious, reflexive life. Not only reflexive, but rational, acknowledged by the majority of people as life, yet not having in itself the true properties of life. The true life, the life that is conscious of its divine origin, exists only like rare little islands upon this ocean of unconscious life, which proceeds according to definite, material laws. And only these moments, joining one with another, excluding all that divides them, make up the true life. The rest is sleep.

> "When the materialists say that life is nothing other than physico-chemical processes proceeding according to definite laws, they are entirely right. They consider life objectively, and, considering it thus, they can see nothing else. But they see only that foundation upon which and in which is born and proceeds the true life, not observed by them. When at the same time they speak of inner experience (employed by them for psychology), then this is only a misunderstanding: one cannot observe the observer."

Social life too interests him, and he in passing gives it its due assessment:

> "I read a speech at an agricultural congress. Bombastic, void of content, stupid, self-assured. We all want to help the people; whereas we are the beggars whom it feeds and clothes. What can beggars give to the rich? This must be understood once and for all, and then our relation to the people will be put right. Only stand aside, you beggars who pester it, do not hinder it, as the beggars in Italy do, and it will do everything — and not those stupidities which you propose to it, but that of which you have not even a notion."

The appeal to the Chinese, long since conceived by him, leads him to such thoughts:

> "I thought further that the address to the Chinese ought to be set aside. And to entitle it outright: 'A Godless Age, or the New Fall of Rome.' And to begin outright with an indication of the absence of religion."

Little by little the question of religion fills his consciousness, and he ponders a special treatise on religion.

> "I wanted to begin an article on the absence of religion thus:
>
> It is terrible when one sees the useless sufferings of some people from want, of others from excess; still more terrible to see how these people inevitably corrupt themselves, both by life itself and by upbringing; but most terrible of all is to see that, perishing thus physically and morally, people consider that so it must be, that there is and can be no way out of this position. And we, our Christian society, are in this position."

And in the diary he begins now to sketch out the corresponding thoughts. Thus, he gives the following definition of religion:

> "My definition of religion is this: it is such an establishment of the human relation to infinity by which the aim of one's life is determined."

L. N.'s physical powers were all the while weakening, while his spiritual powers grew, and together with this grew his influence in Russia and abroad. Abroad this influence aroused interest in a society that had lost its faith in its former ideals, while in Russia the attitude toward this growing influence was more serious. The ruling spheres clearly felt that L. N.'s influence was shaking the foundations of their dominion, and they sounded the alarm. State Orthodoxy, as a source of wild, slavish superstition, was of course on its guard, and its protectors, in their impotence, resolved upon an extreme measure, which soon turned against them.

On 22 February the resolution of the Synod was issued, published for general information and beginning with the following introduction:

> "The Most Holy Synod, in its care for the children of the Orthodox Church, for their preservation from a pernicious temptation, and for the salvation of those who go astray, having had under consideration Count Leo Tolstoy and his anti-Christian and anti-ecclesiastical false teaching, has recognized it as timely, in prevention of the disturbance of the peace of the Church, to make public, through printing in the *Church Gazette*, the following epistle."

In this epistle L. N. is declared the author of all manner of heresies, a blasphemer, and so forth, and is recognized as having fallen away from the Orthodox Church. This epistle ends with a prayer for the salvation of the soul of Lev Nikolaevich.

Strictly speaking, from the ecclesiastical point of view this act was entirely logical. But it was tactless from the point of view of the struggle against the influence of Lev Nikolaevich. And indeed the consequences were unexpected.

The epistle was published on 24 February; this was a Sunday. On that day the disturbances of the students broke out, unexpectedly mingling with the general protest against the absurd resolution.

We borrow the description of what took place in Moscow on 24 February from a letter of Sofya Andreyevna to her sister Tatyana Andreyevna Kuzminskaya, who was then living in Kiev, at her husband's place of service:

> "…We are for the present still in Moscow, and have lived through much that was interesting here in these days. After your Kiev students, ours, the Moscow ones, rose up. But not at all as before; the difference is that earlier the students were beaten by the butchers and the people did not sympathize with them. Now the whole people — shop-clerks, cabmen, workers, not to speak of the intelligentsia — all are on the side of the students. The 24th of February was a Sunday, and in Moscow, in the squares and the streets, there stood and roamed crowds of people in their thousands. On that same day, in all the newspapers, the excommunication of Lev Nikolaevich from the Church was printed. More stupidly could not have acted the government which made such a disposition. On that day and on the following ones we received so much sympathy, both by deputations and by letters, addresses, telegrams, baskets of flowers, and so on and so forth. All are indignant, without exception, and all consider the Synod's escapade absurd. But best of all is that on that day, 24 February, Lyovochka happened to go out for a walk, and on Lubyanka Square someone said of him ironically: 'There he is, the devil in human form!' Many turned round, recognized Lev Nikolaevich, and began to cry out to him: 'Hurrah! Lev Nikolaevich! Greetings, L. N.! Hurrah! Hail to the great man!' And everything in this vein.
>
> Lyovochka wanted to drive off in a cab, but they all began to drive away, because the crowd and the cries of 'Hurrah!' were growing stronger. At last some technician brought a cab, they seated Lyovochka, the people caught at the reins and the horse; a mounted gendarme intervened, and so Lyovochka arrived home. The day before yesterday he ailed a little; he had a fever, 38.5; his arms ached, but now it is better, though he has been ailing all winter, now with one thing, now with another, and has grown very thin."

Sofya Andreyevna's indignation soon poured itself out in the form of a letter to Metropolitan Anthony. This letter is very characteristic, and we shall give it here in full, in the redaction which we received personally from the author over her signature.

This letter is a circular one, addressed to all who had signed the excommunication and to the Chief Procurator Pobedonostsev. Whereas the published letter is addressed only to Metropolitan Anthony. Here is the text supplied to us by S. A.:

> "Having read in the newspapers the cruel resolution of the Synod concerning the excommunication of my husband, Count Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy, from the Church, with the signatures of the pastors of the Church, I was unable to remain entirely indifferent to it. There are no bounds to my grievous indignation.
>
> And not from the point of view that from this paper my husband will perish spiritually: that is not the affair of men, but the affair of God. The life of the human soul, from the religious point of view, is known to no one and, happily, is subject to no one. But from the point of view of the Church, to which I belong and from which I shall never depart, which was created by Christ for the blessing in God's name of all the most significant moments of human life — birth, marriages, deaths, the joys and sorrows of men — which ought loudly to proclaim the law of love, of all-forgiveness, of love toward enemies, toward those who hate us, to pray for all — from this point of view the resolution of the Synod is incomprehensible to me.
>
> It will call forth not sympathy, but indignation among people, and a greater love and sympathy for Lev Nikolaevich. Already we are receiving such expressions, and there will be no end to them from all sides of the world.
>
> I cannot but mention also the grief I experienced from that piece of nonsense of which I had heard earlier, namely: of the secret order of the Synod to the priests not to give Lev Nikolaevich a Church funeral in the event of his death. Whom, then, do they wish to punish? The dead man, the man who no longer feels anything, or those around him, the believing people close to him? If this is a threat, then to whom and to what? Can it be that, in order to give my husband a funeral and to pray for him in church, I shall not find either some decent priest who will not fear men before the true God of love, or an indecent one who could be bribed with much money for this purpose?
>
> But I have no need of this. For me the Church is an abstract concept, and as its servants I acknowledge only those who truly understand the significance of the Church.
>
> But if one is to acknowledge as the Church those people who dare by their malice to violate the higher law of Christ's love, then long ago all of us, the truly believing and church-going, would have departed from it.
>
> And guilty of the sinful departures from the Church are not those who have gone astray, the people seeking the truth, but those who have proudly recognized themselves as at its head and, in place of love, humility, and all-forgiveness, have become the spiritual executioners of those whom God will more surely forgive for their humble life, full of renunciation of earthly goods, of love and help to people, though outside the Church, than those who wear diamond mitres and stars but who chastise and excommunicate from the Church its own pastors.
>
> To refute my words by hypocritical arguments is easy. But the deep understanding of the truth and of the real intentions of men will deceive no one.
> Countess Sofya Tolstaya.
>
> 26 February 1901.
>
> Moscow, Khamovnichesky Lane, 21."

Anyone who has read this letter will not doubt its sincerity, notwithstanding the illogicality of its construction. It is indeed the cry of indignation of an offended soul.

Metropolitan Anthony answered Sofya Andreyevna likewise with an open letter, more logical but less sincere, as was to be expected.

Upon Lev Nikolaevich this episode produced a weak impression. Here is what he records in his diary on 19 March:

> "During this time there was the strange excommunication from the Church and the expressions of sympathy called forth by it, and at the same time the student affairs, which took on a public character and made me write an address to the Tsar and his helpers, and a programme. I tried to be guided only by the desire to serve, and not by personal satisfaction. I have not yet sent it. As soon as it is ready, I will send it."

It is characteristic that L. N., after this excommunication, is more interested in public questions having no direct relation to his excommunication, and writes his answer to the excommunication considerably later, a month and a half after the publication of the Synod's epistle. Remaining faithful to the sequence of events, we shall fix our attention on these public events and on L. N.'s attitude toward them, and only then pass on to his answer to the Synod.

In its impotent struggle with the growing revolutionary movement, the government resolved upon an extreme measure and issued a decree on giving over as soldiers the students implicated in revolutionary activity. These so-called "Temporary Regulations" had already begun to come into force and, naturally, called forth acute indignation in the Russian intelligentsia of all parties.

In Petersburg there took place, on 4 March, a great demonstration, which ended in a savage beating of the demonstrators by the police and in numerous arrests. Among those who suffered on Kazan Square were Russian writers too: N. F. Annensky was subjected to beating, and P. B. Struve, M. I. Tugan-Baranovsky, and others to arrest. All these events raised the public mood to a still greater height. On 9 March a general meeting was held of the members of the Union of Russian Writers, at which it was unanimously decided (the protocol of the meeting was signed by 155 writers) to address to the Minister of the Interior a protest against the actions of the authorities on Kazan Square and against the conditions that give rise to such events as took place on 4 March. In answer to the declaration to this effect sent by the Union of Writers to the Minister of the Interior, already on 12 March there came to the committee of the Union, from the Petersburg city-governor Kleigels, a paper of the following content: "By order of the Minister of the Interior, set forth in his proposal of the 12th of this March, No. 2814, the Union of Mutual Aid of Russian Writers is closed, of which I inform the committee for its information and for the corresponding dispositions."

Thus the Union of Writers ended its days. On the occasion of this fact, addressed now to the individual members of the Union (a general meeting was not permitted even for the liquidation of the Union's affairs), greetings began to come in from various parts of Russia with expressions of sympathy for the Union. One of such greetings to the Union, received from Moscow by the now late P. I. Weinberg (he was then chairman of the committee of the Union), ran as follows:

> "With sincere sympathy we have learned of the protest of the Petersburg writers against the bestial acts of the police on 4 March, and of the declaration that followed from the Union of Russian Writers. This declaration entailed the closing of the Union. We think that this closing will be useful rather than harmful to those aims which are dear to Russian writers. By the closing of the Union the administration has acknowledged itself guilty and, being unable to justify its illegal acts, commits yet a new act of violence, thereby still further weakening its own and strengthening the moral influence of the society struggling with it. And therefore we thank you with our whole soul for what you have done, and we hope that your activity, notwithstanding the forcible closing of the Union, will not weaken but will grow stronger and continue in that same direction of freedom and enlightenment in which it has always manifested itself among the best Russian writers."

This greeting, covered with many signatures, was headed by the signature: Lev Tolstoy.

There followed the signatures of Countess S. A. Tolstaya, I. L. Tolstoy, the Krandievskys, Maklakova, Prince D. I. Shakhovskoy, S. A. Skirmunt, and many others.

During this demonstration on Kazan Square one of those present, Prince Vyazemsky, notwithstanding his court rank, indignant at the conduct of the police, took the part of those being beaten and was likewise rudely shoved aside and arrested. Of course, he was soon released, but his act was deemed reprehensible in court circles, and he received a reprimand from the Sovereign.

This circumstance too called forth a public protest, in which L. N. took part. He himself composed a sympathetic address to Prince Vyazemsky and signed it together with other public figures. The text of this address is as follows:

> "Esteemed Prince Leonid Dmitrievich.
>
> Your courageous, noble, and humane conduct on the 4th of March before the Kazan Cathedral is known to all Russia.
>
> We hope that you, just as we do, refer the reprimand you received from the Sovereign for this conduct only to the coarseness and cruelty of those people who deceive him. You did a good deed, and Russian society will always remain grateful to you for it.
>
> You chose to give yourself up to the feeling of indignation against coarse violence and to the demands of humanity, rather than to the conventional demands of propriety and of your position, and your act calls forth the universal respect and gratitude which we hereby express to you by this letter."

However, L. N., in signing these addresses, clearly recognized the worth of these public, and especially student, disturbances, and, while taking the part of the wronged, he did not approve of their conduct… Here is what he records in his diary at this time:

> "People who have in view the people and its welfare, quite needlessly — and I among them — ascribe importance to the students' disturbances. This is, properly speaking, discord among oppressors — between those already ready to be oppressors and those who only as yet wish to be such."

Nonetheless, the continuing oppressions and disorders called L. N. forth to a more energetic action. He writes a letter "to the Tsar and his helpers" and gives it out for the widest possible circulation; on the 25th of March he sends it off to its destination. Besides this "Letter to the Tsar and His Helpers," well known to all, L. N. wrote yet a brief address under the title "What the Russian People Desires Above All." This represents a résumé of the "Letter to the Tsar" and is far less known to the public than the first, and therefore we cite it here in full:

> What the Russian People Desires Above All.
>
> The majority of the Russian people desires above all: first, the abolition of all special laws for the peasant population, namely — the abolition of the land captains, who dispose of the peasants according to their own arbitrary will; the abolition of all the special peasant obligations: the cartage duty, the billeting duty, the village police duty (the *sotskie*, the *desyatskie*), in which the other estates take no part; the abolition of all the special rules concerning the relations of workers to employers; the abolition of joint surety; the abolition of the redemption payments for land already long since redeemed by the peasants, if one reckons the interest they pay at the rate at which it is levied in state institutions. The abolition of the senseless, needless, and only shameful corporal punishment, retained for the peasants.
>
> Secondly, it desires that the rules on the reinforced safeguard be nowhere applied, so that all people, always and everywhere, should be governed by common laws alone. The people desires this because these rules of the reinforced safeguard subject people to the autocratic power of often bad officials, develop spying and denunciation, and encourage and provoke the use against peasants and workers of cruel corporal punishments in cases of land and factory disturbances. Chiefly, they introduce the death penalty — previously abolished, contrary to Christianity, and corrupting to people.
>
> Thirdly, it desires freedom of education and upbringing, that is, that all should have the right to found schools, both lower and higher, that every man not deprived of this right by judicial sentence should have the right to teach, and that the teaching in all schools should be conducted in the languages of those peoples for whom they are established. That all, without distinction of confession and of nationality, should be able to enter and to send their children to all the existing schools; that there should be no exceptions for the books admitted into libraries and reading rooms.
>
> Fourthly, and chiefly, it desires that all laws restraining people in the profession of their faith should be abolished, that the laws should be abolished which punish as a crime the passing over from a faith recognized by the government to another, as well as the unhindered, public profession by each of his own faith; that services in the Old Believers' chapels and churches and meetings in the prayer-houses of the Molokans, the Stundists, and others should not be forbidden. That every believer should be able to profess what he considers the truth, and should be able to bring up his children also in it.
>
> One might desire very much more, but we think that these four measures, were they adopted by the government, would calm the disturbances and would establish the mutual trust between people and government needful for the welfare of all.
>
> 16 March 1901. Moscow."

Certain of L. N.'s friends, sincerely well-disposed toward him, but infected with intelligentsia liberalism, were struck and grieved that in the "Letter to the Tsar and His Helpers" L. N. had not only not put in the foreground, but had not even mentioned, "freedom of speech" and "freedom of the press."

L. N., in a letter to one friend, replied thus to these reproaches:

> "Of freedom of speech I made no mention in the most deliberate manner. The remarks of all the intellectuals that it is necessary to include this only confirm me the more in the necessity of not mentioning it. All four points will be understood by the greyest representative of the 100 millions. As for freedom of the press, it is not only not needful to him, but he will not understand why it is, when they do not even give him the books that are permitted. In general I think that what the people needs above all is that it should not be set apart from others, and all four points treat of this (with the exception of freedom of conscience, which is the foundation of everything and is consciously needed by the people).
>
> I look from below, from the 100 millions, and therefore it is understandable that those who look from above, from the half-million of liberals and revolutionaries, see something else.
>
> If freedom of speech, then freedom of assembly, representation, and the whole catechism, the fulfilment of which gives nothing but the imagination that people are free. Now the people may desire that it should not be set apart from all the rest; afterwards, if it shall desire anything, then above all the freeing of the land from property, then from the taxes laid on by someone, then from soldiering, then from the law-court, and not freedom of the press, representation, the eight-hour working day, funds, and the like."

At last L. N. proceeds to the answer to the resolution of the Synod, compelled to it by circumstances. He explains the motives of his answer thus:

> "At first I did not wish to answer the resolution of the Synod concerning me, but this resolution called forth very many letters, in which correspondents unknown to me — some abuse me for rejecting what I do not reject, others exhort me to believe in that in which I have never ceased to believe, and a third sort express a like-mindedness with me which in reality scarcely exists, and a sympathy to which I scarcely have a right; and I have resolved to answer both the resolution itself, indicating what is unjust in it, and the appeals to me of my unknown correspondents."

In his answer he convicts the authors of the epistle of a whole series of absurdities and corrects the sense of their attack, indicating wherein their accusation is correct and wherein it is not. And the very correctness of the accusation he turns against them, since with new force he affirms and explains those thoughts for which he is subjected to condemnation.

At last, with new force, he sets forth in brief his belief, expanding it to the dimensions of a universal religion of truth. Here is his conclusion:

> "I believe in the following: I believe in God, whom I understand as Spirit, as love, as the Beginning of all. I believe that He is in me and I in Him. I believe that the will of God is most clearly and intelligibly expressed in the teaching of the man Christ, whom to understand as God and to whom to pray I consider the greatest blasphemy. I believe that the true good of man lies in the fulfilment of the will of God, and that His will is that people should love one another and consequently should do to others as they wish that others should do to them, as is also said in the Gospel, that in this is the whole law and the prophets. I believe that the meaning of every man's life lies therefore only in the increase of love within himself; that this increase of love leads the individual man, in this life, to ever greater and greater good, gives after death a good the greater the more love there shall be in the man, and at the same time, more than anything else, furthers the establishment in the world of the Kingdom of God, that is, of such an order of life in which the discord, deceit, and violence that now reign will be replaced by free agreement, truth, and the brotherly love of people among themselves. I believe that for the attainment of success in love there is only one means — prayer; not public prayer in the temples, directly forbidden by Christ (Matt. VI, 5–13), but the prayer of which the model was given us by Christ — solitary prayer, consisting in the restoration and strengthening in one's consciousness of the meaning of one's life and of one's dependence on the will of God alone.
>
> If these beliefs of mine offend, grieve, or tempt anyone, hinder anything or anyone, or are displeasing — I can change them as little as I can change my body. I have to live alone, myself, and to die alone, myself (and very soon), and therefore I cannot in any way believe otherwise than as I believe, preparing to go to that God from whom I came forth. I do not believe that my faith is undoubtedly true for all time, but I see no other, simpler, clearer, and more answering to all the demands of my mind and heart. If I learn of such a one, I will at once accept it, because God needs nothing but the truth. But to return to that from which, with such sufferings, I have only just come out, I can in no way do, as a flying bird cannot enter into the shell of the egg from which it came forth.
>
> 'He who begins by loving Christianity more than truth will very soon love his own Church or sect more than Christianity, and will end by loving himself (his own peace of mind) more than anything in the world,' said Coleridge.
>
> I have gone the reverse way. I began by loving my Orthodox faith more than my peace of mind, then I loved Christianity more than my Church, and now I love truth more than anything in the world. And so far truth coincides for me with Christianity, as I understand it. And I profess this Christianity; and in the measure in which I profess it, I live peacefully and joyfully, and peacefully and joyfully draw near to death."

Out of the letters and telegrams expressing sympathy for or censure of L. N. on the occasion of the Synod's resolution, a whole literature was formed. At our disposal there are about a hundred such expressions of sympathy and censure, and we shall try to give a brief survey of these documents, which vividly illuminate the attitude toward L.
