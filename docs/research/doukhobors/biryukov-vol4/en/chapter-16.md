# Volume IV, Chapter 16. 1910. The Tragedy of Life at Yasnaya Polyana

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-16.md`](../ru/chapter-16.md).

---

In January of this year L. N. continued chiefly to occupy himself with the compilation of the collection *For Every Day*.

But this practical work did not disturb the growth of his spiritual life, and this spiritual growth of his was reflected in the entries of his diary, which were becoming ever deeper, clearer, and wiser. Thus, on 13 January he writes:

> "It is not anarchism, the teaching by which I live, but the fulfillment of an eternal law that admits of no violence and no participation in it. As to the consequences — whether they will be anarchism or, on the contrary, slavery under the yoke of the Japanese or the German — this I do not know and do not wish to know."

On the same day he writes an interesting letter to Professor Totomyants, answering his inquiry as to what his opinion of cooperation was. Here is the essential part of his letter:

> "You suppose quite correctly that the cooperative movement cannot but be sympathetic to me. Although I continue, and shall never cease, to think and to say that the only radical means capable of destroying the existing evil of struggle, of violence, and of the oppression of the majority of the people by the non-laboring estates — is the renewal of the religious consciousness of the people, I cannot but acknowledge also that cooperative activity — the establishment of cooperatives, participation in them — is the only social activity in which, in our time, a moral, self-respecting man, not wishing to be a participant in violence, can take part.
>
> I acknowledge also that cooperation can relieve the need of the laboring people, which has of late reached an extreme degree. I do not think, however, as some do think, that the cooperative movement could call forth or establish a religious attitude of people toward the questions of life. I think, on the contrary, that only the rise of religious consciousness can give a durable and fruitful character to the cooperative movement.
>
> In any case, I think that in our time this is one of the best activities to which the young people seeking an application of their powers, wishing to serve the people — and there are so many of them — can devote themselves. Were I young, I should take up this work; but now I do not despair of trying to do what I can among the peasantry close to me."

A few days later L. N. goes to a court session in Tula and afterward records in his diary his impressions of that journey:

> "I woke up vigorous and resolved to go to Tula to the court. I read the letters and answered a few and set off. First, the trial of peasants: lawyers, judges, soldiers, witnesses — all very new to me. Then the trial of a political prisoner: the accusation was that he had read and disseminated, with self-sacrifice, more just and sound thoughts about the ordering of life than the one that exists. Very sorry for him. The people gathered to look at me, but, thank God, not many. The oath agitated me. I barely restrained myself from saying that this is a mockery of Christ. My heart contracted, and that is why I kept silent."

We give further the account of L. N.'s secretary, V. F. Bulgakov, who had replaced Gusev. In his notes he gives an interesting and characteristic picture of a conversation at the evening tea table. I myself often had occasion to be a witness of such talks. The conversation given below took place in the last year of his life. How clearly it points out to us the tragedy of L. N.'s soul, and how clearly it indicates the causes of his departure. Here is this account:

> "At table there was struck up an interesting, animated conversation: about patriotism, about the superiority of abroad over Russia, and, finally, about the land and about the landowners and peasants. To this theme, as I had had occasion to notice, the conversation in the large dining room of the white Yasnaya Polyana house usually came round. They talked much and long, disputed passionately and stubbornly. A part of those disputing noted the extreme embitterment of the peasants against the landowners and the gentry in general.
>
> — The Russian muzhik is a coward, — objected Andrei Lvovich. — I myself saw, before my own eyes, five dragoons flog in turn a village of four hundred households.
>
> — The peasants are drunkards, — says Sofya Andreyevna. — The army costs as much as is spent on drink — this has been proved by statistics. They are by no means in want because they have too little land.
>
> Tolstoy came in. The conversation had been about to die down, but for no more than half a minute. L. N. sat at the table frowning and listened.
>
> — If the peasants had the land, — he uttered quietly, but in a very firm voice, — there would not be here these stupid flower-beds, — and with a contemptuous gesture he pointed at the basket adorning the table, with its beautiful fragrant hyacinths. No one said anything.
>
> — There would not be such stupid trifles, — L. N. continued, — and there would not be such foolish people who pay a lackey ten rubles a month.
>
> — Fifteen, — they corrected Lev Nikolaevich.
>
> — Well, fifteen...
>
> — The landowners are the most unfortunate of people, — they continued to object to L. N. — Is it the impoverished landowners who buy such gramophones and the rest? Not at all. They are bought by the merchants, the capitalists, who have robbed the people...
>
> — So what do you mean to say, — uttered Tolstoy, — that we are less scoundrels than they? — And he burst out laughing.
>
> Everyone laughed. L. N. asked Dušan to bring a letter he had received a few days before, and read it.
>
> In this letter there was said approximately the following:
>
> 'No, L. N., I cannot in any way agree with you that human relations will be set right by love alone. Only people well brought up and always well-fed can talk like that. But what is one to say to a man hungry from childhood and suffering all his life under the yoke of tyrants? He will struggle against them and will try to free himself from slavery. And here, just before your very death, I tell you, L. N., that the world will yet choke in blood, that more than once they will beat and slaughter not only the gentry, sparing neither men nor women, but their little children too, so that he may expect no harm from them either. I am sorry that you will not live to see that time, so as to convince yourself with your own eyes of your error. I wish you a happy death.'
>
> The letter made a strong impression on everyone. Andrei Lvovich lowered his head low to his glass of tea and was silent; Sofya Andreyevna decided that if the letter was from Siberia, then it had been written by an exile, and if an exile, then that meant a brigand.
>
> — Otherwise he would not have been exiled, — it was explained at this point.
>
> They tried to dissuade her, but in vain."

At the end of January, in the village of Yasnaya Polyana, a people's library named after L. N. Tolstoy was opened at the initiative of the Moscow Literacy Society. As a delegate from the society there appeared Prince N. D. Dolgorukov with a correspondent and a photographer. Upon L. N. the ceremony of the opening, as always, produced an unfavorable impression.

Apropos of this event he makes a brief entry in his diary:

> "30 January. In the evening: Dolgorukov with the library."

On the next day: "The correspondent and photographer arrived... Then I had to go to the library. All very contrived, unnecessary, false. Muzhiks, photography."

After the opening of the library L. N. with Dušan rode out on horseback, and the photographer who was there at the time took a picture of them both on their horses.

At this same time, in February, L. N. received an interesting and sincere letter, which agitated him greatly, from a student of Kiev University, Boris Manzhos. In this letter Manzhos implores L. N. to complete his apostolic feat, to leave his home and go forth as a beggar to wander and to preach the gospel. In the letter there were also other requests and pieces of advice, which L. N. did not need, since he had long since done that which his correspondent was advising him to do. But the sincerity and heartfelt warmth of the letter prompted L. N. to answer it. Here is what he wrote to him:

> "Your letter touched me deeply. That which you advise me to do constitutes my cherished dream, but up to now I have not been able to do it. There are many reasons for this (but by no means the one that I should be sparing myself), and the chief one is that this must be done in no wise in order to act upon others. That is not in our power, and it is not that which should guide our activity. It can and should be done only when it shall be necessary not for any supposed external aims, but for the satisfaction of an inner demand of the soul, when to remain in one's former position shall become as morally impossible as it is physically impossible not to cough when one's breath is gone. And to such a position I am near, and with each day I grow nearer and nearer.
>
> That which you advise me to do — to renounce my social position, my property, and to distribute it to those who consider themselves entitled to count upon it after my death — was done already more than 25 years ago. But one thing — that I live with my wife and daughter in shameful conditions of luxury amid the poverty surrounding me — torments me without ceasing, more and more, and there is not a day that I do not think of fulfilling your advice.
>
> Very, very grateful to you for your letter. This letter of mine will be known to only one person. I beg you, likewise, to show it to no one."

One may with great probability think that this letter was one of the drops that tipped the scale of the balance toward departure.

In the month of March there appeared in print the well-known article by Vlad. Galakt. Korolenko, "An Everyday Occurrence." A cry of the soul against the cases of capital punishment that had then become more frequent. Upon Lev Nikolaevich this article produced a deep impression, and he at once wrote a letter to the author of this article:

> "Vladimir Galaktionovich. I have just heard read your article on capital punishment, and all the while during the reading I tried, but could not restrain — not tears, but sobbing. I find no words to express to you my gratitude and love for this article, superb both in expression and in thought, and, chiefly, in feeling. It must be reprinted and disseminated in millions of copies. No Duma speeches, no treatises, no dramas, no novels will produce one-thousandth of the beneficial effect which this article ought to produce.
>
> It ought to produce this effect because it calls forth such a feeling of compassion for that which these victims of human madness have lived through and are living through, that involuntarily one forgives them, whatever their deeds may have been.
>
> Besides all these feelings, your article cannot but call forth yet another feeling too, which I experience in the highest degree — the feeling of pity not for the killed alone, but also for those who commit these horrors...
>
> One thing rejoices me — that such an article as yours unites many living, undepraved people by one ideal common to all, of good and of truth, which, whatever its enemies may do, flares up ever brighter and brighter."

From that time forward there was established between L. N. and V. G. Korolenko a sincere, kindly relation.

Among all the phenomena of life surrounding L. N., the dearest of all to him remained the life of the laboring and especially of the peasant people. And so in March he records in his diary a thought reflecting his state of soul:

> "Life for the muzhik is, before all else, labor, which gives the possibility of continuing life not only for himself, but for his family and for other people too. Life for the intellectual is the assimilation of those branches of knowledge or of art which are considered important in their circle, and, by means of this knowledge, the making use of the labors of the muzhik. How then can the muzhik's understanding of life and of its questions fail to be reasonable, and the intellectual's understanding of life fail to be senseless?"

As an illustration of this thought there may serve the tale of "Ivan the Fool," written by L. N. as far back as 1885. Not for nothing did this tale endure a long-lasting boycott on the part of the intelligentsia, a boycott that extended to many of L. N.'s other works as well.

One of the following entries speaks still more strongly of those sufferings which L. N. experienced in feeling the sin of the privileged estate before the laboring one:

> "Did not dine. A tormenting anguish from the consciousness of the vileness of my life amid those who work in order, with the greatest difficulty, to escape a cold and hungry death, to save themselves and their family. Yesterday fifteen people gorge themselves on pancakes; some five or six family people run about, barely managing in time to prepare and serve the food. Tormentingly shameful, terrible. Yesterday I rode past men breaking stone — it was just as if I had been driven through the gauntlet. Yes, heavy and tormenting is want, and envy and malice toward the rich; but I do not know whether the shame of my life is not still more tormenting."

On 21 April Lev Nikolaevich was visited by the then still young, but already famous, writer Leonid Andreyev. L. N. received him very cordially, walked with him, talked much, both before others and alone with him. Andreyev spent the night at Yasnaya and in the morning made ready to leave. Here is how Bulgakov describes their parting:

> "Andreyev, agitated, was thanking L. N. L. N. asked him to come again.
>
> — Let us be closer, — he uttered, and then added: — allow me to kiss you.
>
> And he himself was the first to reach out toward the young fellow-writer.
>
> Having stopped in the drawing room, I was an involuntary witness of this scene.
>
> When Andreyev and I went out, I saw how strongly the parting with L. N. had agitated him.
>
> — Tell Lev Nikolaevich, — he said in a breaking voice, as we descended the staircase, turning his agitated face toward me and barely looking at the steps, — tell him that I... was happy, that he... is so kind...
>
> He took his seat in the carriage, took up a small suitcase and a photographic apparatus, and, sped on his way by our farewell wishes, drove off."

On 2 May L. N. went to stay with his elder daughter Tatyana Lvovna at her estate of Kochety. He remained there until 20 May, resting in soul and in body from the bustling life at Yasnaya Polyana, where he was overwhelmed by visitors and where he was troubled by those unnatural relations with those around him which arose out of his principled denial of property, his juridical non-ownership of it, and his actual use of it through his family.

On arriving there L. N. writes to S. A. at Yasnaya:

> "I am writing to you, dear Sonya, in order to inform you of myself in person. I traveled splendidly, and here I cannot believe the reality — that one can come out onto the porch without meeting some eight visitors of every sort, before whom it is painful and shameful, and two or three visitors, very good ones, it is true, but requiring an effort of thought and attention; and that one can then go into a wonderful park and, on returning, again meet no one except the dear little Tanyas and the dear Mikh. Serg. It is just like a magic dream. My health is good; the second day there is no heartburn. How are you? Have you rested? With whom will you come? Tell Andryusha and Katya that I am sorry they did not find me. I am now going to bed. Tanya is so solicitous that one only wants to restrain her. I walk in the park; I have written nothing serious. I kiss you."

An external event during L. N.'s stay at Kochety was the arrival there of V. G. Chertkov, who had been forbidden entry into Tula Province.

V. G. summoned thither his photographer with a photographic and cinematographic apparatus. And L. N.'s stay was marked by many photographs.

V. Gr. gives an interesting conversation with L. N. apropos of this taking of photographs:

> "On 20 May, before breakfast, L. N., putting away his diary, showed me what he had just written and said, smiling:
>
> 'You see, just now I had written that they took photographs of me yesterday and that this was unpleasant to me; but then I struck out the last words for your sake.'
>
> I: 'What was it, then, that was unpleasant to you?'
>
> L. N.: 'The thought of the widespread circulation of my portraits.'
>
> I: 'And not the fact that the photographing itself is unpleasant and tiresome?'
>
> L. N.: 'No, not at all. But the unbefitting significance that is attached to my portraits.'
>
> I: 'That is understandable on your part. But we have in view all those to whom, lacking the possibility of seeing you yourself, it is dear to see at least your likeness.'
>
> L. N.: 'It only seems to you that there are such people. You and I shall never agree about this — about the unbecoming significance which you ascribe to my person.'"

Of course, even at Kochety L. N. did not escape visitors.

Of L. N.'s literary works at Kochety we shall point to the comedy *Every Quality Comes from Her*, the preface to *The Path of Life*, and the long letter "On Suicide."

At this time L. N. was reading the works on religion of the French scholar Réville. Besides this, L. N. made several remarkable entries in his diary. These entries so clearly depict the condition of the so-called civilized world that we consider it necessary to give here the most important of them:

On 10 May he records thus:

> "It goes without saying that men could not spoil human life, could not make of a life that is in its essence good a bad human life. They could do only that which they have done: temporarily spoil the life of the present generations, but in exchange have involuntarily introduced into life that which will move it more swiftly forward. If they have done and are doing the greatest evil by their irreligious depraving of men, they involuntarily, by their inventions, harmful to themselves and to their generations, bring in that which unites all men. They deprave men, but they deprave all: the Hindus, and the Chinese, and the Negroes — all. Medieval theology or Roman depravity depraved only their own peoples, a small part of humanity; now electricity, the railways, the telegraph deprave all. All assimilate, cannot but assimilate, all this, and all alike suffer, are alike compelled to change their life, are all set under the necessity of changing in their life the chief thing: their understanding of life, their religion.
>
> Machines — to make what? Telegraphs — to transmit what? Schools, universities, academies — to teach what? Assemblies — to discuss what? Books, newspapers — to spread information about what? Railways — for whom and whither to travel? Millions of people gathered together and subjected to one authority — in order to do what? Hospitals, doctors, pharmacies — in order to prolong life; and to prolong life for what?
>
> Millions suffer bodily and spiritually only in order to seize power, in order that they may deprave themselves unhindered. For this — the lie of religion, the lie of science, stupefaction, drink, and education, and where this is not enough — crude violence, prisons, executions."

Two days later he writes:

> "How easily that which is called civilization, true civilization, is assimilated, both by separate individuals and by peoples. To pass through the universities, to clean one's nails, to avail oneself of the services of a tailor and a barber, to make a trip abroad — and the most civilized man is ready. And for peoples: more railways, academies, factories, dreadnoughts, fortresses, newspapers, books, parties, parliaments — and the most civilized people is ready. That is why both individuals and peoples grasp at civilization and not at enlightenment. The first is easy, requires no effort, and calls forth approval; the second, on the contrary, requires strained effort, and not only calls forth no approval, but is always despised, hated by the majority, because it lays bare the lie of civilization."

And, finally, on the day of his departure he records:

> "A prayer I have loved of late: Lord, help me to live independently of human judgment, only before Thee and through Thee."

On 20 May L. N. with all his companions set off for Yasnaya Polyana, stopping on the way from Kochety at the station to visit his friend Kh. N. Abrikosov.

At Yasnaya Polyana there were all the same heavy conditions of life for L. N. The brief notes of his diary say much:

> "4 June. Rode out with Dušan. Had a good ride. Returned and found a Circassian who had brought Prokofy. It became terribly hard; I thought outright of going away. And even now, today at five in the morning, I do not consider this impossible."

> "6 June. And again the same state of sadness and self-pity. Went to the Zakaz. Met a lad; he asks whether one may walk there, for the Circassian beats people. And it became so hard."

The "Circassians" were the watchmen hired by Sofya Andreyevna for the guarding of Yasnaya Polyana. They caused Lev Nikolaevich much grief. He is already thinking of going away and is becoming confirmed in this intention.

On 12 June L. N. sets off to visit Chertkov, who still could not live in Tula Province and was living at Meshcherskoye, near the station of Stolbovaya, on the Moscow–Kursk railway.

Lev Nikolaevich passed his time at the Chertkovs' very animatedly, receiving many visitors and himself visiting the neighbors, in this case two psychiatric clinics, one at Meshcherskoye, the other at Pokrovskoye; he was present there at a cinematographic showing and conversed with interest with the patients.

L. N. was writing at this time the letter "To the Slavs" and the story "By Accident," gave a final working-over to the comedy *Every Quality Comes from Her*, and continued also to work on the little books *The Path of Life*. On 22 June Lev Nikolaevich wrote to Sofya Andreyevna:

> "In three days I shall be with you, dear Sonya, but all the same I want to write a word or two. The letter I wrote you got delayed by mistake, and you, no doubt, have only just received it. Since then all has continued to go well with us. Yesterday Berkenheim was here. He listened to Sasha and said that she may look upon herself as healthy. He advises bathing. Although I do not believe doctors, this was pleasant to me. I too am well. Yesterday I was even unusually well — I worked much, both at Iv. Iv.'s little books, and at yet another empty little story of that meeting and conversation with a young peasant. Yesterday, too, a multitude of people gathered: Strakhov, Buturlin, the *skopets* from Kochety, Berkenheim, Orlenev (dressed like a human being), two workmen — they had been at Yasnaya, from Moscow — and someone else besides. And it was easy for me, because I was perfectly well.
>
> In the evening I drove to Troitskoye, to the district hospital for the mentally ill, to a magnificent cinematographic showing. The doctors are all very kind. But the cinematograph in general does not please me, and I am sorry both for Sasha, who had a migraine, and for myself, who sat there less than an hour and drove away. This was about ten o'clock in the evening. Today, just as I went out at eight to walk — the first encounter, Alexander Petrovich with a bundle. I was glad of him especially because he told me about you what he could know. And even that is good. Today nothing is in prospect, and I sit at home, work, and rest. Perhaps I shall ride out on horseback with Chertkov, who is accompanying me.
>
> How are you? I hope there have been no new unpleasantnesses. And if there have been, then that you bore them calmly, as far as you were able. You have two occupations which engage you and in which you are mistress. These are your edition and your notes. I kiss you, dear friend. Greetings to Varya and Kolechka.
>
> All the dealings I have had with the common people here have been very pleasant. They are gentler than ours and more well-bred. Two days ago I drove to the village where the convalescing patients are lodged with the peasants. And the first person, a peasant, meets me with the words: 'Good day, Lev Nikolaevich.' It turns out that twelve years ago he had been at my house in Moscow, had entered our 'Temperance Society,' and from that time had not drunk. He lives in plenty. He led me off to see his library — hundreds of books, in which he takes pride and rejoices. Well, until our soon meeting."

This letter is remarkable for the friendly tone in which Lev Nikolaevich strove to speak with Sofya Andreyevna. Unfortunately, not all of the people close to L. N. supported this tone.

On the evening of that same day, that is, of 22 June, there was received from Yasnaya Polyana a telegram summoning Lev Nikolaevich home on account of the illness of Sofya Andreyevna. L. N. was able to set out only on the next day, the evening of the 23rd.

With this return to Yasnaya Polyana there began for L. N. the last, perhaps the heaviest, period of his life, which prepared, or, in any case, hastened, his end.

The subsequent circumstances are so complex and unusual that the biographer halts in perplexity, holding that the explanation of these events can be given only by history, which will gather all things together, will sift out the unnecessary, and will leave us the pure truth. But we, taking part in this whirlwind of events, are not in a position to weigh them from the outside, and shall content ourselves with setting forth the events known to us in their chronological order, with such accuracy as is accessible to us, leaving the judgment to posterity.

The matter was this — that Sofya Andreyevna's illness proved to be a mental one, on the ground of hysteria; and with this it is for the most part impossible to draw the line between normal sensitivity and morbid excitation. With this, too, actual facts are often exaggerated to monstrous dimensions, and the measure of truth is lost.

Perhaps, under other, calmer circumstances the illness would have passed of itself; but in the life of L. N. and of those around him, when there were setting in, for all to see, the clear signs of his already not-distant end, a new and menacing phenomenon arose. He was leaving an enormous literary heritage, both material and spiritual. "And they parted his garments, and for his vesture they did cast lots," said the prophet. So it has always been, so it was now too. Around this heritage a struggle arose even in L. N.'s lifetime, and continues to this day. The spiritual part of the literary heritage, that is, the dissemination of his ideas, L. N. entrusted to V. G. Chertkov. But for this it was necessary to safeguard this heritage from the claims of the family, which was counting on its material advantages, whereas L. N.'s wish was to grant to all the right of making use of his works. Sofya Andreyevna felt that not only the ideal but also the material part of the heritage was slipping away from her; she considered V. G. Chertkov the chief author of this and hated him with all her soul. She was jealous of Chertkov on Lev Nikolaevich's account, and in her disordered brain this jealousy took on monstrous dimensions.

At this time I was living with my family in Kostroma Province and, from time to time, when my zemstvo service permitted, would visit Lev Nikolaevich. That summer I had a wish to go to Yasnaya with my whole family, in order to support in my growing children the fascination of the personality of Lev Nikolaevich, who, as everyone felt, was departing from us. I wrote Lev Nikolaevich a letter with an inquiry as to whether my visit would be convenient, and received the following answer:

19 July 1910.

> "Dear, dear Posha. So joyful to receive your letter. Heart, after all, sends tidings to heart. You are as dear to me as I am to you. Mar. Al. has had a fire, but she bore it — chiefly the loss of her manuscripts — as is proper to a man living the spiritual life. One must learn from her. Everyone loves her, and all are ready to help. I shall pass on your words to her. With me it is worse than a fire. S. An. is agitated, irritated, almost mentally ill — hatred of Chertkov, jealousy of him, and it is very hard for me. But I feel that this too is according to my deeds and to my profit. By all means come, all of you, with your family. I think that S. A. will be glad to receive you, though in her condition nothing can be foreseen. And if not at her place, then at the Chertkovs', at the Nikolaevs'. Why, Sasha and I will do all we can to settle you with your family. It is such a joy to me to be with you.
>
> Yes, children — a great question. There is the place for non-doing: not to do what is harmful.
>
> Until we meet, please. The sooner, the better. Greetings to your wife.
> L. T."

Of course, after this letter we began intensively to make ready for the road.

Meanwhile, at Yasnaya, events went on in their usual course. One of the occasions of Sofya Andreyevna's distress was L. N.'s diaries, which were temporarily kept at Chertkov's, while she demanded their return to be kept at home, with herself.

Valentin Fyodorovich Bulgakov relates in his unpublished notes certain episodes of this struggle:

> "Setting off to Telyatinki to hear Fetler, and knowing that I too was intending to go that day to the Chertkovs', S. A. proposed to drive me there. We went by a roundabout way, by the high road, in order to avoid a bad bridge in the ravine on the nearest road. And so S. A. wept the whole way, was pitiful in the extreme, and implored me to tell Chertkov to hand over to her the manuscripts of L. N.'s diaries. 'Let them all be copied out, transcribed, but let me be given only L. N.'s original manuscripts. Why, his earlier diaries are kept by me... Tell Chertkov that if he gives me the diaries, I shall grow calm. I shall then return to him my favor; he will visit us as before, and we shall work together for Lev Nikolaevich and serve him... You will tell him this? For God's sake, tell him.'
>
> S. A., all in tears, trembling, looked at me imploringly: her tears and her agitation were of the most unfeigned...
>
> And so it always seems to me that, had Chertkov then and there given her those wretched manuscripts — needed by no one in comparison with the man we all might lose in this struggle — had he given them up, this would have altered the whole course of events at Yasnaya Polyana. S. A. would either have grown finally calm, or would have grown calm to such a degree that, at the least, her irritation would have ceased to grow, to progress.
>
> This time the diaries were not handed over to S. A. V. G. had as yet no direct instruction from L. N. to this effect. As far as one recalls, she was given to hope that the diaries would be handed over to her after they had been copied out. S. A. was agitated as before and made scenes for L. N., continuing to demand the diaries. A few days later the diaries had after all to be taken from Chertkov, but they were not given to Sofya Andreyevna; instead they were carried off by Tatyana Lvovna for safekeeping to the Tula bank."

This decision of L. N.'s to take the diaries from Chertkov and place them for safekeeping in the bank was adopted by him, of course, after a whole series of tormenting scenes, which compelled him to write Sofya Andreyevna a long letter, which excellently explains to us L. N.'s attitude toward all that was taking place at Yasnaya Polyana, and so we give it in full. This letter was copied out by me, with Sofya Andreyevna's permission, from the original kept by her, and is dated 14 July 1910; that is, it was written two days after the episode described by Bulgakov, and so this letter may be considered an immediate consequence of that episode. Here is this letter, which has the character of a compact.

14 July 1910.

> "1) The present diary I shall give to no one; I shall keep it with myself.
>
> 2) The old diaries I shall take from Chertkov and shall keep them myself, probably in the bank.
>
> 3) If you are troubled by the thought that my diaries — all the places in which I write, under the impression of the moment, about our disagreements and clashes, that these places might be made use of by future biographers ill-disposed toward you — then, not to speak of the fact that such expressions of temporary feelings, in my diaries as in yours, can in no wise give a correct notion of our real relations — if you fear this, then I am glad of the occasion to express in my diary, or simply as if in a letter, my attitude toward you and my appraisal of your life.
>
> My attitude toward you and my appraisal of you are these: as I loved you in my youth, so, despite the various causes of cooling, I have not ceased to love and do love you. These causes of cooling were (not to speak of conjugal relations — such a cessation could only do away with the deceptive expressions of an unreal love), in the first place, my ever-greater and greater withdrawal from the interests of worldly life and my aversion to them, whereas you did not wish, and could not, part with them, not having in your soul those foundations which led me to my convictions, which is very natural, and I do not reproach you. This — in the first place. In the second place (forgive me if what I shall say is unpleasant to you, but what is now taking place between us is so important that one must not fear to speak out and to hear out the whole truth) — your character in the last years grew more and more irritable, despotic, and unrestrained. The manifestations of these traits of character could not but cool — not the feeling itself, but the expression of it. This — in the second place. In the third place — the chief cause was the fatal one, that in which neither I nor you is to blame — our wholly opposite understanding of the meaning and aim of life. Everything in our understandings of life was opposite — the manner of life, the attitude toward people and toward the means of life — property, which I considered a sin, while you considered it a necessary condition of life. In my manner of life, in order not to part with you, I submitted to conditions of life that were hard for me, while you took this for concessions to my views, and the misunderstanding between us grew greater and greater. There were other causes of cooling too, for which we were both to blame, but I shall not speak of them, because they do not bear on the matter. The matter is that I, despite all the misunderstandings that there have been, have not ceased to love and to value you. As for my appraisal of my life with you, it is this: I, a depraved man, deeply vicious in the sexual respect, no longer in my first youth, married you, a pure, good, intelligent girl of eighteen, and, despite this dirty, vicious past of mine, you lived with me almost fifty years, loving me, in a life of toil, a hard life, bearing, nursing, rearing, and tending the children and me, not yielding to those temptations which could so easily seize any woman in your position — strong, healthy, beautiful. But you lived in such a way that I cannot reproach you with anything. As for the fact that you did not follow me in my exceptional spiritual movement — for this I cannot reproach you and do not reproach you, because the spiritual life of every man is the secret of that man with God, and to require anything of him is not for other men to do. And if I did require it of you, then I was in error and am to blame for this.
>
> So there is the true description of my attitude toward you and my appraisal of you. And as for what may find its way into the diaries (I know only that nothing sharp, nothing contrary to what I am now writing, will be found there) — so this is 3) about that which may, and ought not, to trouble you — about the diaries. 4) This is that, if at the given moment my relations with Chertkov are hard for you, then I am ready not to see him, though I shall say that this is unpleasant not so much for me as for him, knowing how hard this will be for him. But if you wish — I shall do it. Now 5) this — that if you do not accept these conditions of mine, born of a good moment of life, then I take back my promise not to go away from you; I shall go away. I shall go away, certainly not to Chertkov; I shall even make it an indispensable condition that he should not come to live near me; but I shall go away without fail, because to live any longer as we are living now is impossible.
>
> I could go on living so, if I could calmly bear your sufferings, but I cannot. Yesterday you went away agitated, suffering. I wished to lie down to sleep — I did not sleep; I wished not so much to think as to feel you, and I did not sleep, and listened until one, until two o'clock, and again woke and listened, and saw you in my sleep. Think it over calmly, dear friend; listen to your heart, feel, and you will decide everything as it ought to be. As for myself, I shall say that I, for my part, have decided after all that otherwise I cannot, could not. Cease, my dove, to torment not others, but yourself, yourself, because you suffer a hundred times more than all. There, that is all."

On that same day L. N., in accordance with his "compact," charged Alexandra Lvovna to take the diaries from V. G. Chertkov. Of course, they were immediately returned. Likewise, in accordance with his intention, L. N. sent these diaries off for safekeeping to the bank.

V. G. Chertkov, in his letter of 16 July, advises L. N., for the calming of S. A., to take the diaries out of the bank and keep them with himself, in such a way that they should be available for reference. But L. N. remained by his decision, and the diaries were kept in the bank until his death.

Sofya Andreyevna's intensified agitation compelled those around her to summon a specialist-psychiatrist. There arrived Dr. Rossolimo, accompanied by Dmitry Vasilyevich Nikitin. They examined and questioned the patient and diagnosed paranoia, in no wise relieving thereby the position either of the patient herself or of those around her.

At this time there enters into the relations between S. A. and L. N. yet one more complicating and aggravating element — this is the will written by L. N. Sofya Andreyevna suspected its existence and was agitated, would broach it with L. N., while he had to evade a direct answer, and this, of course, was extremely hard for him.

On the morning of 30 July I arrived with my family at Yasnaya Polyana and stayed there as a guest for six days.

I found at Yasnaya Polyana a terrible atmosphere.

I copy out from my reminiscences my impression of this visit.

> "We arrived at Y. P. on 30 July, three months before L. N.'s departure. The time was a heavy one. S. A., in hysterical fits of insane jealousy, tormented L. N. The object of the jealousy was Chertkov. The ground for the jealousy was the influence of Chertkov upon L. N., increasing, as it seemed to her. And since everyone supposed that Chertkov's influence must bring also material disadvantages for the family, this influence called forth in many members of the family a feeling hostile toward Chertkov, close to hatred, though in differing degrees. And in S. A. this hatred attained the highest degree and took on a morbid, insane form.
>
> To every newcomer, with varying degrees of detail, S. A. complained of her wretched situation and with cynical frankness recounted all the vicissitudes of her jealousy, all the facts — for the most part existing only in her imagination — which, in her opinion, justified her jealousy.
>
> The history of L. N.'s relations to the world hostile to him is a long one, and it is out of place here to set it all forth. I shall say only that these relations began from the time when there began to dawn in L. N. that consciousness of life which had flashed up in him as far back as the beginning of the sixties and which had been smothered by the family-and-household life for nearly fifteen years. And as soon as it dawned again, L. N. met with a rebuff and continued to meet it to the end of his life in that milieu which had smothered it before too, and which, since the world has stood, always has been and will be hostile to every manifestation of a truth that has not yet entered into the conditions of accepted custom.
>
> At this time, that is, in the autumn of 1910, this hostility manifested itself with a peculiarly passionate, morbid force.
>
> S. A. met me with my family with peculiar cordiality, as if she sought in me an ally in her struggle against L. N., Alex. Lv., and Chertkov. The hope for this was given her by that certain sympathy for her genuinely difficult situation which she had noticed in me and which I had shown her before. And also by that sometimes critical attitude which manifested itself in me toward my friend Chertkov, whom I immeasurably respected and sincerely loved, but with whom I sometimes differed in the application of our kindred thoughts. I was sorry to see how he, as it seemed to me, subjected L. N. to himself, sometimes compelling him to commit acts that seemed not in accord with his cast of thought. L. N., who sincerely loved Chertkov, was, it seemed to me, burdened by this tutelage, but submitted to it unconditionally, since it was carried out in the name of the principles dearest to him. Perhaps this attitude of mine toward Chertkov was guided also by an evil feeling of jealousy on L. N.'s account.
>
> The inhabitants of Yasnaya Polyana were living through a hard time then. Those who came there received the impression of a kind of struggle of two parties: the one, at the head of which stood Chertkov, had its adherents at Yasnaya Polyana in the persons of Alexandra Lvovna and Varvara Mikhailovna; and the other party — S. A. and her sons. Tatyana Lvovna, who was little at Yasnaya, stood somewhat apart and might have been a good mediator between them, had circumstances favored this. I too did not adhere wholly either to the one party or to the other, since I clearly recognized the wrongness of both. And since both parties considered me a person close to themselves, they explained my incomplete sympathy with their conduct by my insincerity, my duplicity, my wish to gain something from both sides, and this caused me much suffering and many insults, which I tried to bear in silence, being assured that I was guided by love of the truth.
>
> My arrival revived the hopes of both parties; in me they hoped to see a mediator-peacemaker. But I did not justify their expectations, and, it seems, with my arrival the struggle grew still more acute, since I brought into it yet my own, personal element.
>
> Lev Nikolaevich, of course, stood above this struggle and, being spiritually, ideologically on Chertkov's side, recognized at the same time clearly his obligations toward Sofya Andreyevna, strove to soften the manifestations of her morbid passion, and not infrequently showed her tenderness and solicitude. Unfortunately, in the people around him he found no support for this loving disposition.
>
> Such was the situation when I arrived at Yasnaya. S. A. rejoiced greatly at this and on the very next day called me into her room and, in an almost hour-long conversation, poured out to me her whole anguished soul.
>
> It is hard, of course, to convey this conversation: it was a passionate wail, a call for help, a desperate, hopeless call, since she herself felt that I personally could do nothing. She declared to me that she was very unhappy, that Chertkov had taken L. N. away from her.
>
> It is impossible to convey the content of all this insane raving. To object was, of course, out of the question. And silence seemed to her assent. The interest which I showed in the new information communicated to me by her, which gave me as a biographer new psychological material, appeared to her a kind of sympathy or approval on my part.
>
> She read to me L. N.'s letter to her, written in July and representing a certain attempt to establish a *modus vivendi* under the present hard circumstances. And much else besides did she say at that time, which I no longer recall. When she had finished her whole account, she concluded it with this question: 'Do you understand me?' I answered sincerely: 'Yes, I understand.' 'And you do not condemn?' she asked, now more boldly. 'And I do not condemn,' I answered, partly won over by the passionateness of her exposition, partly conscious of the impossibility of any logical objection, since before me was, evidently, a person possessed by a morbid mania.
>
> This attitude of mine was enough for me to be considered wholly in solidarity with all her ravings.
>
> We went upstairs. She entered the hall, where the rest of the guests and the inhabitants of the house were sitting — among others, my wife, the wife of Andr. Lvov., Varv. Mikh., and, it seems, my children. Sitting down beside them, she joyfully announced that 'Pav. Iv. agrees with her in everything.' I did not hear this, so that I could not object to this assertion; it remained without protest and was reckoned to me as a manifestation of hostile feelings toward Lev Nikolaevich."

This extract from my reminiscences sufficiently depicts the terrible atmosphere which Lev Nikolaevich breathed in his old age in his own home.

As I have already mentioned earlier, these relations were further complicated by the question of the will, whose drawing-up S. A. suspected. To this question I shall devote the following chapter, and this one I shall conclude with the mention of certain events in the course of this month.

Having stayed several days at Yasnaya, we, the whole family, set out for Moscow on the evening of 4 August. Already while we were there a telegram had been received about the arrival at Yasnaya of Vlad. Galakt. Korolenko. Their meeting was joyful, and between them there was established a heartfelt bond. In his diary L. N. records thus:

> "A conversation with Korolenko. An intelligent and good man; but wholly under the superstition of science."

According to Bulgakov's testimony, Korolenko told L. N. very much about his impressions of everyday life and interested him greatly in many things.

Of course, the first theme of their conversation was Korolenko's article on capital punishments ("An Everyday Occurrence"). Korolenko said that, thanks to L. N.'s letter to him about this article, it had indeed acquired enormous social significance. L. N. said that if this had happened, it was by virtue of the merits of the article itself.

L. N.'s life was becoming ever more serious and concentrated.

On 10 August he records in his diary:

> "Health worse and worse. S. A. is calm, but just as estranged. Letters. Answered two. Quite hard. I cannot but wish for death."

On 15 August L. N., S. A., Alex. Lv., and Dušan Petrovich went to stay at Kochety with Tatyana Lvovna. They were accompanied also by Tatyana Lvovna herself, who was returning home. There they lived this time more than a month and returned to Yasnaya only on 23 September. S. A. returned a little earlier, on 13 September.

Lev Nikolaevich lived there relatively more calmly than at Yasnaya. Although S. A.'s morbid manifestations continued there too, still she restrained herself before strangers, and these manifestations were not so stormy.

During L. N.'s stay at Kochety I, living at Kostroma, received from L. N. his last letter, precious to me. I had sent him beforehand my article with a description of my life in exile. At first he gave me a reserved opinion of it, and now he hastened to correct his mistake. Although it is not altogether modest, I shall permit myself to set down this letter in full, since it was the last. Here it is:

2 September. Kochety.

> "Yesterday, dear Posha, I wrote not quite the truth about the fact that I had read your reminiscences of exile. I read them yesterday, but not all of them, and in haste. Today I read them over calmly, and I want to write you that they are very good. Your gentle firmness — yours precisely — and your strict truthfulness, and besides that, or rather precisely because of it, make those measures which are employed against you appear especially revolting, more so than, by the descriptions of certain of the most terrible acts of violence. So please write, and continue to love me, as I love you."

While at Kochety, L. N. wrote an interesting letter to Konstantin Yakovlevich Grot, the brother of the deceased philosopher Nikolai Yakovlevich, with whom he had long been in great friendship.

In this letter, recalling the character of his friend, L. N. gives a definition of religion, philosophy, and science, and speaks of their correct interrelation. Thus he writes, among other things:

> "The religious understanding says: there exists, before all else and more indubitably than all else, a certain indefinable something known to us: this something is our soul and God. But precisely because we know this before all else and more indubitably than all else, we can in no way define it by anything, and we believe that it exists and that it is the foundation of all things: and it is upon this faith that we build all our further teaching. The religious understanding singles out, from all that is cognizable by man, that which is not subject to definition, and says of it: 'I do not know.' And such a procedure in relation to that which it is not given to man to know constitutes the first and most necessary condition of true knowledge. Such are the teachings of Zoroaster, of the Brahmins, of Buddha, of Lao-Tzu, of Confucius, of Christ. The philosophical understanding of life, on the other hand, not seeing the difference, or closing its eyes to the difference, between the cognition of external phenomena and the cognition of the soul, of God, considers chemical combinations and man's consciousness of his 'I,' astronomical observations and calculations and the recognition of the source of the life of all things, to be alike subject to rational and verbal definitions; confusing the definable with the undefinable, the cognizable with the consciously-apprehended, not ceasing to build fantastic theories, each negating the other, one upon another, striving to define the undefinable. Such are the teachings on life of the Aristotles, the Platos, the Leibnizes, the Lockes, the Hegels, the Spencers, and of very, very many others, whose name is legion. In essence, all these teachings represent either empty reasonings about that which is not subject to reasoning — reasonings which may be called philosophistry, but not philosophy, not love-of-wisdom, but wisdom-mongering — or bad repetitions of that which, with respect to the moral laws, is expressed far better in the various religious teachings."

There too, at Kochety, Lev Nikolaevich wrote a remarkable letter to the Hindu Gandhi, then still a modest public figure among the Transvaal Hindu workers, but already even then an ardent follower of the teaching of non-resistance, under the influence of reading L. N.'s works in conjunction with Hindu wisdom.

Since that time Gandhi has become world-famous; he raised a mass movement of passive resistance in British India. And he has already sealed his activity with a heavy sacrifice. He is sentenced to six years' imprisonment. Lev Nikolaevich's letter cast seeds upon good soil, and these seeds gave an abundant harvest. Here is this letter:

Gandhi. Iohannesburg
Transvaal, S. Afr.

> "I received your journal *Indian Opinion* and was glad to learn all that is written there about the non-resisters. And I wished to tell you those thoughts which this reading called forth in me.
>
> The longer I live, and especially now, when I keenly feel the nearness of death, I want to tell others that which I so especially keenly feel and which, in my opinion, is of enormous importance — namely, that which is called non-resistance, but which, in essence, is nothing other than the teaching of love, undistorted by false interpretations. That love — that is, the striving toward the union of human souls, and the activity arising out of this striving — is the highest and only law of human life, every man feels and knows in the depth of his soul (as we see most clearly in children), knows so long as he is not entangled by the false teachings of the world. This law was proclaimed by all, by the Indian as well as the Chinese and the Hebrew, the Greek, the Roman sages of the world. I think that it was expressed most clearly of all by Christ, who even said directly that in this one thing is the whole law and the prophets. But more than this, foreseeing the distortion to which this law is subjected and may be subjected, he pointed directly at that danger of its distortion which is proper to people living by worldly interests — namely, that of permitting themselves the defense of these interests by force; that is, as he said, of answering blows with blows, of taking back by force objects that have been appropriated, and so on. He knew, as every reasonable man cannot but know this, that the employment of violence is incompatible with love as the fundamental law of life; that as soon as violence is admitted, in whatsoever cases, the insufficiency of the law of love is acknowledged, and thereby the very law is denied. The whole of Christian civilization, so brilliant in its externals, has grown up upon this obvious and strange, sometimes conscious, for the most part unconscious, misunderstanding and contradiction. In essence, as soon as resistance was admitted alongside love, then there no longer was, and could not be, love as the law of life; and since there was no law of love, then there was no law at all, save violence, that is, the power of the strongest. So for nineteen centuries Christian humanity has lived. True, in all ages men have guided themselves by violence alone in the ordering of their life. The difference of the life of Christian peoples from all others lies only in this — that in the Christian world the law of love was expressed so clearly and definitely as it was not expressed in any other religious teaching, and that the people of the Christian world solemnly accepted this law, and at the same time permitted themselves violence and built their life upon violence; and therefore the whole life of Christian peoples is a continuous contradiction between that which they profess and that upon which they build their life: a contradiction between love, recognized as the law of life, and violence, recognized even as a necessity in various forms — as the power of rulers, the courts, and the troops, recognized and extolled. This contradiction grew along with the development of the people of the Christian world, and of late has reached the last degree. The question stands evidently thus: one of two things — either to recognize that we recognize no religious-moral teaching whatever and guide ourselves in the ordering of our life by the power of the strong alone; or that all our taxes, gathered by violence, our judicial and police institutions, and, chiefly, our troops, must be abolished.
>
> This spring, at the examination in the Law of God of one of the women's institutes of Moscow, the teacher of religion, and then the bishop present too, questioned the girls about the commandments, and especially about the sixth. To a correct answer about the commandment the bishop usually put yet another question: whether killing is always, in all cases, forbidden by the Law of God; and the unfortunate girls, depraved by their instructors, were obliged to answer, and did answer, that not always, that killing is permitted in war and at the executions of criminals. However, when to one of these unfortunate girls (what I am relating is not an invention, but a fact conveyed to me by an eyewitness), upon her answer, was put the same customary question — whether killing is always sinful? — she, agitated and blushing, resolutely answered that it is always; and to all the bishop's customary sophistries she answered with the resolute conviction that killing is always forbidden, and that killing was forbidden in the Old Testament too, and was forbidden by Christ — not only killing, but all evil against one's brother. And, despite all his grandeur and his art of eloquence, the bishop fell silent, and the girl left victorious.
>
> Yes, we may discourse in our newspapers about the successes of aviation, about complicated diplomatic relations, about various clubs, discoveries, unions of every kind, so-called artistic productions, and pass over in silence what that girl said; but to pass over this in silence is impossible, because this is felt, more or less dimly, but is felt, by every man of the Christian world. Socialism, communism, anarchism, the Salvation Army, the increasing criminality, the unemployment of the population, the increasing insane luxury of the rich and the destitution of the poor, the frightfully increasing number of suicides — all this is a sign of that inner contradiction which must, and cannot but, be resolved. And, of course, resolved in the sense of the recognition of the law of love and the denial of all violence. And therefore your activity in the Transvaal, as it seems to us, at the end of the world, is the most central, the most important of all the works that are now being done in the world, and one in which not only the Christian peoples, but the peoples of the whole world, will inevitably take part.
>
> I think that it will be pleasant to you to learn that with us in Russia too this activity is rapidly developing in the form of refusals of military service, of which there are more and more with every year. However insignificant be the number both of your people, the non-resisters, and, with us in Russia, the number of those refusing — both the one and the other may boldly say that God is with them. And God is mightier than men.
>
> In the recognition of Christianity — even in that distorted form in which it is professed among the Christian peoples — and in the recognition, together with this, of the necessity of troops and of armaments for killing on the most enormous scale in wars, there lies such an obvious, crying contradiction, that it must inevitably, sooner or later, probably very soon, become manifest and destroy either the recognition of the Christian religion, which is necessary for the maintenance of power, or the existence of the troops and of all the violence supported by them, which is no less necessary for power. This contradiction is felt by all governments, both by yours, the British, and by ours, the Russian; and out of a natural feeling of self-preservation it is persecuted by these governments — as we have seen in Russia, and as is evident from the articles of your journal — more energetically than any other anti-governmental activity: the governments know wherein lies their chief danger, and keenly guard, in this question, not only their interests now, but the question of to be or not to be.
> With perfect respect, Lev Tolstoy."

7 September 1910. Kochety.

I had occasion to visit L. N. once again at the beginning of October 1910. It seemed to me that S. A. was a little calmer. L. N. was concentrated, but bright and joyful.

But the work which he had taken upon himself had broken down his strength.

On 4 October L. N. rode out on horseback, returned tired, and lay down to sleep before dinner without undressing, without even taking off his boots.

We had already sat down to the table, by custom, about six o'clock in the evening. L. N. was to come and join the dinner. As he tarried, they began to dine without him. He did not come for a long time, and S. A., uneasy, went to look in on him — he was sleeping soundly; she returned to the table. After some time Dušan went to look in on him and found that he was pale and that his sleep in general was abnormal, and he expressed the apprehension of some complications. He proposed that someone keep watch over the sleeping L. N., and I went and sat down by the door of the bedroom in such a way as to see him lying on the bed.

After a few minutes I noticed a twitching of the legs. I at once gave word, and all gathered around the bed — that is, S. A., Dušan, Serg. Lv., I, and the servant Ilya Vasilyevich.

There began in Lev Nikolaevich terrible convulsions, first in the legs, then in the whole body and in the face. We all, several men, tried to hold L. N., since we feared that the convulsions would throw him from the bed onto the floor, but we could not prevent the morbid contraction of all the muscles.

Such attacks of convulsions recurred five times, with intervals of quiet. All the necessary measures were taken. L. N. was undressed; in the intervals of rest he would begin to be delirious. It was hard to make out the words of the delirium. At the same time he would fold his right hand into the usual position of one writing and would move it swiftly over the blanket. Then we tried to put a pencil into his hand and to slip a writing-pad under it, but he could write nothing.

These fits continued about an hour, and then a quiet sleep set in. We arranged a watch; I remained sitting, and after about an hour L. N. awoke and, seeing me, was much surprised that I was there, asked why, and when I had explained to him all that had happened, said with astonishment that he remembered nothing. Then L. N. fell asleep again, and the night passed quietly.

On the next day, still lying in bed, he already began to occupy himself, to write letters, to make notes, and in general to show a lively interest in all the surrounding life.

The behavior of S. A. during this fit was touching. She was pitiful in her fear and humiliation. At the time when we, the men, were holding L. N. so that the convulsions should not throw him from the bed, she would throw herself on her knees by the bed and pray a passionate prayer, of approximately the following content: "Lord, save me, forgive me, Lord, do not let him die; it is I who have driven him to this; only not this time, do not take him from me, Lord."

When L. N. grew calm, she would throw herself toward him and take an active part in all kinds of the help rendered to him.

On the next day, in the morning, there took place the joyful event of the reconciliation of S. A. with Al. Lvovna.

It must be noted that some time before, Alexandra Lvovna, after one stormy scene with her mother on account of her attitude toward her father, had left Yasnaya Polyana with her friend and settled at Telyatinki, two versts from Yasnaya Polyana, on her own farmstead. When L. N. fell ill, horses were at once sent for her, and she arrived even before the end of the fit.

Here is how Bulgakov relates this:

> "The initiative of the reconciliation belonged to S. A. She had long awaited A. L., when the latter, before her departure for Telyatinki, came in to her father. But she let her slip, came out after her onto the porch, and, as A. L. afterward related, stood there in a single dress, stooped, pitiful, lonely. A. L., meanwhile, had gone out by the back door and now had to pass by her mother. S. A. stopped her daughter, beckoned her over, began to embrace, to kiss her, and to beg forgiveness, sobbing and trembling as in a fever. A. L., too, fell to weeping, begged her to forgive her, promising to return that very day. S. A. called for Varvara Mikhailovna too, begged that it be passed on to her that she loved her, was not angry with her, and asked her forgiveness if she had offended her. She promised not to disturb L. N.'s peace by a single word. Both, mother and daughter, experienced a tender emotion."

A few days after my return from Yasnaya Polyana, Dušan Petrovich wrote to me:

> "L. N. has recovered. S. A. is again behaving as is unbecoming. Two days ago V. Gr. was with us; today, Nazhivin."

Of Nazhivin's arrival L. N. wrote in a letter to T. L.: "Of the visitors, Nazhivin, who is agreeable to me, was here." Ivan Fyodorovich Nazhivin described this visit in his interesting book *From the Life of L. N. Tolstoy*.

We borrow from this description a significant conversation devoted to the question of the religious rite.

Iv. Fyodorovich Nazhivin addressed L. N. with the following words:

> "A year before this my daughter Mirusha, the dearest being in the world to me, had died. We buried her without the observance of the established rite, but this was terribly hard: one longed for a certain setting, for solemnity.
>
> Around me live many sectarians. Observing their life, I see that they too somehow yearn for a rite, and that in them the inner religious life seeks expression in external forms: now they begin to whirl, now to leap, now to dance, and all this, evidently, does not satisfy them.
>
> And this need for an external religion lives in all humanity: no sooner had Christ departed, no sooner had Buddha departed, who taught a pure, rite-less religion, than their disciples covered the whole earth with temples and established a complex ritual. And you yourself clearly felt this, — I reminded him again, — you remember your letter to Fet, where you say: well, very good, we reject the rite, but here a dear person dies with us; what then — call the coachman and order him to carry the body out in a sack somewhere farther off? No, that is impossible, you said; both the little pink coffin, and the incense, and even the solemn Slavonic tongue seemed to you necessary...
>
> — Yes, I remember. And I understand you, — answered L. N. — But this is only a weakness, with which one must struggle. This shows how firmly our superstitions sit in us.
>
> — What then? Is one really to call the coachman and bid him carry out the corpse of a dear being?
>
> — No, if this is painful to you, if this offends you... — answered L. N. — And I so far understand this feeling that I am ready to reason with you, as much as you like, in order to work out forms so as to do this as well, as solemnly, as possible. I say only that this has nothing in common with religion — it is only a question of... well, of convenience, let us say, of propriety... Let us think up something together, only one must not think that this is religion. But once we acknowledge this to be religion, by that very act we open in the dam a little hole, through which all the water will run out. And this is so terrible, this has brought so much evil to people, — said L. N. in a voice trembling with agitation, — that I am ready sooner to give the corpses of my children, of all my dear ones, to be torn to pieces by hungry dogs, than to summon some special people for the performance of a religious rite over their bodies.
>
> In the course of this conversation L. N. touched upon the question of the sin that hinders the manifestation of love:
>
> — The manifestation of love is hindered usually by our weakness, by temptations, by sins. Consequently, one must perfect oneself, cleanse oneself of weaknesses and sins, and then that feeling of union with God which you seek to attain by higher means will come of itself... There, your beloved child died, — continued L. N. after a silence, — you suffered; but let some Marfushka there die, — he made an indefinite gesture toward the village hidden in the dark, — it would be all the same to you...
>
> — Yes...
>
> — Well, there. But your attitude both to the death of your daughter and to the death of Marfushka ought to be the same. And if it is not the same — bear the punishment for your weakness, suffer. Suffering, like the needle of a compass, shows that you have strayed from the road."

In the middle of October L. N. wrote Chertkov a letter which characterizes both his state of soul at that time and shows his strong love for his friend. Here is what he wrote to him:

> "I want, dear friend, to talk with you from the heart. To no one as to you can I so easily speak out — I know that no one will understand as you will, however unclearly, however unsaid, may be that which I want to say.
>
> Yesterday was a very serious day. The factual details they will tell you, but I want to tell you my own — the inward.
>
> I pity and pity her and rejoice that at times, without effort, I love her. So it was yesterday night, when she came penitent and began to take pains to warm my room and, despite her exhaustion and weakness, pushed at the shutters, fastened the windows; she busied herself, fussed over my... bodily peace. What is to be done, if there are people for whom (and that, I think, only for a time) the reality of the spiritual life is inaccessible. Yesterday, from the evening, I had almost made ready to go away to Kochety, but now I am glad that I did not go. Today I feel bodily weak, but in my soul very good. And it is just because of this that I want to tell you what I think and, chiefly, feel.
>
> I had thought little, up to yesterday, about my fits — had not even thought at all; but yesterday I clearly, vividly pictured to myself how I shall die in one of such fits. And I understood that, despite the fact that such a death in the bodily sense, wholly without bodily sufferings, is very good, it in the spiritual sense deprives me of those precious minutes of dying which can be so beautiful. And this led me to the thought that, if I am for a time deprived of these last conscious minutes, then it is, after all, in my power to extend them over all the hours, days, perhaps months, years (hardly that), which precede my death. And it is this very thought — even feeling — which I experienced yesterday and experience today and shall try to hold to until death, that especially rejoices me, and it is to you that I want to convey it. In essence, all this is very old, but to me it has been disclosed from a new side.
>
> This same feeling also lights up my path in my situation, and out of that which was, and might have been, hard, makes a joy.
>
> I do not want to write of affairs — afterward.
>
> And do you, likewise, open to me your soul.
>
> I do not want to say to you 'farewell,' because I know that you do not even wish to see that for which one would have to forgive me; but I say always the one thing that I feel: I thank you for your love.
>
> It is I who have permitted myself to grow so sentimental, but do you not follow my example.
>
> I am sorry only that I have not yet managed to see Galya. Her I beg to forgive me. And she, probably, will fulfill my request."

L. N.'s love for his friend, expressed in this letter, laid upon him a considerable responsibility for his conduct in those complex, tormenting circumstances which surrounded L. N.

The readers of this chapter will probably notice certain gaps in the exposition. These gaps have come about for the following reason: the year 1910, besides the current events in L. N.'s life of a general character, was marked by yet two large events of a particular kind, which influenced in one way or another the end of his life. One of these events was the drawing-up of the will, and the other — his departure from Yasnaya Polyana, which was prepared with especial intensity in this year. In order to depict more clearly these two important events, we have extracted them from the general course and shall devote to each of them a separate chapter.
