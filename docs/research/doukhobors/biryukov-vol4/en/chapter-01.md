## Part I. 1900–1902. The Excommunication. The Crimean Illness.

On 1 January L. N. makes the following entry in his diary:

> "I sit in my room, and all of mine are seeing in the New Year. All this while I have written nothing, I am unwell. There is much to set down."

L. N. was living at this time in Moscow with his family. From the entries of his diary a certain physical weakness is noticeable, which breathes upon him melancholy thoughts. He even begins to repent of the sins of his youth. He recalled his boyhood, and chiefly his youth and his early manhood: "No moral principles whatever were instilled in me, none at all, while around me grown people smoked, drank, debauched (debauched in particular), beat people and demanded labour of them, all with assurance. And much that was bad I did, not wishing to do it, only out of imitation of the grown people."

At the same time his thoughts penetrate the very essence of things, and he makes interesting and profound generalizations:

> "I was riding on the top of the horse-tram, looking at the houses, the signboards, the shops, the cabmen, the passengers, the passers-by, and suddenly it became so clear that this whole world, with my life in it, is only one of the countless numbers of possibilities of other worlds and other lives, and that for me it is only one of the countless stages through which it seems to me that I pass in time."

Interesting is the entry which L. N. makes a few days later:

> "I read the newspapers, the journals, the books, and still I cannot grow accustomed to assigning their true value to what is written there, namely: the philosophy of Nietzsche, the dramas of Ibsen and Maeterlinck, and the science of Lombroso and of that doctor who makes eyes. Why, this is utter poverty of thought, of understanding, and of intuition." "I read about the war in the Philippines and in the Transvaal, and horror and loathing take hold of me. Why? The wars of Frederick, of Napoleon, were sincere and therefore were not without a certain grandeur. This was so even in the Sevastopol war. But the wars of America and of England, in the midst of a world in which war is already condemned by schoolboys — are terrible."

On 8 January Chaliapin sang at L. N.'s in Moscow. About this same time there came to pass an interesting event — L. N.'s acquaintance with Gorky, who had come to him in Moscow. And L. N. notes this event in his diary in these words:

> "Gorky was here. We talked very well. And I liked him. A genuine man of the people. What an astonishing instinct women have for recognizing a celebrity! They know it not by the impressions they receive, but by how and whither the crowd runs. Often, very likely, a woman has received no impression at all, yet already she appraises, and rightly."

At the same time his friendship with Chekhov continues. L. N. valued him highly both as a man and as an artist, but did not approve of his dramatic works.

Thus on 27 January he records: "I went to see *Uncle Vanya* and was indignant." But, evidently, the drama touched him to the quick all the same, for he adds: "I wanted to write a drama, *The Corpse*, and sketched out an outline. It seems to me that in the drama *The Living Corpse* there are notes breathed in by Chekhov's work. Such is the mystery of artistic creation."

Here too L. N. gives an interesting definition of matter and motion, a definition that might serve as the foundation of a whole philosophical system.

> "Seryozha and Usov were talking about the various understandings of the structure of the world: the discontinuity or continuity of matter. According to my understanding of life and of the world, matter is only my representation, arising out of my separateness from the world. Motion, on the other hand, is my representation, arising out of my communion with the world; and therefore for me there exists no question of the discontinuity or continuity of matter."

At the beginning of January Vl. Vas. Stasov came to Moscow. L. N. went with him to the Tretyakov Gallery and expressed a negative attitude toward the pictures of Vasnetsov and, on the contrary, admired the pictures of N. N. Ge.

At this time, in L. N.'s relations with his family, a certain calming is to be noticed; thus, in a letter to his daughter T. Lvovna, L. N. writes: "What is good in our life is that I live very harmoniously with mama, which is the chief thing, and also with Seryozha, ever closer and closer, and ever more touchingly. When she begins to ask about how my stomach is working, or with timidity offers to rub my back in the bathhouse — that acts upon me with a particular tenderness."

Toward the end of January L. N.'s health, which had been restored, again gave way, but, thank God, not for long.

At this time the English were waging war with the Boers in the name of "civilization."

This dreadful crime of falsehood and cruelty greatly agitated Lev Nikolaevich.

To all it was evident that the English would, sooner or later, crush with their fist the brave but weak Boers. And this weakness of theirs inspired an involuntary sympathy toward them. L. N. too caught himself in this sympathy.

One correspondent reports thus his conversation with L. N. concerning the Transvaal war:

> "Of his own works the count spoke in general reluctantly, but the moment the talk turned to the Transvaal and the Anglo-Transvaal war, the great old man grew animated; his eyes began to shine.
>
> 'Do you know to what lengths I went,' he said. 'There is no more of that now; I have overcome myself… In the morning, taking the newspaper in hand, I passionately desired, every time, to read that the Boers had beaten the English. This war is the greatest folly of our days. How can it be! Two highly civilized nations, the Dutch and the English, exterminating one another; England, a country that prided itself on the title of a free country, attempting to crush the few Boers, who had done the English not the slightest harm. This is something incomprehensible, incredible!…
>
> 'Do you know what this insane assault resembles?' Lev Nikolaevich remarked after a short pause. 'It is the same thing as if you and I, men already old, were suddenly to drive off to the gypsies at the "Strelna," having lost all shame. And this slaughter, observe, is being committed after the Hague Conference, which made such a noise. The Transvaal war is a sign of our times, but a mournful sign, telling that the world is governed by soulless huckstering…'"

The count, after a silence, added:

> "'From the Transvaal one of my acquaintances, who is now there, writes to me, and therefore the circumstances there are well known to me.'"

L. N.'s words were caught up, and it turned out that Tolstoy was on the side of the Boers and consequently approved of all their actions. But to L. N. by no means all the actions of the Boers were sympathetic, and he could in no way take the side of one of the combatants. This attitude of his he tried to express in a letter to the Englishman Maude, who had turned to him for an explanation.

> "I, of course," writes L. N., "could not say, and did not say, what is ascribed to me. This came about because, to the newspaper correspondent who had come to me under the guise of an author bringing his book, I said, in answer to his question about my attitude toward the war, that I had been horrified at myself, having caught myself during my illness in the wish to find in the newspaper news of a Boer victory, and that I was glad of the occasion to express in a letter to Volkonsky my true attitude toward this affair, which consists in this: that I cannot sympathize with any military exploits whatsoever, even were it David against a dozen Goliaths, but sympathize only with those people who destroy the causes — the prestige of gold, of riches, the prestige of military glory, and the chief cause of all the evil — the prestige of patriotism and of a false religion, which justify fratricides.
>
> I think it is not worth while to print in the newspapers a refutation of an opinion falsely ascribed to me. You cannot say 'bless you' to every sneeze. I, for example, have lately been receiving letters from America, in which some reproach and others approve me for having renounced all my convictions. Is it worth refuting, when tomorrow twenty new items of news may be invented, which will serve to fill the columns of the newspaper and the pockets of the publishers? However, do as you find needful."

The letter (to Volkonsky), to which L. N. refers, is no less interesting. In it L. N. for the first time clearly expresses his view on the causes of war in general. We shall give from it the most essential extract:

> "If two men, having got drunk in a tavern, fall to fighting over cards, I shall in no way bring myself to condemn one of them, however convincing the arguments of the other may be. The cause of the disgraceful acts of the one or the other lies in no way in the injustice of one of them, but in the fact that, instead of working or resting in peace, they found it needful to drink wine and play cards in a tavern. Just so, when I am told that in any war whatever that has flared up only one side is to blame, I can in no way agree with this. One may admit that one of the sides acts more badly, but an analysis of which acts worse will in no way explain even the nearest cause of why so strange, cruel, and inhuman a phenomenon as war comes about.
>
> These causes, for every man who does not close his eyes, are perfectly evident — both now, in the Transvaal war, and in all the wars that have been of late. Of these causes there are in all three: the first — the unequal distribution of property, that is, the robbing of some people by others; the second — the existence of military estates, of people brought up and destined for killing; and the third — the false, for the most part consciously deceitful, religious teaching in which the young generations are forcibly brought up.
>
> And therefore I think that it is not only useless but harmful to see the cause of war in the Chamberlains, in the Wilhelms, and the like, concealing thereby from ourselves the actual causes, which are far nearer, and in which we ourselves take part. At the Chamberlains and the Wilhelms we can only be angry and abuse them; but our anger and our abuse will only spoil our blood, and will not change the course of things: the Chamberlains and the Wilhelms are the blind instruments of forces lying far behind them. They act as they must act and as they cannot act otherwise. The whole of history is a series of just such acts of all political men, like the Transvaal war; and therefore to be angry with them and to condemn them is perfectly useless and even impossible, when one sees the true causes of their activity and when one feels that one is oneself the author of this or that activity, according to one's own attitude toward the three fundamental causes of which I have spoken.
>
> So long as we shall make use of riches exclusively, while the masses of the people are crushed by toil, there will always be wars for markets, for gold-mines, and the like, which are necessary to us in order to maintain our exclusive wealth. The more inevitable will wars be so long as we shall take part in the military estate, allow its existence, and not fight against it with all our strength. We ourselves either serve in the military estate, or recognize it as not only necessary but praiseworthy, and then, when a war arises, condemn in it some Chamberlain or other. Chiefly, however, there will be war so long as we shall not only preach, but, without indignation and revolt, allow that perversion of Christianity which is called Church Christianity, and under which there is possible a Christ-loving host, the blessing of cannon, and the recognition of war as a thing Christianly just. We teach this religion to our children, we ourselves profess it, and then say — some, that Chamberlain, and others, that Kruger, is to blame for men killing one another."

Simultaneously with this war, which once more showed what dreadful sacrifices are offered up to please the god of state power, there went on another, peaceful war against war, of which L. N. had written as far back as 1898 in his article *Two Wars*. The one war then just concluded was the Spanish-American; and the other was the Doukhobor war, the war against war, expressed in the resistance of the Doukhobors to the Russian state power. The Doukhobors, who had not submitted to the demands of the Russian authorities, left Russia and settled in Canada. By the beginning of 1900 this emigration was already almost completed; there remained still in Russia the Doukhobors of that party who had been exiled to the Yakutsk region.

In proportion as the Doukhobors who had emigrated to Canada began to settle themselves there, various difficulties, frictions, and sometimes even grave disagreements began to arise. Rumours of this began to reach L. N. as well. In the accounts of the life of the Doukhobors in Canada there was much that was exaggerated or coloured in this or that hue, according to the subjective element of the observer. But none the less one could not but recognize that the Doukhobor ideal of "the Christian universal brotherhood" was being attained with great difficulty and was still far from having been carried out to the end. All this prompted L. N. to write them a friendly epistle, in which he gives them the possibility of becoming conscious of this ideal and of defining their true attitude toward it. In this letter L. N. clearly and briefly sets forth the foundations of Christian communism, which denies the rights of private property. This it is that constitutes the particular value of this letter, and we shall give from it the most essential extracts:

> "Why, it only seems to us that one can be a Christian and have property and withhold it from other people, but this is impossible. Let men only recognize this, and from Christianity there will very soon remain nothing but words, and, unhappily, insincere and hypocritical words. Christ said that one cannot serve God and Mammon: it is one of two things — either to gather up property for oneself, or to live for God. At first it seems that between the denial of violence, the refusal of military service, and the recognition of property there is no connection whatever. 'We Christians do not worship outward gods, do not take the oath, do not judge, do not kill,' say many of us; 'while the property which we acquire by our own labour, not for enrichment but for the providing for those near to us — by this we not only do not violate the teaching of Christ, but even fulfil it, if from our superfluity we help the destitute.' But this is untrue. For 'property' means this: that what I consider mine I will not only not give to everyone who wishes to take this thing of mine, but I will defend it from him. And to defend from another that which you consider yours is impossible otherwise than by violence, that is, in case of need, by struggle, by fighting, even by killing. Were there not these acts of violence and these killings, no one could hold property. And if we do hold property without committing violence, it is only because our property is guarded by the threat of violence and by the very violence and killing which are committed upon people round about us. With us, even if we do not defend it, our property is not taken from us only because they think that we, like the others, shall defend it; and therefore the recognition of property is the recognition of violence and killing; and there was no need for you to refuse military and police service, if you recognize property, which is upheld only by military and police service. Those who perform military and police service and make use of property act better than those who do not bear military and police service, yet wish to make use of property. Such people, not serving themselves, wish for their own advantages to make use of the service of others. The Christian teaching cannot be taken piecemeal: it is all or nothing. It is all inseparably bound together into one whole. If a man recognizes himself a son of God, then from this recognition there flows love toward one's neighbour, and from love toward one's neighbour there follows equally the denial of violence, and of the oath, and of service, and of property."

Having set forth these religious foundations of communism, he passes on to utilitarian motives:

> "The temptation of property is the subtlest temptation, whose harm is very cunningly concealed from people, and therefore so many Christians have stumbled against this stone.
>
> And therefore, dear brothers and sisters, in arranging your life in a foreign land after you had been driven out of your fatherland for faithfulness to the Christian teaching, I see clearly that it is in every way more advantageous for you to continue to live a Christian life than to betray this and to begin to live a worldly life. It is more advantageous to live and work in common with all those who wish to live such a life, than for each to live apart, gathering only for himself and for his family, not sharing with others. It is more advantageous to live and work thus, first, because, not laying up for the future, you will not endlessly spend your strength upon the providing for yourself and your family, which is impossible for mortal man; secondly, you will not spend your strength upon struggle with others, in order to withhold from your neighbours each his own property; thirdly, because beyond comparison more will you work and acquire, working as a commune, than you would work, working each apart; fourthly, because, living as a commune, you will spend less upon yourselves than living each apart; and fifthly, because, living a Christian life, you will call forth in the people around you, instead of envy and unfriendliness, love, respect, and perhaps even imitation of your life; sixthly, because you will not ruin that work which you have begun and by which you have put your enemies to shame and gladdened the friends of Christ. But chiefly it is more advantageous for you to live a Christian life because, living such a life, you will know that you are fulfilling the will of Him who sent you into the world."

L. N. does not conceal, either from himself or from them, all the difficulty of this matter, but the consciousness of its importance all the same leads him to encourage them to a stubborn struggle:

> "I know that it is hard to have nothing of one's own, hard to be ready to give to everyone who asks that which one has and which is needful for the family, hard to submit to chosen leaders when it seems that they dispose of things wrongly, hard to refrain from the habits of luxury, of meat, of tobacco, of wine. I know that all this seems hard. But, dear brothers and sisters, why, today we are alive, and tomorrow we shall go to Him who sent us into this world in order to do His work. Is it worth while, for the sake of calling things one's own and disposing of them as one pleases, for the sake of a few poods of flour, of dollars, of a fur coat, of a pair of oxen, for the sake of not letting the non-working make use of what I have worked for, for the sake of an offensive word, of pride, of a tasty morsel, to go against Him who sent us into the world and not to do that which He wishes of us and which we can fulfil only in this our life? And He wishes of us but little: only that we should do to others that which we wish for ourselves. And He wishes this not for Himself, but for us, because, if only we would agree to do this, it would be as good for all to live on the earth as can be. But even now, though the whole world should live contrary to His will, for every separate man who has understood that for which he was sent into the world, there is no profit in doing anything other than only that for which he was sent."

In speaking, in Volume III, of the autobiographical part of *Resurrection*, we have already mentioned that the type of the old schismatic whom Nekhlyudov met on the ferry in Siberia is based on a personal acquaintance with a similar "seeker after truth," Andrei Vasilyevich Vlasov. L. N.'s correspondence with him continued. Lev Nikolaevich sent him little books, and evidently the old man became more and more imbued with L. N.'s views.

Thus in February 1900 L. N. answers Vlasov, in reply to one of his letters, with the following interesting letter:

> "Dear brother Andrei Vasilyevich, I was very glad to receive your letter. Your son thinks in vain that what you write cannot be understood. I understand it all and agree with it all, and should very much wish that learned men would write as clearly and intelligibly.
>
> Especially dear to me in your reasoning is that you understand that the Gospel too must be read with selection, and not regard all of it, one passage after another, as the work of the Holy Spirit.
>
> In the Gospel itself it is said that the letter is dead, but the spirit lives. Dear to me in your reasoning is that you set at the head of everything that which ought indeed to stand at the head of everything, namely, the human reason, which is older than all books and the Bible, from which all Bibles too have come, without which nothing can be understood, and which is given to each of us not through Moses, or Christ, or the apostles, or through the Church, but is given directly from God to each of us, and is one and the same for all. And therefore error may be in everything, but only not in reason. And men can diverge only when they shall believe different human traditions, and not the one reason, the same for all, given immediately from God to all.
>
> All idolaters and false teachers always preach that one must not believe reason, because, forsooth, it is special in each man and will lead to disagreement, but that one must believe all that which they teach (one one thing, another another — Christian, Mohammedan, Buddhist, and all their sects). But this is a lie, and is that very blasphemy against the Holy Spirit which is worse than all other blasphemies. False reasonings, those very ones which the false teachers have bred in the world, will lead to disagreement and discord; but reason, if it be not perverted, cannot lead to disagreement, because, as all men have the same body — all have hands, feet, ears, eyes — so reason too in all is one, and only the one reason unites all. As soon as the talk turns to how one should cross oneself, with two fingers or with three, or to whether the bread and wine pass into the blood and body or whether this is only to be done 'in remembrance,' or to whether the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son or from the Father alone, or to whether Christ was God or man, or to whether God is a personal being or an impersonal one — then men divide and quarrel and even hate one another because some do not believe just as others do. But if we shall hold only to that which is in accord with the reason of every man, namely, that we have come into this world not by our own will and shall depart from it not by our own will, but by someone's higher will, and that therefore we ought to live in this world according to that will which has brought us into the world and will lead us out of it. And this will, as reason tells us, is that we should love one another and act with others as we wish that others should act with us. The reason of all men says that if all lived thus, the life of all would be the very best. And therefore, if all held only to this, there would be no disagreement in faiths, but all would be in accord. And therefore every man, for his own good and for the good of men, ought to strive to destroy all the various false faiths, so that all might unite in the one true faith, in the light of reason given to all men for guidance in life.
>
> Before all else one must believe in reason, and only then select from the scriptures — the Hebrew, and the Christian, and the Mohammedan, and the Buddhist, and the Chinese, and the secular modern ones — all that is in accord with reason, and cast away all that is not in accord with it.
>
> I rejoice greatly that you understand it so. God help you to continue to confess and to live thus.
>
> I send you what I consider it possible to send, and wish you firmness of soul, calm, and joy in the truth. Write to me.
> Your loving brother Lev Tolstoy."

In March L. N. records in his diary:

> "For more than two months I have not written. Masha has gone away. Then Andryusha and Olga went away. My health during this time has considerably improved. I have written all the while: 1) the letter to the Doukhobors, which I finished and sent; 2) the article on patriotism, which I have copied out many times and which is dreadfully weak, so that yesterday I resolved either to throw it up or to do it all again from the beginning; and it seems there is something to say from the beginning. One must show that the present state of things, especially the Hague Conference, has shown that there is nothing to be expected from the higher authorities, and that the unravelling of this ruinous state of things, if it be possible at all, is possible only through the effort of private, separate individuals."

That same day L. N. records a remarkable thought, which gives the key to the understanding of social evolution and revolution and is a prediction of the present order.

At this time L. N. began to write his article *Slavery Ancient and Modern*. The occasion for the writing of this article was information that had reached L. N. of the existence of a sixteen-hour working day in some industrial enterprise. This news produced a strong impression upon him, and he records the following thought:

> "On the sixteen-hour day, it seems, something will come out. Chiefly, it will be shown that the present, impending emancipation will be just such as was the emancipation from serfdom, that is, that they release one chain only when the other holds firm. Slavery is abolished when serfdom is established. Serfdom is abolished when the land is taken away and taxes are imposed. Now they emancipate from taxes when the instruments of labour are taken away. They will give, they have the intention of giving, to the workers the instruments of labour, only on condition of the obligatoriness of work for all."

Further, the following notes of the diary strike the eye:

> "I pass by a dashing cabman; he has risen out of the grey peasants — has set up a turnout, has shaved himself, has a horse-cloth, a coat with sable, knows the good gentlemen… How is one to instil into him that all this is not important, but that what is important is the fulfilment of the moral law? At home, at school, in church, at the readings (such as the one at which I was, in the workhouse) — what does he hear?
>
> In the workhouse a priest, expounding to the people the first commandment of the Sermon on the Mount, explained that one may and must be angry, as the authorities are angry, and that one may kill by command of the authorities. This was dreadful. Everything can be forgiven, but not the perversion of those highest truths to which mankind has come with such difficulty."

At the same time within L. N. there went on uninterruptedly an inner work; here is a specimen of his prayer of that time:

> "Deliver me, O Lord (God within me), from the first temptation, the care for outward things, for food, dwelling, possessions, for human glory; let me remember that the life of man is only in the increase of reason, of love. And from the second temptation deliver me, from the thought that all is from God, that I am nothing, from indifference to the work of one's life, from the ceasing of effort; and let me remember that I am a servant, a messenger of God, an executor of His will. And from the third temptation, the serving of anything whatsoever more than God — deliver me, and let me remember that every work is good only then, when it is the supplement or the consequence of the service of God."

In April of this year there came to pass the first assault of the Synod upon L. N. for his world-view. The Synod sent round an edict to the effect that, should L. N. die, one must not pray for him. There has reached us the content of this secret edict, from the Vladimir Spiritual Consistory. Here is its exact text:

> "By the edict of His Imperial Majesty, the Vladimir Spiritual Consistory have heard: the communication of the presiding member of the Most Holy Synod, Ioannikii, Metropolitan of Kiev, addressed to His High Eminence, in which it is set forth that Count Lev Tolstoy, in his writings, in which he expresses his religious views, has clearly shown himself an enemy of the Orthodox Christian Church. The one God in three Persons he does not acknowledge; the 2nd Person of the Holy Trinity — the Son of God — he calls a simple man; he treats blasphemously the mystery of the incarnation of God the Word; he distorts the sacred text of the Gospel; he reviles the holy Church, calling it a human institution; he denies the Church hierarchy and mocks at the holy sacraments and rites of the holy Orthodox Church. Such people the Orthodox Church solemnly, in the presence of the faithful children, declares to be alien to Church communion. Therefore the performance of requiems and of the funeral liturgy over Count Lev Tolstoy, in the event of his death without repentance and reconciliation with the Church, will undoubtedly trouble the faithful children of the holy Church and call forth a scandal, which must be forestalled.
>
> In view of this the Most Holy Synod has resolved:
>
> To forbid the performance of commemoration, of requiems, of funeral liturgies for Count Lev Tolstoy in the event of his death without repentance."

L. N.'s mood at this time is excellently expressed in an entry of his diary:

> "6 April 1900. Moscow. It is now evening, Seryozha is playing, and I feel myself, for some reason, moved to tears, and I long for poetry. But I cannot write at such moments. I live not very ill, I am still working at that same work which has barred my way to the artistic one, and I miss the artistic one. It begs to be done very much."

But here his soul begs for prayer, and he records thus:

> "Lord, awake in me and enlighten me and my life! Such ought to be the prayer of every hour."

That same day he gives in his diary an interesting definition of the word "anarchy."

> "Anarchy means not the absence of institutions, but only the absence of such institutions to which people are made to submit by force, and the presence of such institutions to which people submit freely, by reason. It would seem that otherwise a society of beings endowed with reason could not and should not be ordered."

At the beginning of May L. N. went to stay with his daughter Marya Lvovna, who was married to Obolensky and lived on her own estate, in the neighbourhood of L. N.'s brother Sergei. There he was visited by his two English like-minded friends, St. John and Kenworthy. L. N. spent some two weeks there and again returned to Yasnaya Polyana. On the following day, 19 May, he records in his diary:

> "Yesterday we arrived from Pirogovo, where I spent fifteen days splendidly, finished *Slavery* and wrote two acts. I am well here too. My health had gone wrong. Now it is better. I have read a heap of letters. Nothing important. Today I have written the last chapter. It is late. Tomorrow I shall copy out of the little notebook."

The "two acts" relate, evidently, to the comedy *The Living Corpse*; the finishing of *The Slavery of Our Times* was not yet the last. We shall see that L. N. worked much more upon this work.

After this there comes a break of more than a month in the keeping of the diary, and L. N., taking it up again, sums up the time he had spent thus:

> "23 June 1900. Yasnaya Polyana. For more than a month I have not written; I have spent these 25 days not badly. There were heavy moods, but the religious feeling conquered. All the time, without ceasing, I have diligently written *The Slavery of Our Times*. I have brought in much that is new and clarifying.
>
> I dreadfully long to write something artistic — and not dramatic, but an epic continuation of *Resurrection*: the peasant life of Nekhlyudov.
>
> Nature moves me to tenderness (the meadows, the woods, the corn, the ploughland, the hay-making). I think: am I not living out my last summer? Well, and that too is good. I give thanks for everything — I have been blessed beyond measure. How one can always give thanks, and how joyful that is.
>
> There have been, during this time: the American Kurti, Boulanger, St. John. I have come to love him. My health is good. There is much to set down. But the chief thing is that all beings and I are completing a circle, or a half-circle, or some other lines within given limits, and in the course of our passage we gather communion with other beings, love them, expanding our 'I' into an idea, preparing it for expansion in the following form."

On 13 July L. N. makes the following entry, evidently in answer to the questions of the contemporary economic world-view:

> "There are no firmer convictions than those which are founded on advantage. Convictions founded on reason are always subject to discussion, to verification, while those are beyond appeal and decisive, however contrary to reason they may be.
>
> There are people who cannot be guided by reasonable convictions, but are guided only by advantages. And you devise arguments in order to convince them."

Is there not in these words the solution of the riddle of the epoch of economic materialism?

At the beginning of August I received from L. N. an interesting letter, in which he defines his inner spiritual state.

Having first told of outward events and of family life, he writes further thus: "My inner life is in this — that all the time, for some seven months, I have been writing *The Slavery of Our Times*. I think I have made a few things clear. The other day I wrote a small note on the murder of Umberto. The one and the other I have sent to Ch. Now I am adding a few things to it as well, and wish to continue the artistic works I have begun. I know that what I call the 'appeal' is more needful, but I want to rest from condemnation. This is the inner, but the superficially inner; the true inner is that I see death ever nearer and nearer, and therefore the true life as well, and ever more often I experience a spiritual love, distinct from the bodily — not the sexual, but the bodily sympathy — by this: that to the latter, that is, the bodily, one wishes to give oneself, whereas the spiritual, on the contrary, is for the most part called forth by the opposite feeling: you feel an unkind feeling — and you recall that there is death, that life is only in love, and you come to love with a spiritual love, sometimes stronger than the bodily. And spiritual love is for the most part turned toward enemies. In general I can say that it is well with me, it was well with me in my illness too, and I hope and strive that it may be well with me as I die. Of the supernumerary joys of life the greatest are afforded by young people, both from the rich and from the working classes, who come and write. It is gladdening too that anarchism without violence, the anarchism of non-participation in violence, spreads more and more."

The August diary is again full of profound thoughts. We give the most characteristic:

> "7 August. Our feelings toward people colour them all in one hue; if we love them, they all seem to us white; if we do not love them, black. Yet in all there is both black and white. Seek out the black in those you love, and chiefly the white in those you do not love.
>
> 15 August. To set up in our societies the rule: thou shalt not kill — is just the same as to set up in a bank the rule not to take interest. It would be worth while to clarify this thought, old though it is.
>
> 21 August. A mark of the depravity of our world is this — that people are not ashamed of riches, but proud of them. Strange is my position in the family. They may, perhaps, even love me, but I am not needed by them, rather *encombrant* (*); if I am needed, then I am needed as all people are. But in the family they see less than others how much I am needed by all. Hence: 'A prophet is not without honour,' and so on.
>
> (* In the way, taking up unnecessary room. *)
>
> 30 August. I asked myself somehow: do I believe? Do I truly believe that the meaning of life is in the fulfilment of the will of God, and that the will is in the increase of love (of accord) in oneself and in the world, and that by this increase, by the uniting into one of that which is loved, I am preparing for myself a future life?
>
> And involuntarily I answered that I do not so believe, in this definite form. In what, then, do I believe? — I asked. And I sincerely answered that I believe in this — that one must be good: humble oneself, forgive, love. In this I believe with my whole being.
>
> Everything in life is very simple, connected, of one order, and is explained one thing by another, but only not death. Death is altogether outside all this, it violates all this, and ordinarily it is ignored — this is a great error; on the contrary, one must so bring life together with death that life should have a part of the solemnity and incomprehensibility of death, and death a part of the clarity, simplicity, and comprehensibility of life."

Western scholars begin seriously to take an interest in L. N., and at the end of the nineteenth and the beginning of the twentieth century there appears a whole series of monographs on Tolstoy in every possible language. In 1900 there came out a very interesting book in German, by Eltzbacher, a doctor of juridical science, under the title *Anarchism*. In this book, with the seriousness proper to German scholars, the teachings of the seven most renowned anarchists are analysed and set forth, among them that of Lev Tolstoy as well. The author of this book sent his work to Lev Nikolaevich, and the latter answered him with a letter of thanks. Here are its essential parts:

> "Your book does for anarchism that which thirty years ago was done for socialism: it introduces it into the programme of the political sciences.
>
> Your book pleased me extremely. It is perfectly objective, intelligible, and, so far as I can judge, in it the sources are admirably worked up. It seems to me only that I am not an anarchist in the sense of a political reformer. In the index to your book, at the word 'compulsion,' there are made references to the pages of the works of all the other authors you analyse, but there is not to be met a single reference to my writings. Is not this a proof that the teaching which you ascribe to me, but which in reality is only the teaching of Christ, is a teaching not at all political, but religious?"

And in another form too L. N. interests the European scholars: he is elected an honorary member of the French ethnographic society. At Breslau (in Prussian Silesia) there was formed an international union in the name of L. N. Tolstoy. The aim of the society is the dissemination among its members and among the people of the principles of moral perfecting in the spirit of the ideas of the first centuries of Christianity (Urchristenthums). Persons of both sexes who have reached the age of eighteen may enter the society. All political and religious aspirations are excluded from its programme. The draft of the statutes contains only seven paragraphs of a very general content. The authors of the statutes explain the general character of the provisions by the wish to give it an international tinge, in order to have the possibility of asking the confirmation of the statutes in any state. With great warmth the founders explain the necessity of the dissemination of Tolstoy's ideas. "The peculiar personality of the aged Russian writer and philosopher rivets, with an irresistible force, the gaze of every thinking man. As strong in spirit as in the nobility of his world-view, he holds sway over almost all the intellectual and literary development of contemporary Russia. An altruist, demanding nothing for himself personally, the personification of gentleness and capable of self-sacrifice, L. N., with his prophetic figure, at one and the same time warns us and instructs us. To more than one of us, in the hours of wavering of conscience, there has risen before us his kind face, overgrown with grey hair, calling us onto the true path," and so on.

In such features is L. N.'s personality outlined. As regards its practical aims, the society strives toward the dissemination of the philosophical and ethical ideas of L. N. Tolstoy, and with this aim, besides other means of influence, had it in view to publish an organ, in which there might be placed articles of "the outstanding authorities of Tolstoyan literature" and his works might be commented upon. Inquiries and other particulars concerning the new society those interested may obtain from the secretary (Breslau, Fridrichstrasse, 75).

Unfortunately, we did not succeed in establishing connection with this likeable undertaking. After the worldwide carnage of the last war, has this group of good people held together, and does it continue to strive to realize the great ideal? Time will show this, and if this group has not succeeded in doing it, then others will arise and will go along the same path.

In September of this year L. N. is again ailing and falls into a gloomy mood. But his spiritual life does not stop. Here are a few thoughts from his diary:

> "Today is 22 September 1900. Yasnaya Polyana. All this time I have worked badly. And what I worked at was an empty matter. Galya Ch. writes, asking whether I will not let two beginnings of the appeal be printed. I began to look them over and worked at this all the while. In one I wrote in something not bad, on the theme that the Christian peoples have no religion of any kind. All the time I am in a very bad, unkind frame of mind. The recollection that God is within me no longer helps. I have been at Masha's and at my brother Sergei's. It was very good at Andryusha's. I await something. Yet there is nothing to await, except work, good godly work, and death. My health is feeble. Of late, anguish, shivering, and fever. At this minute, eleven o'clock at night, it is well with me. Tanya has gone away. Today there is a dear letter from her.
>
> I thought at first that the proposition that the capacity for learning is a sign of stupidity was a paradox, and I particularly did not believe it because I learned badly; but now I am convinced that it is true and cannot be otherwise. In order to take in the thoughts of others, one must have none of one's own. Somnambulists learn best of all."

At times moments of repentance came upon L. N., and he recalls with horror his past:

> "What was important during these days was that — I no longer remember on what occasion, it seems after an inner accusation of my sons — I began to recall all my vile deeds. I vividly recalled all, or at least much — and was horrified. How much better is the life of others and of my sons than mine. I have not to be proud of the past, nor even of the present, but to humble myself, to be ashamed, to hide myself, to ask forgiveness of people (I wrote 'of God,' and then struck it out). Before God I am less guilty than before people: He made me, allowed me to be such. The one consolation is that I was never wicked; on my conscience there are two or three acts which tormented me at the time, but cruel I was not. But all the same I am a loathsome reptile. And how good it is to know and to remember this. At once one becomes kinder toward people, and that is the chief thing, the one thing needful."

And again he speaks in his favourite paradoxical language:

> "If a man is always talking about the poetic, know that he is devoid of poetic feeling. The same of religion, of science (I used to love to talk of science); of goodness — he is wicked."

To his daughter Marya Lvovna he writes in a jesting tone:

> "…These last days the literary folk have come thick and fast. It began with Veselitskaya, then the young Marxist Totomyants, from the *Severny Kurier*, then Posse, the editor of *Zhizn*, then Gorky, then Nemirovich-Danchenko. This is all because the rumour has gone round that I have written a drama, whereas I have only sketched one out."

On this visit of Gorky's, Sofya Andreyevna succeeded in taking his photograph beside L. N. This was exceedingly pleasant to Gorky.

On 17 October L. N. goes to his daughter T. L. at Kochety and lives there until the beginning of November, when he moves to Moscow. While living at Kochety, L. N. was writing an epistle to the Chinese.

His wish to write an epistle to the Chinese L. N. explains in a letter to Chertkov:

> Today, reading of the punishments inflicted on the Chinese in the name of Christianity, I conceived a dreadful longing to write an epistle to the Chinese, in which to tell them that those Christians whom they know and who torment them are impostors and slander the Christians, and that Christ would love them and would help them."

And in the next letter he adds:

> "You mention the Chinese address. Thank you… I think I wrote to you that the chief thing in this is, seriously, the slander upon the Christianity by which I live — a slander that is not only revolting, but that wounds me painfully. It is unpleasant when under my name they print some unclear, weak, vulgar article; but what is it like when, under the name of 'Christianity,' they preach the most dreadful demands."

Of the thoughts recorded by him during this time we shall give the most characteristic:

> "Life is a passage from one form into another — and therefore self-renunciation, that is, the going out from one's own form, is not enough; one must form a new form. The life of this world is the material for this new form.
>
> I thought that if one is to serve people by writing, then the one thing to which I have a right, the thing I ought to do, is to expose the rich in their wrongdoing and to lay open to the poor the deceit in which they are kept."

In November, living in Moscow, L. N. continues to think of the epistle to the Chinese and takes up again their religious teaching, reads and translates Confucius, and in his diary thus sums up this teaching:

> The essence of the Chinese teaching is this: the true (great) teaching teaches men the highest good, the renewal of men, and the abiding in this state. In order to possess the highest good, it is needful: 1) that there should be good order among all the people; in order that there should be good order among all the people, it is needful: 2) that there should be good order in the family; in order that there should be good order in the family, it is needful: 3) that there should be good order in oneself; in order that there should be good order in oneself, it is needful: 4) that the heart should be set right (pure?). For where your treasure shall be, there will your heart be also. In order that the heart should be set right (pure?), there is needful: 5) the consciousness (truthfulness) of thought. In order that there should be consciousness of thought, there is needful: 6) the highest degree of knowledge. In order that there should be knowledge, there is needful: 7) the study of oneself (as one commentator explains it).
>
> All things have a root and its consequences; all matters have an end and a beginning. To know what is the most important, what ought to be first and what last, is that which the true teaching teaches. The perfecting of man is the beginning of everything. If the root be in neglect, then that which is to grow out of it cannot be good."

Further he sets forth this teaching in detail, recording this exposition in his diary. Here too thoughts of another kind are to be met with:

> "Disclose to people the truth, like the Gospel, which is to save them and to deliver them from evil — and, save unpleasantness, nothing will come to the discloser. Write a play, or better still a bawdy novel — and you will be showered with flowers, with praise, with money. True, someone has said that the more intelligent crowd is children. They are very fond of saying of the people that they are children, whereas the children are precisely they — the rich, ruling classes."

The Chinese teaching continues to interest him, and a few days later he again records:

> "I am occupied with Confucius, and everything else seems perfectly insignificant. It seems pretty good. The chief thing is that this teaching — that one must be especially attentive to oneself when alone — acts upon me strongly and beneficently. If only it would keep itself in the same freshness."

And further he records a thought indicating his strong spiritual work:

> "My God, how hard it is to live only before God — to live as men live who are buried in a mine and know that they will not come out of it and that no one will ever learn how they lived there. And yet one must, one must so live, because only such a life is life. Help me, O Lord."

The whole of November L. N. spent in Moscow, surrounded by numerous visitors.

At the end of November he records the following important thought:

> "We, the rich classes, ruin the workers, keep them in coarse, unceasing toil, ourselves enjoying leisure and luxury. We do not give them, crushed by toil, the possibility of producing the spiritual flower and fruit of life: neither poetry, nor science, nor religion. We undertake to give them all this — and we give them a false poetry — 'Why hast thou sped away to the fatal Caucasus,' and the like — science: jurisprudence, Darwinism, philosophy, the history of tsars; religion: the Church faith. What a dreadful sin! If only we did not suck them dry, they would manifest both poetry, and science, and a teaching of life."

At the end of November L. N. was visited by a remarkable man, the Dutchman Engelberg, who had come to him with his young friend. The mother of this man was a Malay, and his father a Dutchman, and his appearance — a swarthy, expressive face, black hair — betrayed his mixed origin. He occupied an important administrative post in Dutch India, on the island of Java. Professing the teaching that denies violence, he knew how, by his strong will and his fearlessness, astonishingly to subdue, without the application of reprisals, the riotous outbreaks of the natives. The Dutch government knew this and valued him highly and sent him on dangerous expeditions. He was satisfied that by his presence he always averted armed intervention in the affairs of the natives, but he was tormented by the fact that his activity served to strengthen the power of the metropolis and consequently, in the end, served violence. It was for the resolution of these and other doubts that he went to L. N., having taken a year's leave in order to rest and to ponder his course of action. The meeting with L. N. was for him a source of great joy. In a letter to his friend Mirop, also a like-minded man, he describes this meeting in the most rapturous notes and with great seriousness. Of course, L. N. left to his conscience the matter of deciding the chief question, whether to continue or to leave the administrative activity in the colonies; but communion with L. N. strengthened Engelberg in his world-view.

In connection with this visit, we find in L. N.'s diary a note that he is reading the Gospel in Dutch and is astonished at the new sense that opens to him on reading it in an unaccustomed form.

In a letter to me after this visit L. N. communicates:

> "I have come to love Engelberg very much; he will soon be with you."

To see him, however, after his return from Russia, I did not succeed. He had often been at my house before the journey and had left in me the most joyful impression. But he related and described in detail, in a letter, his meeting with L. N. to his friend Mirop, who also printed his account in the Dutch journal *Vrede*, whence we borrow these particulars.

December finds L. N. still in Moscow and busy with a new care for the Doukhobors. Recording this in his diary, he adds a few lines vividly portraying his spiritual state:

> "8 December. During this time I received a letter from Canada about the wives who wish to go to their husbands in Yakutsk, and I wrote a letter to the Sovereign, but have not yet sent it. I keep trying to be a little better: to destroy the germs of unlove in my heart, but I move forward still very slowly. I can refrain from speaking, from doing — but I cannot speak and act lovingly. I am sinful in this — that on previous days, and especially today, I feel a Sehnsucht (*) for death: to go away from all this tangle, from my own weakness — I will not say my personal weakness, but the weakness arising from the conditions in which it is especially hard to enter a new school. But perhaps this is the very thing that is needful. And it is precisely for this that I am still living — to struggle, here and now, with the evil within myself (and therefore round about myself too). Even, surely, it is so. Help me, Thou who canst help. I weep, for some reason, in writing this. And it is both sad and good. All is impossible, except love. And all the same, as a holiday — precisely as a holiday, a rest — I await death.
>
> From Masha a dear letter. How I love her, and how joyful is the atmosphere of love, and how heavy the reverse one!"

There has been preserved with us the first version of L. N.'s letter to the Sovereign about the wives of the Doukhobors. This version, which was not sent, shows us that even with L. N. the cup of patience overflowed. Here is this letter:

> "Your Imperial Majesty, Sovereign Nikolai Alexandrovich. You surely do not know even a thousandth part of those dreadful, inhuman, godless deeds which are done in your name. And if you do know anything, then it is presented to you in such a distorted form that you do not see all the inhumanity, and often the foolish — rather harmful than useful to the cause which is being defended — cruelty with which they are done.
>
> Of all these criminal deeds the most loathsome and the most revolting to the soul of every honest man are the deeds done by your repulsive, heartless, conscienceless adviser in religious affairs, a villain whose name, as that of a model villain, will pass into history — Pobedonostsev.
>
> Thousands upon thousands of the best, the most highly moral, pure, religious, convinced people, those who make up the strength of the people, have already perished in want and exile and are now perishing only because they are the best people among the people. And how many wives, children of these people have suffered, starved, and died, and are now dying in want and separation by a slow death. The flower of the population not only of the Caucasus, but of Russia — the Doukhobors — notwithstanding all the torments and sufferings — more than 20% of them have died off — have forsaken for ever their fatherland, Russia, recalling with scorn and horror all that they suffered in it. Five thousand Molokans of Kars, as many of Erivan, also the best of the Russian people, the Molokans of Tashkent, the Christians of Kharkov, of Kiev, tens of thousands of people desire only one thing — to leave their fatherland, the land of savage fanaticism, of persecutions and violence, and, having shaken its dust from their feet, to go away to where people are not hindered from confessing God as they understand Him.
>
> I am old, I have not long to live, and I have long been intending, before death, to say this to you: I consider it my duty before God, toward whom I am going. The letter I have received from Canada, which I here enclose, has compelled me, without waiting any longer, to do this. Read this letter; it is short and was not intended for you. From it you will see all and will understand, if you have indeed a kind heart, as they say of you. These unhappy people, and not they alone (there are also exiled the guiltless brothers Verigin, who languish, where they have languished for more than ten years, in the most dreadful places of Siberia), have been exiled to the Yakutsk region. Wives and young women, free, living in plenty, after five years of separation ask, as a mercy, the possibility of sharing with their husbands their sufferings. However hard it may be to believe that you have a kind heart, judging by those horrors which are unceasingly committed in your name — I believe in you. And when you were ill, I was sorry for you, I feared that you would die and that without you it would be worse. I somehow hope in you.
>
> Read this letter yourself and, when there shall have settled in you the feeling of offended, inflated pride which this letter of mine will call forth in you, think — think with your heart (les grandes et les bonnes pensées viennent du cœur) (*) — and do that which this kind heart of yours shall prompt you. Drive away from yourself this wicked and soulless old man Pobedonostsev, who compromises you both before the Russian people, and before Europe, and before history; order to be reviewed and abolished the absurd, unnatural, and shameful laws about persecutions for faith, such as there are in no states whatever and which dishonour those who uphold them; put a stop to all persecutions for faith and bring back all the exiled, the imprisoned, who are punished for confessing that faith which even your advisers do not confess, but only consider that one ought to confess.
>
> (* Great and good thoughts come from the heart. *)
>
> You are bound to do this, because you know that persecution for faith is bad, and you know that tens of thousands of people in your name are subjected, for their faith, to sufferings, and you know that you can put a stop to this order of things. But if you shall not do this, you cannot but feel yourself guilty, you cannot calmly give yourself up to any simple and kind human feeling — neither to love for your family, nor for people; you cannot calmly enjoy any joy, you cannot pray (Matt. V, 23, 24):
>
> "…So if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath aught against thee, leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift."
>
> If I have not been mistaken in you… (three lines were not copied)… it is hard, but afterwards especially joyful.
>
> Forgive me if I have written something amiss. God help you to do that which is pleasing to Him."

This version was corrected, softened, and sent off; unfortunately, after the correction this letter lost a considerable part of its force and sharpness. On 15 December he adds the following in his diary:

> "The event is that Davydov approved the letter to the Sovereign and undertook to send it. I try to remove myself, in my own consciousness, from this affair — only that there should be care for the affair."

Further he sets forth thoughts, so rarely met with in his diary, about his own works and their fate. The more valuable do they become:

> "I passed by a little bookshop and saw — *The Kreutzer Sonata*. I recalled: both *The Kreutzer Sonata*, and *The Power of Darkness*, and even *Resurrection* — I wrote without any thought of preaching to people, of usefulness, and yet this, especially *The Kreutzer Sonata*, brought much benefit. Is it not so with *The Corpse* too?
>
> I think that Schopenhauer's *Parerga and Paralipomenon* is far stronger than his systematic exposition. I have no need (and no time, besides), chiefly, I have no need to write a system. From that which I here record, my view of the world will be made clear, and if it be needful to anyone, then they will make use of it."

Besides the work already mentioned, *Slavery Ancient and Modern*, L. N. in this year wrote also the article *Patriotism and Government* and *Thou Shalt Not Kill*, occasioned by the murder of the Italian king Umberto. And a whole series of letters, which have the character of articles.

In conclusion of this chapter let us give yet one more interesting entry from the December diary:

> "The two most dreadful plagues of our time: Church Christianity, or rather the dogmatic, supernaturalistic Christianity, which is inoculated into people from childhood and maintained hypnotically until death; and physiological, anthropological, and, chiefly, historical materialism, that is, the conviction that everything goes on of itself by mechanical, physical, chemical, biological, and even psychological (in the sense of materialistic psychology) laws, and that therefore all efforts to be good, to do good, are idle, aimless. And this materialism lies in wait for people at their liberation from dogmatic Christianity. No sooner do they free themselves from the immoral falsehood of the Church than they fall into the still worse falsehood of materialism."

At the beginning of December L. N. had some original guests: fifteen American men and two American women.

The end of 1900 L. N. spent in Moscow and recorded on the morning of 1 January 1901: "1 January of the new year and of the new century." This new year, which had now come, brought him many agitations, and to all people — events of world importance.
