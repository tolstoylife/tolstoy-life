## Part IV. 1909–1910. Old Age. The Departure. The End.

I happened that year to visit L. N. several times, and the first thing that struck the eye on meeting him was a kind of radiant, loving, spiritual joy upon his face; and yet his physical powers were becoming ever less and less, and his temporal, earthly life was visibly drawing toward its sunset. This weakening of the physical powers was in part made up for by spiritual energy, so that to a superficial observer it might seem that Lev Nikolaevich was often particularly well and vigorous.

But here is what he himself says about himself in his diary:

> "3 January... I have almost completely lost my memory. The past has vanished. In the future I desire nothing, I await nothing. What could be better than such a state? And I am experiencing this great good. How one ought, without ceasing, to thank God for this wondrous life, free, joyful."

> "10 January. Humanity moves on through millennia, through centuries, and you wish to see this movement in the span of years. It moves by this, that the foremost people little by little alter their surroundings, pointing to an ever-distant perfection, pointing out the path (Christ, Buddha, and also Kant and Emerson and others), and the surroundings little by little are altered."

> "12 January. Just now I thought much about work. And artistic work — 'it was a clear evening, there was a smell of...' — is impossible for me. But work is necessary, because it is obligatory for me. A megaphone has been put into my hands, and I am bound to wield it, to make use of it. Something is pressing to be said; I do not know whether it will succeed. What presses is this — to write outside of any form whatever: not as articles, disquisitions, and not as something artistic, but to utter, to pour out, as best one can, that which one feels strongly. And I feel, with a tormenting strength, the horror, the corrupting power of our situation. I want to write that which I should like to do, and how I picture to myself that I would act. God help me. I cannot but pray. I am sorry that I pray so little."

On 20 January L. N. was visited by the Tula archbishop Parfeny. News of this strange visit got into the newspapers, and from Moscow there came to L. N. a correspondent of *Russkoye Slovo*, Spiro. L. N., wishing that no false rumors should go about concerning Parfeny's visit, related to this correspondent the following:

> "In Tula there lives a general, Kun, to whom the Tula archbishop Parfeny had said that he should like to come to me and have a talk with me. Kun told this to Chertkov, and Chertkov passed it on to me. And the archbishop, it appears, said that the one thing he did not know was whether I should wish to receive him, and that he was afraid that if I did receive him, I should 'talk him round'... For these words, however, I cannot vouch, since I heard them at third hand...
>
> On one of my usual walks, — Lev Nikolaevich continued, — I went to the school and told the schoolmistress that if the archbishop should come and should wish to come from the school to me — I should be glad to see him.
>
> On the day of his visit to the school I lay down to sleep at my usual time, at 5 o'clock, before dinner, and slept longer than usual.
>
> At last my wife woke me and said that the archbishop had already been here about an hour — he had arrived, it turned out, soon after I had fallen asleep.
>
> With him were two priests, the parish priest and the district one, the inspector of schools.
>
> I came out and found, with pleasure, that the first meeting passed off without awkwardness: without giving a blessing, the archbishop rose and gave me his hand.
>
> He acted in the same way with all the household, too.
>
> After some general, insignificant conversation, I invited him into my room and told him that I receive many letters and visits from clerical persons, and that I am always touched by the good wishes which they express, and by his visit too, but that I always very much regret that it is as impossible for me — as to fly up into the air — to fulfill their wishes.
>
> Then I said to him: one thing is unpleasant to me, that all these persons reproach me with destroying the faith of people.
>
> Here there is a great misunderstanding, since my whole activity in this respect is directed only toward delivering people from the unnatural and ruinous condition of the absence of any faith whatsoever.
>
> Among other things, in proof of this, I read to him from the *Circle of Reading* compiled by me, for 20 January, the very day on which our meeting had chanced to take place. In that day there were beautiful passages from Channing, Emerson, Thoreau, and especially Kant."

Here are those thoughts:

> "Christianity establishes the immediate communion of man with God.
>
> 1
>
> You ask, wherein lies the chief essence of the character of Christ, the Savior of the world. I answer that it is his assurance of the greatness of the human soul. He saw in man the reflection and the image of the Divinity, and therefore he thirsted for his redemption and loved man, whoever he might be, whatever the conditions of his life and character. Jesus looked upon people with a gaze penetrating the material covering — the body vanished before him. He looked through the attire of the rich man and the rags of the beggar into the soul of man; and there, amid the gloom of ignorance and the stains of sin, he found a spiritual, immortal nature and the germs of power and perfection which can develop infinitely. In the most lowly fallen, depraved man he saw a being who might be transformed into an angel of light. Channing.
>
> 2
>
> For peoples, as for individuals, deliverance from prejudices does not diminish the moral barriers, but only replaces the coarse obstacles with finer ones. Many poor souls lose their support in the process. But in this there is nothing bad or dangerous. It is only growth. A child must learn to walk alone. At first a man who has been deprived of his accustomed superstition feels himself lost, homeless... But this taking away from him of external supports drives him inward into himself, and he feels himself grown stronger. He feels himself face to face with the majestic presence of God. He reads, not in a book, but in his soul the very original of the Ten Commandments, of the Gospel, and of the Epistles. And his little chapel widens into the majestic cathedral of the vault of heaven. Emerson.
>
> 3
>
> The cognition of God may be either speculative, and such cognition is unreliable and subject to dangerous errors, or moral, proceeding from faith, and such cognition conceives no other qualities of God than those which condition morality. Such faith is natural and supernatural. Kant.
>
> 4
>
> Seek not only the moral life, but strive toward that which is higher than morality. Thoreau.
>
> Fear all that comes between you and God the Spirit, whose image and likeness lives in your soul."

Having read these thoughts, Lev Nikolaevich continued his account:

> "I saw that this reading made a good impression upon him, which was very pleasant to me.
>
> But, in spite of this, he nevertheless expressed to me the reproach that my activity destroys the faith of people.
>
> Then I told him of a long-ago incident, very insignificant in outward appearance and very important in its inner meaning for me.
>
> Late at night in winter I went out to take a walk and, going through the village, where all the lights had already been put out, passing by one house in which a light was shining, I glanced in at the window and saw, kneeling and praying, the old woman Matryona, known to me from her youth, one of the most vicious, dissolute women of the village. I was struck by this outward look of her prayerful state.
>
> I looked, walked on, but, turning back, glanced in at the window and found Matryona in the same position. She was praying and making prostrations to the ground and raising her face to the icons.
>
> This — is prayer. God grant that we all may pray in the same way, that is, may be conscious in the same way of our dependence upon God — and to violate that faith which calls forth such prayer I should consider the greatest of crimes... And indeed it is impossible. No sages could do this.
>
> But it is not so with people of our educated condition — among them there is either no faith at all, or, what is still worse, a pretense of faith, of a faith which plays the role only of a certain propriety.
>
> And therefore I have considered, and do consider, it necessary to point out to all those who have no faith that a man cannot live without it, and to deliver those whose faith is false, external, from that which conceals for them the necessity of the true faith.
>
> The archbishop made no objection to this, but repeated that it is not good to destroy faith."

L. N. presented the archbishop with a *Circle of Reading* bearing his autograph and a packet of postcards with reproductions of Chertkov's photographs. As he took leave and pressed his hand, L. N. said: "Once more I thank you for your courage," and wept. This visit was very pleasant to him.

In his diary, after the archbishop's visit, L. N. makes the following entry:

> "Yesterday the archbishop was here. I spoke with him from the heart, but too cautiously; I did not utter the whole sin of his calling. And I ought to have. What spoiled it for me was Sonya's account of his conversation with her. He, evidently, would have wished to convert me; if not to convert me, then to destroy, to diminish my — in their opinion — pernicious influence upon faith in the church. It is especially unpleasant that he asked to be let know when I should be dying. Lest they should contrive something or other to assure people that I had 'repented' before death. And therefore I declare — it seems I am repeating it — that to return to the church, to take communion before death, I can as little do as I can, before death, speak smutty words or look at smutty pictures, and therefore all that may be said about my deathbed repentance and communion is a lie. I say this because, if there are people for whom, according to their religious understanding, communion is a certain religious act, that is, a manifestation of the striving toward God, then for me any such external action, such as communion, would be a renunciation of the soul, of good, of the teaching of Christ, of God.
>
> I repeat on this occasion, too, that I ask to be buried likewise without so-called divine service, but to have my body interred in the earth, so that it shall not stink."

In the last years, refusals of military service in various parts of Russia began to repeat themselves ever more and more often. The tsarist government dealt cruelly with these men. For the most part these men fell into the disciplinary battalions and there were subjected to every kind of torment, moral and physical. Some even perished there. But the enormous majority bore all these torments courageously and held high the light of the true life which they had kindled. L. N. carried on a correspondence with many of them, supporting them with his sympathy in their hard struggle, and at last he wrote them a general greeting of the following content:

## A Greeting to Those Who Have Refused Military Service

Greetings to you, suffering for our common cause, the cause of all humanity, brothers!

Unhappy, erring people, not understanding what they do, torment you, and millions, millions of people diligently close their eyes to what is being done with you, in order not to see that simple and clear question which, whether they wish it or not, stands now with such vividness before all humanity, and especially before that part of it which calls itself Christian.

You are few, you are units among millions, but the strength is not on their side, but on yours, and they dimly sense this, and therefore strive not to see, not to acknowledge the question, to make as though there were no question at all; but you, by your direct answer to the question, an answer confirmed by you through suffering, so illumine the question that they can no longer help seeing it.

The simple and clear question is, after all, this: which of the two laws do you acknowledge as binding upon yourself — you, every man of our time: the law of God, that is, of your own conscience, or the law of man, that is, the law of the state? Do you believe the Code of Laws or the Gospel? Which commands do you consider more binding upon yourself: the commands of God or those of the military regulations?

The erring people thought to get away from this question, wished to conceal it, to hush it up, but you, by your clear answer to the question, have inescapably set it before all. It is no longer possible to pretend that there is no question, but one must, in one way or another, answer it. And by this very thing, few though you are, let them torment you to death, the victory will inevitably remain on your side, on the side of truth, of good, of plain common sense. By this very thing, so great, so enormous is that deed which you are doing by your sufferings, so unnoticed by worldly life, and it is just this that we, who share your views, who rejoice over you, who are heartened by you, wish to express to you.

Do not think that the aim of this epistle, which I write not only in my own name, but also in the name of all those who feel toward you the same as I do — and of such, as I know, there are very many — do not think that our aim was the desire to support your strength in the trial that lies before you. We know that you cannot be guided and encouraged by the opinion of people, since you do what you do only because you consider it, before God, the work of your life, and you will equally do what you do, independently of whether people praise or censure you for what you do.

Neither think this — that we, fearing lest you, by your defection, should weaken the force of the great work being accomplished by you, not only for all of us, but also for all future generations, should wish to confirm you in your decision. We are far from this thought. We know that the great work which you are doing will, sooner or later, but inevitably, be accomplished, and therefore, however weak the bodily powers of some of you may be, however brief your exposure of the deception and your struggle with it, we value this struggle equally and love you for it, whether it has lasted days or years.

And therefore our aim in this address to you is one: to express to you — both to those who have only of late been subjected to bondage and violence, and to those who, with unshakable firmness, calm, and forgiveness toward their tormentors, have borne and are bearing these trials for years — our aim is one: to express those feelings of ours of respect, of love, and of gratitude toward you for that spiritual liberation which you so self-sacrificingly and faithfully serve by your bodily sufferings.

God help you, with your former meekness, patience, and forgiveness toward those unhappy, erring people who vainly strive to do you evil, to bear the sufferings you have voluntarily taken upon yourselves.
With my whole soul, loving and honoring you, L. Tolstoy.

10 February 1909. Yasnaya Polyana.

Nik. Nik. Gusev adds that, although he had no hope that this address would reach them, he nevertheless sent it round to all those who had refused, whose addresses he had. Afterward it came to light that only two of those to whom it had been delivered, evading the administration, received this address.

The most varied visitors flocked at this time to L. N. from all the lands of the world. On the 12th of February there came to him Vaisov, the leader of a Mohammedan sect in Kazan. Here is what L. N. related about him to one of his visitors, even before his arrival:

> "In Kazan there is such a thing as a 'Regiment of God,' a Tatar Mohammedan sect. At its head stands a certain Vaisov. Yesterday I received a letter from him, saying that his views have much in common with those which I express, consequently with Christianity as I understand it, and that he wishes to come to me. This interests me in the highest degree. With them one of the basic propositions is that the faith ought to be one and the same for all people. This is one Mohammedan sect, while the second, in Persia, are the Babists, or Babids. They are the followers of Bahá, who was the continuer of the Báb. I had the joy that one of these Bahaists came to me. He is not very intelligent, but all his beliefs are such that I subscribe to them with both hands. Especially dear is this trait, both in the Kazan ones and in these: that they acknowledge the necessity of a single religion. In essence, when one comes to one's senses, one is always astonished how it is that such a simple reflection does not come into one's head: there live an Orthodox man, a Catholic, a Buddhist, people believe in this, consider it the truth, but cross a certain boundary — and they consider that to be a lie, and this to be the truth. How is it that this does not make one doubt, does not make one seek that religion common to all?"

N. N. Gusev managed to record L. N.'s conversation with Vaisov; here is how he conveys it:

> L. N. I took the liberty of telling you that in the Koran I, at least, cannot accept everything that is written in it.
>
> Vaisov. In the Koran the interpretations are incorrect, the translation is clumsy. He who translates it does not approach it with love, in order to make the truth clear; he has in his heart another feeling. From this, too, much remains that is not understood... With us all things are founded upon the Koran.
>
> L. N. I think that man has something higher than the Koran. In man's soul there is God, who shows him what is good and what is bad. But the Koran is the work of human hands. Between God and me there stands the Koran, if I am to obey the Koran; but if I obey the voice of God, then he is directly in my soul, I commune directly with God. But here there is the Koran. The Koran is the work of human hands, just as is our Gospel. For people made them, and people err. You speak of translations, but here too there might have been errors. One cannot regard the Koran in such a way that every word in it is true and from God. He who believes in God will by no means say that God could express himself in a little book. God will not fit into a little book.
>
> Vaisov agreed with this, but said:
>
> — The Koran is a guide for man.
>
> L. N. Just in the same way as I read thoughts in Muhammad and found much that was useful for myself, so you too know that you have found much in the Gospel. Today I read a Hindu book — for the soul there is much that is useful too. Who, then, chooses? I choose. Whatever the case, I must choose. We are told that we must choose nothing, but believe in whatever we are bidden to believe. But this is impossible. For here are the Kazan Mohammedans, who believe in one thing, while you in your own way. Why? Because you have decided; therefore it is not the little book that has decided, but you.
>
> Then L. N. read aloud, translating them from the English, several sayings from that very Hindu book (of Ramakrishna) of which he had spoken to Vaisov. Here are those sayings:
>
> 1) "A boat may stand in the water, but the water must not stand in the boat. He who desires the spiritual life may live in the world, but the world must not live in him."
>
> 2) "Knowledge has an entrance only into the outer chambers, into the outer rooms of God, but love can enter into the very innermost chambers."
>
> 3) "To discourse of God only because one has read the scripture — it is just the same as to discourse to another of the city of Benares only because one has seen it in a picture."
>
> 4) "Be not a traitor to your thoughts, be sincere and act in accordance with your thoughts, and you will surely succeed."
>
> 5) "How is one to get rid of one's self? If you cannot in any way get rid of it, make of your self a servant."
>
> After this L. N. read two thoughts of Muhammad, translating them from an English collection of his sayings:
>
> 1) "Paradise lies at the feet of the mother."
>
> 2) "Do you know that which undermines the foundations of Islam and destroys it? The errors of the learned destroy it, and the disputes of the hypocrites, and the orders of the rulers who have lost the true path."
>
> — How good that is, — said L. N., having read this saying."

Mohammedanism, in its free, non-canonical exposition, keenly interested L. N. Here is what he read to one of his visitors from the English book *Thoughts of Muhammad Not Included in the Koran*. Noticing that the visitor had become absorbed in reading the manuscript, which represented a translation of these thoughts, L. N. came up to him and said:

— Ah, you are reading Muhammad. Beautiful thoughts. Have you read about prayer? He asks God to grant him poverty: "O Lord. Keep me in poverty during my life and let me die a poor man." For the first time I meet with such a prayer. And have you read about how Muhammad's physician wished to kill him?

And, taking the manuscript, L. N. read:

> "Muhammad was sleeping under a palm tree and, suddenly awaking, saw before him his physician Dyutur, with a sword raised over him. 'Well, Muhammad, who will save you now from death?' cried Dyutur. — 'God,' answers Muhammad. Dyutur lowered the sword. Muhammad snatched it from him and in his turn cried: 'Dyutur, who will save you now from death?' — 'No one,' answered Dyutur. — 'Then know that the same God will save you,' said Muhammad, returning him the sword. And Dyutur became one of the most faithful friends of the prophet."

— Why, this is remarkable! — exclaimed L. N. — Where is your support? Outside of God there is none.

On the 6th of February N. N. Gusev makes this entry:

> "Today there arrived the man of letters Kupchinsky, who lives abroad. The chief aim of his visit was to propose to L. N. that he write an article against capital punishment. He told L. N. that the Moscow weekly newspaper *Zhizn* (Life) was willing to print everything that L. N. should write against capital punishment, without any cuts, however sharp it might be. L. N. at first told Kupchinsky that he could hardly write anything just now on this subject, and I had already brought Kupchinsky a complete copy of *I Cannot Be Silent*, in order to select together with him the suitable passages from it for printing in the newspaper, when L. N., having returned from his walk, without looking in on us in the dining room, went straight to his room and wrote a short article against capital punishment, which he then gave to Kupchinsky. Kupchinsky kept his word.
>
> This note was soon printed in the form of a facsimile autograph, under the title 'There Is No Evil Without Some Good' (so the note began). For the printing of it the editor was subjected to a fine of 3,000 rubles or arrest for 3 months. As he had not the means to pay, he had to choose the latter."

Here is the text of this note:

## There Is No Evil Without Some Good

There is no evil — without some good.

So there is also a good side to those terrible crimes against all laws, divine and human, to those killings which, without ceasing and ever more frequently, are committed under the name of capital punishments by people called the government.

The good side is in this — that before every man there is set, directly and irrevocably, the question: in what does he believe — in God, or at least in the human conscience, or in the state and in all that shall be prescribed in its name? It is terrible to say: the majority of what is called the higher estate acknowledges as binding the subordination of the law of God, of the demands of conscience, to the law of the state and its demands. However strenuously, and — terrible to say — successfully, the corruption of the so-called lower estates proceeds, on them is the one hope. One cannot believe that the simple, illiterate, uneducated, that is, uncorrupted Russian people would barter God for the state, the Gospel for the code of laws, and the articles "Thou shalt not kill" and "Love your enemies" for Article 129 or some other articles of such-and-such sections. It is time for the people to come to its senses, and the people will come to its senses.
Lev Tolstoy.

Yasnaya Polyana, 6 February 1909.

In L. N.'s service to his neighbors, as always, a large share was taken up by his correspondence with his friends; let us adduce here one remarkable contemporary letter, written by L. N. to his friend M. S. Dudchenko, of whom we have already spoken at the beginning of this volume. The subject of this letter is extraordinarily important: L. N.'s opinion of the agricultural communes. In this letter L. N.'s attitude toward the communes becomes perfectly clear: he considers the communal form of life the highest, and above it he sets only the vagrant life, but by no means the life of one's own little household.

Here is that remarkable letter:

> "I have received your good letter, dear M. S., and, however strange it may seem to you, I am completely in agreement with you in that general significance which you ascribe to the commune, and especially to the striving of people toward union, manifesting itself in the commune. If I have pointed out the temptations inherent in this form of life, that by no means shows that I find this form incorrect and unprofitable. A disadvantageous side and certain temptations are inherent in every ordering of life. It may very well be that I pointed out these temptations with especial sharpness, because I myself have not experienced this kind of life and am aware of all the wrongness and falsity of that form of life in which I continue to live. One thing on which I insist, and which becomes ever clearer and clearer to me with the years, is that danger of the weakening of the inner spiritual work when energy — effort — is transferred from the inner sphere to the outer.
>
> In general, the communal form of life can be condemned only by those people who live in a form of life more in accordance with the Christian and moral cast than the communal. But I know of no such, except that one life of the homeless vagrant, which is the most natural of all to a man wishing fully to fulfill the teaching of Christ.
>
> And therefore I consider that I have no right to condemn the communal form; all that I have said about this was only a pointing out of those temptations which are inherent in it."

At this time there was approaching in Russia a new literary festival — the jubilee of Gogol, the 100th anniversary of his birth. L. N., wishing to fulfill the request of the editor of a journal sympathetic to him, V. A. Posse, set about rereading Gogol.

— How glad I am that I am rereading Gogol, — he said to Gusev. — I am now reading the *Correspondence with Friends*. Side by side with banalities there are such profound religious truths.

The next day he said:

— I want to write about Gogol. This superstition of art, as of something especially important, completely took possession of him. *The Marriage* — the whole play is foolish, tactless, and here all at once they write with importance: "one word could not be made out..." This is the fruit of our idle life.

To Gusev's question about the further development of Gogol's worldview, L. N. said:

— Afterward he accepted the whole of religion, as it is, in a childlike way, submitted, without choosing what he needed from it and what he did not need.

After this conversation L. N. dictated to Gusev the following article:

> "Gogol — an enormous talent, a beautiful heart, and a small, timid, faint-hearted mind.
>
> He gives himself up to his talent — and there come forth beautiful literary works, such as *Old-World Landowners*, the first part of *Dead Souls*, *The Inspector-General*, and especially — the height of perfection in its kind — *The Carriage*. He gives himself up to his heart, to 'religious feeling' — and there come forth, in his letters, such as the letter 'On the Significance of Illnesses,' 'On What the Word Is,' and in many, many others, touching, often profound and instructive thoughts. But the moment he wishes to write artistic works on religious-moral themes, or to give to works already written a moral-religious instructive meaning not proper to them — there comes forth terrible, repulsive nonsense, as this manifests itself in the second part of *Dead Souls*, in the concluding scene to *The Inspector-General*, and chiefly in the letters.
>
> This comes about because, on the one hand, Gogol ascribes to art a high significance not proper to it, and on the other — still less proper to religion — the low significance of church faith, and wishes to explain this imagined high significance of his works by this church faith. If Gogol, on the one hand, had simply loved to write tales, comedies, and had occupied himself with this, without ascribing to these occupations any special Hegelian, priestly significance, and, on the other hand, had simply acknowledged church teaching and the state order as something with which he had no need to dispute, and which there was no ground to justify, then he would have gone on writing his very good stories and comedies and would, on occasion, have uttered in his letters, and perhaps in separate works too, his often very profound, heart-felt moral-religious thoughts. But, unfortunately, at the time when Gogol entered the literary world, especially after the death not only of the enormous talent, but also of the cheerful, clear, unentangled Pushkin, there reigned, in relation to art — I cannot say it otherwise — an incredibly foolish teaching of Hegel, according to which it came out that to build houses, to sing songs, to paint pictures, and to write tales, comedies, and verses constitutes a kind of sacred rite, a 'service to beauty,' standing only one step below religion — a service that continues to have significance even after religion has been acknowledged as something outlived and unnecessary.
>
> Simultaneously with this teaching there was disseminated at that same time another, no less absurd and no less entangled and bombastic — the teaching of Slavophilism about some special significance of the Russian, that is, of that people to which those who reasoned belonged, and at the same time about some special, exclusive significance of Orthodoxy.
>
> Gogol, though with little consciousness, assimilated both teachings. The teaching about the special significance of art he naturally assimilated, because it ascribed great importance to his activity; the other, the Slavophile teaching, also could not but attract him, since, justifying all that exists, it soothed and flattered his self-love.
>
> And Gogol assimilated both teachings and tried to combine them in application to his own writing. And from this attempt there came forth those astonishing absurdities which so strike one in his letters of the latter time."

Lev Nikolaevich handed this article and these marks to the *Russkoye Slovo* correspondent Spiro, who thereupon asked him:

— What is your opinion, Lev Nikolaevich, of the honoring of Gogol?

— I can in no way sympathize with this honoring, just as I cannot sympathize with my own, since I cannot ascribe to art in general that significance which is accepted in our so-called higher, but in reality lower in its moral cast, society. And therefore, in my opinion, if by some miracle everything that is called art and artistry were to collapse, to be annihilated, then humanity would lose nothing. Even if it were to lose some good works, then in return it would be delivered from that terrible, pernicious trash which now irrepressibly grows and floods over it.

Having said this and smiling good-naturedly, Lev Nikolaevich added:

— Well, it seems a good pretext for abusing me...

To this same time belongs an interesting appraisal by L. N. of the novel by A. I. Ertel, *The Gardenins*. This appraisal L. N. expressed in a letter, which served as a preface to the 5th volume of the collected works of A. I. Ertel:

> "In connection with the complete collected works of the late Alexander Ivanovich Ertel, now being published, I was asked to write a few words about his writings. I was very glad of this occasion to reread *The Gardenins*. In spite of ill health and occupations, having begun to read this book, I could not tear myself away until I had read it all and had reread some passages several times over.
>
> The chief merit, apart from the seriousness of his attitude toward the matter, apart from such a knowledge of the life of the people as I know in no other writer, apart from the strong love for the people, often not realized by the author himself, whom he sometimes wishes to portray in a dark light — the inimitable merit of this novel, met with nowhere else, is the astonishing, in its fidelity, beauty, variety, and force, popular language. Such a language you will not find either in the old or in the new writers. Not only is his popular language faithful, strong, beautiful, it is infinitely varied. The old house-serf speaks in one language, the artisan — in another, the young lad — in a third, the women — in a fourth, the girls — again in another. With some writer they counted the number of words used by him. I think that with Ertel this number, especially of popular words, would be the greatest of all the Russian writers, and what faithful, good, strong words they are, words used nowhere except among the people, and nowhere are these words underlined, nowhere is their exceptional quality exaggerated, nowhere is there felt that which so often happens, that the author wishes to show off, to astonish with some little word he has overheard. To Ertel it seems more natural to speak in the popular than in the literary language.
>
> In reading Ertel's popular scenes, you forget that you are reading an author; it seems that you are living with the people: you see not only all the weaknesses of the people, but also all those merits of theirs, surpassing those weaknesses an incalculable number of times, and chiefly — its untouched and, until now, not revolutionary, but religious force, upon which alone one can now, in Russia, set one's hopes.
>
> And therefore for one who loves the people, the reading of Ertel is a great pleasure. For one who wishes to learn to know the people without living among them, this reading is the very best means. For one, then, who wishes to learn to know the popular language — not the ancient one, which no one now speaks, and not the new one, which, thank God, only a few of the people speak, but that real, strong, where it needs to be — tender, touching, where it needs to be — severe, serious, where it needs to be — passionate, where it needs to be — lively and brisk language of the people, which, thank God, the enormous majority of the people still speak, especially the women, the old women — for him there is need not only to read, but to study the popular language of Ertel."

At this time the persecutions of L. N.'s friends grew stronger. First Chertkov suffered. He was expelled from the bounds of Tula Province for "harmful activity." No petitions or protests availed to revoke this order. Of course, this expulsion caused L. N. great grief.

Then a serious chastisement befell the writer of these lines too. I, who was living at that time in Kostroma, had a search made at my town apartment and at my country estate, and as a fair quantity of L. N.'s pamphlets of the "Osvobozhdenie" edition were found at my place — namely: *The End of the Age*, *Thou Shalt Not Kill* — I was given over to trial, a whole series of charges being preferred against me under Articles 103, 104, 129, and 132. Of course, I informed L. N. of this and received from him a reply:

> "2 April 1909. I did not expect, dear friend Pavel Ivanovich, that your affair would take such a turn.
>
> From the worldly point of view — this is offensive; from the Christian one, as you yourself write — only joyful. But it is hard for the family, and that is what pains me. Joyful to me is especially the consciousness that I have such friends as you. It may very well be that the moving cause of the affair is in Kostroma, but it seems to me that the starting point, both in relation to you, and to Chertkov, and to others, is more central. The whole building, that has stood so long, is being destroyed, and one must somehow both prop it up and fence it round. So I think sometimes, but in good moments, driving away such thoughts about the general external matter, I strive only to remember that I myself should do as little evil as possible.
>
> You know this and feel it and do it better than I. God help you.
>
> Please, let me know if there is anything new. Is there any hope of seeing you soon?
>
> A hearty greeting to your dear wife and sister-in-law."

In this same month of March there was arranged an exhibition in the name of L. N. in Petrograd. The occasion for arranging it was the following:

In the jubilee year, 1908, there was formed, at the Congress of Writers, a committee for the honoring of L. N.'s jubilee. As L. N. publicly declined a jubilee, the committee had to cease its activity. Then among the members of the committee, especially in the late V. Bogucharsky, there arose the idea of perpetuating the memory of L. N. by creating for him a grandiose monument in the shape of a "House-Museum in the name of L. N. Tolstoy."

This idea was taken up in literary circles, and in order to make, so to speak, a first survey of what a Tolstoy museum might offer, it was decided to arrange a temporary exhibition of everything artistic, literary, and biographical that relates to L. N. This exhibition, arranged on the Liteiny in the splendid premises of the Theatrical Club, had a great success and gripped the visitor by its interest and by the grandiosity of the personality and influence of L. N. Tolstoy.

At this time in Paris they were preparing to celebrate the literary jubilee of the writer Lamennais. A French man of letters, Paul-Hyacinthe Loyson, addressed himself to me with a request to draw L. N. into participation in this honoring. I wrote to L. N. and received from him this reply:

> "As regards the honoring of Lamennais, I should not advise them to elect me to membership of the committee, since this is an empty formality and I can take no part whatever in the affairs of the committee. But I should nevertheless wish to express to them my deep respect and reverence for the memory of Lamennais, who, as I think, both by his life and by his writings, is far from being appreciated, not only by the European, but also by the French public, if I am not mistaken. His chief trait, which is especially precious to me — is the ardent faith in the teaching of Christ in its true significance, passing into a feeling which infects those who read him, which I experience every time I read him."

This reply I, of course, did not delay in forwarding to its destination, having translated it into French.

At this time one of L. N.'s young friends, Valentin Fyodorovich Bulgakov, had undertaken the compilation of an extensive work, systematically setting forth L. N.'s worldview. This work is now printed under the title *Christian Ethics*. In compiling this work, V. F. Bulgakov was met with doubts as to how to reconcile certain thoughts of L. N.'s, contradictory at first sight, on the question of upbringing and education, which L. N. had expressed at the beginning of his pedagogical activity, back in the sixties, and in the latter time.

He addressed himself to L. N. with a request to explain these doubts to him and received from him a reply in the form of a long letter, in which L. N. sums up his views on this subject.

The essence of this letter consists in the following:

1) Upbringing and education ought to be free, without the threats of punishment and without the lure of encouragement.

> "I think," says L. N., "that already such complete freedom alone, that is, the absence of compulsion and of advantages both for the taught and for the teachers, would deliver people from a great portion of those evils which are now produced by the compulsory and self-interested education accepted everywhere. The absence in the majority of people of our time of any religious attitude whatever toward the world, of any firm moral rules, the false view of science, of the social order, especially of religion, and all the ruinous consequences proceeding from this — all this is engendered in great degree by the forcible and self-interested methods of education.

2) There ought to exist some criterion for the choice of the subjects of education out of their countless number. Such a criterion can be only religion.

3) It is of such importance to know how to arrange all the subjects of knowledge in the order of their importance, so as to work out a plan for their acquisition, and to this too religion ought to serve.

4) At the present time such a common religion is not acknowledged in humanity, and from this comes the reigning chaos in education.

5) But such a religion exists — it is the wisdom of humanity of all times and peoples.

6) If one adopts this criterion of a common-human religion, expressing itself in the brotherly union of all peoples, then the most important subjects of knowledge will of themselves be selected. One of such important subjects L. N. considers the study of the manner of life and the beliefs of various peoples, both one's own and others', that is, so-called ethnography.

7) Equally important is the determination of the meaning of life, that is, the answers to the questions: what am I and how am I to live, which is usually completely ignored in contemporary teaching.

Only such a planned distribution of knowledge, founded upon a religious understanding of the world, can give that harmonious development which will lead unhappy, strayed humanity out of darkness into light.

Among the Russian intelligentsia there was taking place that year a considerable ferment: the old principles were being reviewed and new ones established. And in a considerable group of this intelligentsia the religious-moral question was seriously raised.

Lev Nikolaevich became interested in this movement, and the result of his acquaintance with it was an annihilating criticism of this movement. L. N. expressed his thoughts in an article which he did not intend for the press, but in a conversation with the *Russkoye Slovo* correspondent Spiro he briefly summed up this view, and we convey it here in his own words:

> "The other day I read in a newspaper about a gathering of writers in which, in discussing the views, as it was there put, of the old and the new 'intelligentsia,' it came to light that the new intelligentsia acknowledges, for the bettering of the life of people, not a change of the external form of life, as the old intelligentsia acknowledges, but the inner, moral work of people upon themselves.
>
> As I have long and firmly been convinced that one of the chief obstacles to the movement forward toward a reasonable life and good consists precisely in the widespread and established superstition that external changes of the form of social life can better the life of people, I rejoiced on reading this news and hastened to obtain the literary collection *Vekhi* (Landmarks), in which, as was said in the article, these views of the 'young' intelligentsia were expressed.
>
> In the preface there was expressed that same thought, in the highest degree sympathetic to me, about the superstition of external reconstruction and the necessity of the inner work of each upon himself. And I set about reading the articles of this collection.
>
> I awaited an answer to the question that naturally follows — as to what that inner work ought to consist in, which is to replace the external — but this is just what I did not find.
>
> And if there is anything resembling such an answer, then there were answers expressed in especially confused, indefinite, and strikingly artificial words."

Lev Nikolaevich took in his hands an extract from *Vekhi* and, smiling, read it to me:

— It was said, for example:

> "Of the piety before the martyrology of the intelligentsia," of how "heroically the maximalism is projected into me," how "the psychology of intelligent heroism impresses some group or other," how "religious radicalism appeals to the inner essence of man, while irreligious materialism casts aside the problem of upbringing"; it spoke of "the artificially isolating process of abstraction," of "the adequate intellectual reflection of the world," of how "revolutionism is only a reflection," of "the metaphysical absolutization of the value of destruction," and so forth.

Lev Nikolaevich continued:

> "Besides this, the answers themselves of the various authors of the collection were various and not in agreement with one another. So that I was disappointed, not finding what I sought.
>
> And, reading all this, there involuntarily comes to my mind my old, dead friend, the Tver peasant Syutaev, who in his declining years came to his clear, firm understanding of Christianity, not in agreement with the church.
>
> He set himself that very question which the authors of the collection *Vekhi* set themselves.
>
> To this question he answered, in his Tver dialect, in five short words:
>
> 'All is in thee,' he used to say, 'in love.'
>
> By a strange chance, besides this recollection of Syutaev, called up in me by the collection, on that same day on which I read the collection, I received from Tashkent one of the significant letters that I receive from peasants — a letter from a peasant, discussing those very questions that are discussed in the collection, and answering them just as definitely as Syutaev's words, but in more detail.
>
> Here is one page from this letter, written in an astonishingly illiterate manner."

Hereupon Lev Nikolaevich handed me the content of the letter as set forth by him:

> "The foundation of human life is love," writes the peasant, "and man ought to love all without exception.
>
> Love can unite one with anyone at all, even with the animals — and this very love is God.
>
> Without love nothing can save man, and therefore one need not pray into empty space and to a wall — each man need only entreat his own self that he may be not a monster, but a man.
>
> And each man ought to strive himself for a good life, and not to hire judges and pacifiers.
>
> Let each man be a judge and a pacifier to himself.
>
> If you are humble, meek, and loving, then you will be united with anyone at all.
>
> Let each one try to act so, and he will see another world and another light and will attain a great good, so that the former life will seem a savage brutishness.
>
> One need not ask of others, but must oneself discern what is good and what is bad.
>
> One must not do to others what one does not wish for oneself.
>
> As when guests sit at one table and all eat one and the same thing and all are satisfied, so too one must live in the world: we all make use of one and the same earth, one and the same light, and therefore all ought to labor and to feed themselves, because everything belongs to no one, and we are all in this world — temporary guests.
>
> One need not restrict anything, one need only restrict one's own pride and replace it with love. And love will annihilate all malice.
>
> But we now all only complain of one another and condemn, while we ourselves, perhaps, are worse than those we condemn.
>
> And all now, both the lower and the higher, hate, so that they are even ready to kill one another.
>
> The lower think by this killing to enrich themselves, and the higher to pacify the people.
>
> And this — is a delusion.
>
> One can enrich oneself only by justice, and one can order people only by loving exhortation, by support, not by killing.
>
> Besides this, people have so strayed that they think other peoples — Germans, Chinese, French — are their enemies and that one may wage war with them.
>
> People must rise to the spiritual life and forget about the body and understand that the spirit in all is one.
>
> Were people to understand this — they would all love one another, there would be no evil among them, and the words of Jesus would be fulfilled, that the kingdom of God is upon earth, within us, within people."

— So, — said Lev Nikolaevich, — thinks and writes an illiterate peasant, knowing nothing either of "political impressionism," or of "staged provocation," and so forth, or even of Russian orthography.
