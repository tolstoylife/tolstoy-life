## Part III. 1906–1908. The Children. *I Cannot Be Silent*. The Jubilee.

Lev Nikolaevich begins the year 1906 with an interesting correspondence with his new friend and like-minded companion V. A. Sheyerman; he received from him a letter enclosing his open letter from "A Landowner." Here is that letter:

> "Belonging to the number of ordinary landowners (I am also a member of the zemstvo assembly), I am living through the same thing as you are.
>
> But I am coming out of this unbearable position, and by announcing my withdrawal I may perhaps help someone...
>
> To go on living like this is impossible... We have already lived our way to the very edge of hostile relations with the peasants. To prove our trampled rights of property, only one thing is left — murder (an armed guard has been permitted). For that matter, this is already being done in many places in Russia, while people go on calling themselves followers of the teaching of Christ.
>
> Others, considering themselves members of various political parties, abuse that very government whose whole business consists, chiefly, in defending them.
>
> Owners of lands... Why do you torment yourselves and others? For your whole heart is wounded by the hatred and malice surrounding you... For you too are people, and therefore have a right to the joy of life and to peace... Why then do you not take that joy, which is right here — beside you?
>
> What are you waiting for? On whom and on what do you set your hopes, when you yourselves do not take a single step forward?..
>
> Do not hundreds and thousands of peasant neighbours look upon each one of us, also waiting and also not believing in their expectations, but tormented by poison and malice?..
>
> Gentlemen landowners. Is it not time?!
>
> I will tell about myself. I could endure it no longer. I went into the village around which lies my land, and addressed the peasants... Here, approximately, is the discourse that I delivered to them:
>
> "Although there are no disorders in our locality, you know what is being done throughout Russia, and life has become unbearable both for you and for me. You know the manifesto of our Tsar — civil freedom has been proclaimed to us — which means that we have become, not only before God but also here on earth, before the Tsar, one and the same people — both you, the peasants, and I, the landowner. And if we are equal, must we not be comrades, friends, wishing one another good and not evil?
>
> I am the same as you, we have one homeland, and, like you, I love the village and the land, and yet we are at enmity, our interests are different. My land surrounds yours, and every farming of mine, my labour on this land — is a knife in your heart. For you there is no way out, and for me there is no life because of your hopelessness and, consequently, because of your hostility.
>
> And so, is it not clear that the hour has now come when we must unite, unite altogether into one?
>
> And all my land, the proprietor's, and yours, the peasants', must be common. And I, as your equal, shall have to receive from all my homestead and land allotment, like each of you.
>
> And only then will our interests be common and not opposed. There will no longer be: this is mine, and that is yours, but everything will be for all 'ours.' I shall be your fellow villager, and then I shall be equal to you, shall be free from oppression, and you will be free from oppression.
>
> Do you agree, peasants, not only to receive my land, but also to take me and my family into your society with rights equal to your own?"
>
> This is what I said and did.
>
> Oh, gentlemen owners. If you could have seen their triumphant joy and my boundless inner exultation... I had believed in the kind heart of the people, but I had not expected and had not deserved what I found... Joy, love, and peace surrounded me.
>
> O you, owners of lands, you seek your own way out in the political questions of the state — why then do you not first do that which you can do and which lies right under your hands? Can one stand for the people without having stood together with them? Those of you who love people and labour on the land — hasten quickly, little time is left for your one and only work.
>
> I do not say that mine is the only way out, but I do say: stand with the people and for them.
>
> And the people will explain to you what they need from you, and you will understand and will give it to them — believe the people, that they too will understand you, and that you too will have all that you need.
>
> Be bold, if you go by the path of faith in the truth!
>
> What is it to you what will come of it, if you can do no better! Hasten. There is no time for hesitation. Now there is nowhere to flee to, even if you were to go off to the end of the earth, you would remain even there a participant in murders for the right of your property.
>
> Believe in the truth and in love to yourself and within the people — not you, but it will lead you out of everything and will show you your road.
>
> Former owner of 840 desyatinas in Kharkov Province, Starobelsk District, the village of Muratovo. Vladimir Sheyerman."

Of course, L. N. could not but sympathize with such a solution of the land question. Sheyerman asked L. N.'s assistance in publishing this letter. L. N. sent it to the newspaper *Novosti Dnya* (News of the Day) with the following letter to the editor:

> "Mr. Editor, I received today a very remarkable letter, both in thought and in content, from a Mr. Sheyerman unknown to me, with a request to assist in placing it in a newspaper. Mr. Sheyerman writes to me that two liberal newspapers, to which he had sent this letter, refused to print it; whereas it seems to me that this letter, besides being a sign of the times, just as occurred at the emancipation of the peasants, can and must have the most beneficial influence both upon landowners, serving them as an indication of the serious and splendid activity that is presented to them and possible for them, and upon the peasants, showing them that the injustice of exclusive landholding is recognized not only by the latter, but also by the landowners."

And to Sheyerman himself he answered thus:

> "Your letter is splendid, and your act is better still. May God grant you not to yield to any temptations and to hold fast in that happy state, proper to man, in which you wrote your letter. I will do everything possible to print it both in Moscow and in Petersburg. I shall be very glad of communion with you.
>
> You are, probably, a young man, and your act was called forth by an impulse in which the motive causes are both the demands of conscience and the desire for glory — for human love. Beware of this second motive. Deeds called forth by this motive are not enduring. It happens that of good deeds called forth by this motive alone a man repents. That is terribly sad. Ask yourself whether you would have done the same if you had known for certain that no one would ever learn of your act. If what I write is superfluous, forgive me."

The open letter was printed, and between its author and L. N. communication was established.

The development of revolutionary events compelled L. N. to come forward with his own definite word. He writes the remarkable article "To the Government, the Revolutionaries, and the People," disclosing their relations and summoning them to the one true path of good and truth. Addressing the government, he gives it the following definition:

> "By the government I mean all those people who, making use of the established power, can change the existing laws and carry them into execution. In Russia, up to now, such people have been and continue to be: the Tsar and his ministers and nearest counsellors."

There is only one justification for the existence of governments — that is the good of the people. If it makes the people suffer, it is a bad government; and such it was at the time when L. N. was writing this article. Alarmed by the popular disturbances, it began timidly to let go, one after another, of the governmental reins, in order then to draw them tight again with even greater force. And so L. N., an enemy of all half-measures, says to the government:

> "Your salvation lies not in dumas with these or those elections, and by no means in machine-guns, cannon, and executions, but in this: that you recognize and set before the people an ideal of justice, good, and truth that is higher and more realizable than those which your opponents set forth. Set before people such an ideal, and seriously and sincerely, not in order to save yourselves, but in order to fulfil your duty, set about realizing it, and you will save not only yourselves, but will save Russia from those calamities which have already come upon her and still threaten her."

And, in giving these general indications, he dwells especially on the liberation of the people from land-slavery, from landed property, and calls upon the government to carry out this act of freedom:

> "Yes, before you, governmental men, there are now only two ways out: fratricidal slaughters and all the horrors of revolution, and withal an inevitable, shameful destruction all the same; or the peaceful realization of the eternal and just demand of the whole people, and the showing to other Christian nations of the path and the possibility of destroying that injustice from which men have so long and so cruelly suffered. In this one thing lies salvation, not only your own, the governmental persons', but the salvation of the whole people from the greatest calamities and corruption.
>
> Whether or not that form of social order under which you enjoy power has outlived itself — while you enjoy it, use it not to multiply tenfold the evil that has already been committed by you, and the hatred toward you that you have already aroused in people, but to fulfil your obligation and to do a great, good deed not only for your own people, but for all humanity. But if this form has outlived itself, then let its last act be an act of good and truth, and not of falsehood and cruelty. For all of you, both the Tsar and the ministers, besides the social position you occupy, are also simply people, and you have obligations before God and before your own conscience. Think about this."

Addressing the revolutionaries, he defined this group of people for himself thus:

> "By the revolutionaries I mean all those people, beginning from the most peaceable constitutionalists down to the most warlike revolutionaries, who wish to replace the existing governmental power by a power differently organized and composed of other persons."

The revolutionaries are a new government, coming to take the place of the old, and since it employs the same means of struggle, then even if it should gain the upper hand, it will repeat the errors and delusions of the old, and will not give the people good. L. N. convicts them of insincerity in their striving for the good of the people, and points to the one path of bettering the life of the people:

> "Your activity, as you say, has for its aim the bettering of the general condition of people, but in order that the condition of people may become better, it is necessary that the people themselves should become better. This is just such a truism as that, in order that a vessel of water may grow warm, all the drops of it must be warmed. But in order that people may become better, they must turn their attention more and more upon themselves, upon their inner life. External social activity, however, and in particular social struggle, always distracts people's attention from the inner life, and therefore always, inevitably corrupting people, lowers the level of social morality, as has happened everywhere and as we see it now to a striking degree in Russia. And the lowering of the level of social morality brings it about that the most immoral parts of society come more and more to the top, and an immoral public opinion is established, which permits and even approves crimes. And a vicious circle is established: the worst parts of society, called forth by social struggle, give themselves up with ardour to social activity corresponding to their low level of morality, and this activity in turn attracts to itself the still worse elements of society."

And in conclusion he gives them a friendly counsel:

> "Those complex and difficult circumstances amid which we now live in Russia demand of you, precisely now, not articles in newspapers, not speeches in assemblies, not going about the streets with revolvers, and the often dishonest incitement of the peasants, but a strict attitude toward yourselves, toward your own life, which alone is in our power and the bettering of which alone can better the general condition of people."

Finally, he addresses the people, and gives this concept the following definition:

> "By the people I mean the whole Russian working people, but predominantly the working agricultural people, those upon whose labours the life of all the rest is upheld."

Before this people stands a dilemma: to which government to attach itself, which to support, which to obey — the old, outliving its day, or the new, the revolutionary, coming to take its place; and L. N. boldly declares his opinion:

> "To attach oneself neither to the old nor to the new government, and to take part in the un-Christian deeds neither of the one nor of the other."

And he underscores this formula of his political credo.

He asserts that this is more reasonable and more advantageous even in the case where this non-submission should call forth temporary sufferings.

> "If you, both rural and urban workers, should at first have to suffer for your disobedience, both from the old and from the new government, and also from internal disagreements which may arise among you, still those calamities which may proceed from these causes are as nothing in comparison with those calamities and sufferings which you now bear from the government, and which you will yet have to endure if, obeying one government or the other, you are drawn into those murders, executions, internecine strifes which are being committed now and will long be committed by the struggling governments, unless only you put a stop to them by your non-participation in them."

And finally he concludes:

> "Out of the present difficult circumstances there is for you, the Russian working people, only one sinless and indubitably reasonable way out: the refusal to obey any violent power whatsoever, the humble and meek endurance of violence, but not participation in it.
>
> "He that shall endure unto the end shall be saved." And your salvation is in your own hands."

Let us glance into L. N.'s diary at the beginning of this year, and we shall find there a whole series of interesting thoughts. Thus, in February he sets down:

> "The firmer the faith in God, the more and more God recedes. In the final representation he is only law. And then it is already impossible not to believe in him. I read today Kant's *Religion in Grenzen der blossen Vernunft* (*). Very good, but in vain does he justify, though allegorically, the ecclesiastical forms. Kant is not right in saying that the performance of rites, faith in historical traditions, is fetishism, and that this is something entirely opposite to a reasonable faith in the moral law. Faith in historical traditions and in the necessity of rites is that same faith in law, and the moral law is understood perversely. Kant is right in opposing the moral law to the ritual; but I wish to say that he who believes in rites and traditions still believes, though he errs, recognizes something higher besides animal needs. So that I would divide people into three: 1) those who believe in nothing, who see nothing beyond what is accessible to the reason; 2) those who believe in false traditions; and 3) those who believe in the law, recognized by them in their own heart. The Chuvash who carries his god in his bosom and flogs him and smears him with sour cream is still higher than that agnostic who sees no necessity in the concept of God."

(* "Religion within the Limits of Pure Reason." *)

A few days later we meet with thoughts from another sphere, the social-political:

> "A people, like a man, may set as the chief condition of its welfare material prosperity, and then political well-being is for it a matter of the first importance; and a people may, just like a man, set as the higher condition of its welfare its spiritual life, and then material prosperity and political well-being are for it not only not important, but repugnant, if it must take part in this political order. The Western peoples belong to the first type; the Eastern, and among them the Russian, to the second. This is the thought of the Khomyakovs — father and son — and the thought is entirely true. But if the Russian people, prizing its spiritual life, which was expressed in Orthodoxy, could content itself with the autocracy of the Russian tsars, willingly submitting to their power, even when it was cruel, only so as to be itself free from participation in the violence of power, this does not prove that such a relation to power — submission to it — ought always to continue. This relation inevitably had to change for two reasons: first, because power in the old days, patriarchal and ruling only over one homogeneous people of one language and one faith, not setting itself the task of joining alien nationalities into one (imperialism), did not compel people to take part in deeds alien to the people (to defend Russia from the Mongols or the French, but not to throttle Poland, Finland, or to seize Manchuria), and therefore did not demand of the people deeds alien to it and cruel; and secondly, the demands of the spiritual life do not always remain one and the same, but are clarified and developed, and Christianity, which formerly demanded only submission to the authorities, even if the authorities demanded murder, in its clarified state demanded of people already something else: non-participation in oppression, violence, murders. So that the relation of the people to power inevitably changes from two ends: power becomes worse, more cruel, more repugnant to the spiritual cast of the people, and the spiritual demands of the people become purer, higher. This very thing is being accomplished now."

And here is a small lyrical digression, as always with a moral foundation:

> "I was riding on horseback through the forest, and it was so fine that I thought: have I the right to rejoice so in life. And I answered myself: yes, every man would have a right to life, were there no sin, no sufferings, produced by some people upon others. But now, when there is sin and there are its involuntary victims, there must be voluntary victims, and we have no right to rejoice in life, but must rejoice in sacrifice, voluntary sacrifice."

Of the literary works of this time let us point to the artistic story from Polish life, "What For?," at which L. N. was working at this time, finishing it for the *Circle of Reading*. By this story, in L. N.'s own words, he paid a tribute of respect and sympathy to the Polish people, which was at this time subjected to cruel persecutions by the Russian government. At the same time, as L. N. told me, he was paying off his old sin, since in his youth, under the influence of the patriotic milieu in which he lived, he had allowed himself hostile relations toward these people.

At this time L. N.'s second daughter, Maria Lvovna, was living abroad with her husband and carried on an active correspondence with her father. In March of this year L. N., answering one of her letters, wrote to her:

> "...I advise you to make use of everything you can take from Europe. I personally would not wish to take anything, in spite of all its purity and loftiness. And, to my regret, I see that we are all picking up the little drops: parties, pre-election agitations, the bloc, and so on. Disgusting. Such depravity, into which the peasants are being drawn, corrupting them. Perhaps it is inevitable, and the peasants too must pass through this depravity, in order to understand all its aimlessness and harmfulness. And sometimes I cannot but think that this is not necessary. And the proof of the unnecessity of this I see in the fact that I, and many along with me, we see that all these constitutions cannot lead to anything else than that other people will exploit the majority, taking turns, as happens in England, France, America, everywhere, and all will be restlessly striving to exploit one another, and more and more will be casting aside the one reasonable, moral agricultural life, laying this grey labour upon slaves in India, Africa, Asia, and Europe, wherever it is possible. Very clean materially is this European life, but terribly dirty spiritually. So that I sometimes doubt whether the Russian people needs to pass through this depravity, to come to that blind alley into which the Western peoples have already gone. I think so, because, when the Western peoples were going upon this path, all the advanced people called them to this path, whereas now not I alone, but we many see that it is destruction. And, warning the people away from this path, we do not say, as the former opponents of the movement said: 'go back or stop,' but we say: 'go forward, but only not in the direction in which you are going, because this direction leads backward'; we say: 'go boldly forward to liberation from power.'"

Such "liberation from power" was constantly being accomplished by people who refused, on religious convictions, to go to military service. Of course, these people endured persecutions from the imperial government, and Lev Nikolaevich had more than once to come forward in their defence before the powers of the world. A defence of this kind is the letter of L. N. to the newspapers given below, written by him in April of this year:

> "Gracious sir, Mr. Editor. The other day I received the following appeal, printed in a Poltava newspaper, with a request to assist in its dissemination. I think that the question proposed for discussion in this appeal has, especially in our time, a great importance, and therefore I am sending it to you with some remarks of mine on this occasion."

In this appeal a group of religious people addresses the future representatives of the people in the State Duma with a request to issue a law releasing from military service, or replacing it with other work, those people whose conscience does not permit them to take part in murder.

L. N. adds to the appeal his own sympathetic word and adduces a letter received by him from one of those who had refused:

> "Most respected Lev Nikolaevich, I have long been meaning to write to you, but, fearing to trouble you, I constantly held back from writing. But since my brother wrote to you (his brother wrote me that, as a military court was impending, could I not find a defender for him) and you at once answered him, I have resolved to write to you. You wrote to my brother that you had asked an advocate to take upon himself the labour of defending me, if a defence is possible. I thank you very much for your services, but I consider myself unworthy of defence and cannot know what defence is possible over me, although I by no means refuse, but fulfil and do, as a slave in the flesh, that which is not against Jesus and not against my conscience, but no one can acquit me, because I am acting against their law. I have come to the conviction that war is an evil, wherefore I refused to take up a rifle and to learn murder, for which the regimental court condemned me to 2 years in a disciplinary battalion, as for the deliberate non-fulfilment of a superior's order. The commander of the regiment reduced it by 1 year. My intention is to refuse again to take up arms even in the disciplinary battalion. But my intention in writing to you is not about this at all, but about something else; I wish to turn to you with questions and with a request, if possible, to answer.
>
> I have not had occasion to read all your works, wherefore I could not come to a conclusion as to what conviction precisely you hold about God and about the resurrection of the dead. And I should like to learn what conviction you hold about God, and whether you believe in the resurrection at some time, according to the tradition of the Gospel, of all the dead. Has this not been confused in the Gospel, instead of the spiritually dead, into the dead in the flesh?
>
> I enclose here my thoughts about God, so that you may the sooner learn the course of my thoughts and may give an answer more briefly. About my act of refusal I consider it superfluous to write. For the present I am in the guardhouse, in the common quarters, until my dispatch to the disciplinary battalion, and when I shall be dispatched is still unknown.
>
> Within, I am very calm and ready to die for the truth."

And L. N. adds on his own part:

> "The 'thoughts about God' enclosed with the letter show in their author a man who has thought much and is deeply religious.
>
> And such people will be flogged with rods in the disciplinary battalions for the non-fulfilment of the orders of a sergeant-major.
>
> Yes, the question of refusals of military service has an exceedingly enormous importance.
>
> One may pretend not to see the contradiction between war and Christianity so long as nothing points to it, but one cannot help seeing it when people by their sufferings declare this contradiction.
>
> The contradiction exists. And sooner or later it must be resolved. And it can be resolved only by two ways: by the abolition of obligatory military service, or by the renunciation of Christianity.
>
> Our government holds, as it were, to the second way: the renunciation of Christianity by means of perverting and falsely interpreting it and of tormenting those who confess it by their lives.
>
> I think that this way is both false and dangerous.
>
> The chief, if not the only, cause of all those horrors that we are now living through is this very perversion and false interpretation of Christianity and the cruelty of the government. The most terrible thing in the events now taking place is the brutalizing of people. And this brutalizing has come about from the renunciation of Christianity and from the cruelty of the government.
>
> And therefore the best thing that our government can do, both in this and in all the other questions now arising, is this: to employ all its forces and attention, not upon perverting or concealing the eternal Christian truth and upon compelling people by threats and violence to renounce it, but upon bringing its activity into accord with it."

In the diary of this time we find especially interesting thoughts on the land question:

> "It has become perfectly clear of late that the manner of agricultural life is not one of the various manners of life, but is life (like a book — the Bible), life itself, the one and only human life, with which alone is possible the manifestation of all the higher human qualities. The chief error in the ordering of human societies, and one which removes the possibility of any reasonable ordering of life — is that people wish to order society without agricultural life, or with such an ordering in which agricultural life is only one and the most insignificant form of life. How right Bondarev is.
>
> Peace is the highest material good of a society of people, as the highest material good of the individual is health. So have people always supposed. And peace is possible only for agriculturists. Only agriculturists feed themselves by direct labour. Townspeople inevitably feed themselves by one another. Among them the state arose, and is possible, and is needed. To agriculturists it is superfluous and ruinous."

As a supplement to this we adduce a thought of L. N.'s, set down by A. B. Goldenweiser in his diary:

> "Now is the moment when Russia, which has always gone behind other countries, is, as it were, called to stand ahead of all by carrying out a land reform. The extreme revolutionary elements are strong only through the support of the people. But the people, if the age-old injustice of holding land as property should cease, will undoubtedly abandon them and return to a peaceful life."

In pointing out the characteristic peculiarities of the Russian people, L. N. often adduced examples from the life of peasants known to him. Here is one of the stories set down by A. B. Goldenweiser at this same time: L. N. was telling about a Telyatinki peasant, the bootmaker Osip Tsyganov:

> "He lived with his family, worked well, and suddenly, one fine day, he threw up his work, put on some sort of long coat, and went to wander as a beggar. The peasants said of him that he had gone 'in the old way,' that is, had become like the holy fools that there used to be in olden times. Many considered him deranged. But he was not at all deranged; rather, there had suddenly opened before him all the falsehood and untruth of life, and he could no longer go on living so. I remember, once he came to me. The first time I simply gave to him, the second time I fell into conversation. The long coat on him had crept all apart from age in lengthwise strips. I gave him something and ask:
>
> — Well then, are you afraid of death?
>
> — And have I, then, renounced him?
>
> When he was dying, I was with him. He was dying perfectly consciously and calmly. When they wished to prepare him for death, to administer the sacrament, he refused and said:
>
> — I need nothing. The Master will not deceive."

It is this religious spirit, so often manifesting itself in simple peasant souls, that allowed L. N. to expect of the Russian people the fulfilment of a mission of special importance before all humanity.

This summer I again visited Yasnaya Polyana, and again found the great old man vigorous, healthy, and full of energy.

This time, upon his calm, stern, and at the same time kind face, one could notice the features of some care, of strained thought, of suppressed disquiet. And this disturbed peace was often expressed by L. N. in conversations with his numerous visitors.

> "Ah, how terrible, how terrible," he said one time, "in what a time we live. Today there came to me a 15-year-old boy, a gymnasium pupil, and asked my counsel as to whether it is possible to kill people for the sake of the social good.
>
> A friend of mine wrote a very sensible article on contemporary questions, in which he, among other things, expressed doubts as to the usefulness of the measure now practised — the killing of policemen. This article, in spite of all its merits, acknowledged by the editorial board of a liberal newspaper, was not accepted, on account of its peaceful spirit. How much malice, how much brutalizing of people develops from all this absurd preaching of murder!
>
> In people, love has decreased... The causes of all the calamities now being lived through by Russia lie far deeper than many contemporary reformers think."

Such were the thoughts of L. N. ten years before the upheaval.

Glancing again into L. N.'s diary, we find there new profound thoughts about prayer, corresponding to his religious mood of that time:

> "My prayer in the mornings is almost always useful. Often, repeating certain words, I do not join feeling to them; but for the most part one or another of the passages of the prayer seizes me and calls forth a kind feeling: sometimes devotion to the will of God, sometimes love, sometimes self-renunciation, sometimes petition, non-condemnation. I advise it always to everyone.
>
> Sometimes I pray at unappointed times in the very simplest manner, I say: 'Lord have mercy,' I cross myself with my hand, I pray not with thought, but with the one feeling of the consciousness of my dependence on God. I will counsel no one, but for me this is good. Just now I sighed thus, prayerfully."

And here are his new "heretical" thoughts on the question, so beloved of the intelligentsia, of the press:

> "Yesterday there came to me a thought that struck me, to the effect that writing, and still more printing, were the chief cause of the perversion of the true faith disclosed by the great founders of the religions: the relation of man to God and the obligations flowing from this relation.
>
> All the great religions were spread orally. And it seems to me that only thus can the true religion be spread. And not so much orally as — not by writing, not by printing, but by life and by a part of life — by oral preaching.
>
> To say nothing of the fact that with such a spreading there can be no fixing of words and afterward a false interpretation of them (like the epistles of Paul, which more than anything else perverted Christianity) — in spreading by life and by the oral testing of the truth — always in the life of the preacher, every error in word, in expression, passes without a trace; there remains his sincerity, and it alone serves as the true conductor. I once said, almost in jest, that the printing of books was the most powerful means of spreading ignorance, and this is not a jest, but a terrible and sad truth. We know to what idle talk in life leads, idle talk with the tongue. The same, a worse idle talk, is now going on in print. Our society, with its journals, newspapers, books, lectures, is exactly like a crazed crowd in which all are talking and no one is listening. But this I say about all the most various subjects with which the press is occupied, from politics to verses and dramas. In the matter of religion, however, it is indubitable that writing, and especially printing, hinder more than anything else the correct spreading of religious truths and contribute to perverting and obscuring them. This subject is very important and worth returning to and thinking over still further."

This same autumn the life of the Yasnaya Polyana house was alarmed by the grave illness of Sofya Andreyevna.

Here is how Ilya Lvovich tells of it in his reminiscences:

> "Very hard minutes did my father live through during the dangerous illness of Mama in the autumn of 1906.
>
> On learning of her illness, all of us, the children, gathered at Yasnaya Polyana. Mama had already been lying in bed for several days and was suffering frightfully from unbearable pains in the abdomen.
>
> Professor V. F. Snegirev, who came at our summons, diagnosed a disintegrating internal tumour and proposed to perform an operation.
>
> For greater certainty in his diagnosis and for a consultation, he asked that Professor Fenomenov be summoned from Petersburg, but Mama's illness went on at such rapid steps that on the third day, early in the morning, Snegirev awoke us all and said that he had decided not to wait for Fenomenov, because if the operation were not done at once, Mama would die.
>
> With these words he went also to my father.
>
> Papa did not at all believe in the usefulness of the operation, thinking that Mama was dying, and prepared himself prayerfully for her death.
>
> He considered that 'the great and solemn minute of death had drawn near, that one must submit to the will of God, and that any intervention of the doctors violates the grandeur and solemnity of the great act of death.'
>
> When the doctor asked him definitely whether he consented to the operation, he answered that let Mama herself and the children decide, but that he stood aside and would say nothing either for or against.
>
> During the operation itself he went off into the 'Chepyzh' and there walked alone and prayed.
>
> — If the operation is successful, ring the bell to me twice, but if not, then... no, better not ring at all, I will come myself, — he said, having changed his mind, and quietly went toward the forest.
>
> Half an hour later, when the operation was over, my sister Masha and I ran at a run to look for Papa.
>
> He was coming toward us, frightened and pale.
>
> — Safely, safely! — we cried, catching sight of him at the edge of the wood.
>
> — Good, go on, I will come presently, — he said in a voice strangled with emotion, and turned again into the forest.
>
> After Mama awoke from the narcosis he went in to her and came out of her room in a depressed and indignant state.
>
> — My God, what a horror! They will not let a person die in peace. A woman lies with her abdomen cut open, tied to the bed, without a pillow... and moans more than before the operation. This is some kind of torture.
>
> Only after several days, when my mother's health was completely restored, did my father grow calm and cease to blame the doctors for their intervention."

Such was the opinion of Ilya Lvovich, but one may think otherwise as well. I was then at Yasnaya Polyana soon after the operation. The family told me how, just before the operation, Sofya Andreyevna prepared herself for death and took leave of the whole house, beginning with L. N. and ending with the last manservant and maidservant, asked forgiveness of all, and all wept, moved by her high spiritual mood. Had she died then, she would have died a saint, blessed by all who knew her. She was cured, was left to live, and she, having entered again into her flesh, poisoned with her morbid life the last years of Lev Nikolaevich's life, herself lived through terrible sufferings, and hastened his end. I suppose that L. N.'s feeling of protest against the operation was founded on considerations of this kind. Here are his thoughts on this occasion from his diary.

> "1 September. Sonya's illness is ever worse. Today I felt a special pity. But she is touchingly reasonable, truthful, and kind. About nothing more do I wish to write. Three sons, Sergei, Andrei, and Misha, are here, and two daughters, Masha and Sasha. The house is full of doctors. This is hard: instead of devotion to the will of God and a religiously solemn mood — the petty, the unsubmissive, the egoistic. I thought and felt well. I thank God. I do not live, and the whole world does not live, in time, but there is being disclosed an immovable but hitherto inaccessible world in time. How much easier and more comprehensible thus. And how death, in such a view — is not the ceasing of something, but a complete disclosure."

> "2 September. Today they performed the operation. They say it was successful. But it is very hard. In the morning she was very fine spiritually. How death pacifies.
>
> I thought: is it not evident that she is being disclosed both for me and for herself; but when she dies, then she is completely disclosed for herself: 'Ah, so that is what it is.' We, however, who remain, cannot yet see that which has been disclosed for the dying one. For us it will be disclosed later, in its time. During the operation I walked among the firs and grew weary in nerves."

> "15 September. Sonya's health is good. Evidently she is recovering. Much has been lived through. I have finished both the article, and the one about the land, and have begun a letter to a Chinese about that same thing.
>
> I want to write quite otherwise, more truthfully. There is much to set down, but I will not today. I rode far into the forest through a snowstorm. My state is not vigorous, but good, kind."

The "letter to a Chinese," which L. N. mentions in the preceding entry, was after all finished and sent off, but since it lost the character of a personal, private letter, L. N. gave it to the press. Here is its brief content:

> "The Chinese people," writes L. N., "having endured so much from the immoral, coarsely egoistic, mercenary cruelty of the European peoples, has up to the latest time answered all the violence committed upon it with a majestic and wise calm, with the preference of patience in the struggle with violence."

But there too begins the imitation of the European peoples:

> "It is for this reason that I now hear with dread and grief, and see in your book, the manifestation in China of the spirit of struggle, of the desire to give a rebuff by force to the villainies committed by the European peoples."

And L. N. wishes to direct this great people upon a new path proper to it:

> "I think that in our time a great upheaval is being accomplished in the life of humanity, and that in this upheaval China must, at the head of the Eastern peoples, play a great part.
>
> It seems to me that the destiny of the Eastern peoples — China, Persia, Turkey, India, Russia, and perhaps Japan (if it is not yet entirely stuck in the snares of the depravity of European civilization) — consists in pointing out to the peoples that true path to freedom, for the expression of which, as you write in your book, there is in the language no other word than Tao, that is, the striving toward true spiritual freedom.
>
> In the peoples there has manifested itself the consciousness that power is an evil, from which one must free oneself.
>
> And the Western peoples have long since felt this necessity and have long since changed their relation to power by one method common to all the Western peoples: by the limitation of power by means of representatives, that is, in essence by the extension of power, by transferring it from one or several to the many.
>
> At the present time, I think, the turn has come for the Eastern peoples too, and for China likewise, to feel all the harm of despotic power and to seek means of liberation from despotic power, which under the present conditions of life has become unbearable.
>
> But the spirit of decay and of imitation has touched the ancient people too.
>
> I see by your book and by other reports that the frivolous people of China, called the party of reform, think that this change must consist in doing that very thing which the Western peoples did, that is, in replacing the despotic government by a representative one, in setting up the same kind of army as the Western peoples have, the same kind of industry."

And Lev Nikolaevich, by a whole series of arguments, persuades the learned Chinese not to go upon the deception of European civilization, but to seek his own path, the path of non-submission to violence. Finally L. N. concludes thus:

> "The matter now confronting, in my opinion, not only China but all the Eastern peoples, is not only that of delivering themselves from those evils which they suffer from their own government and from foreign peoples, but that of pointing out to all peoples the way out of that transitional condition in which they all find themselves.
>
> And there is not, and cannot be, any other way out than the freeing of oneself from human power and submission to the power of God."

Another great, remarkable letter he writes to the French writer Paul Sabatier, the author of the well-known book on Francis of Assisi. Paul Sabatier is, in France, the leader of a new religious movement striving to rehabilitate Catholicism, bringing it to the higher Christian, moral foundation.

L. N. frankly objects to Sabatier, considering useless and incompatible the union of an ecclesiastical league with Christ's truth, and points to a whole series of symptoms giving hope of the resolution of this age-old conflict between the Christian religion and the state, in the sense of the complete liberation of religion from state power.

This correspondence went through me, and it fell to me to deliver L. N.'s answer to Paul Sabatier in Geneva. He was very much touched by L. N.'s answer, regarded it with great respect, and I am certain that upon his young like-minded companions it had a sobering influence.

The dark autumn evenings gave L. N. the possibility of giving himself up more to reading, and here he sets down in his diary his impressions from what he had read:

> "I am reading Goethe and I see all the harmful influence of this insignificant, bourgeois-egoistic, gifted man upon that generation which I came upon, in particular poor Turgenev with his rapture before *Faust* (a quite bad work) and Shakespeare, and, chiefly, with that special tenderness which is attributed to various articles: Laocoöns, Apollos, and various verses and dramas. How much I tormented myself when, having come to love Turgenev, I wished to come to love that which he set so high. I tried with all my might — and could not at all. What a terrible harm are authorities, glorified great men, and false ones at that."

And again a lyrical digression, in which his grief and joy are expressed:

> "Set down thus after a very hard mood: our life is too disgusting altogether: people amuse themselves, take cures, go off somewhere, study something, dispute, take care about that which is no concern of theirs, and there is no life, because there are no obligations. Terrible. More and more often I feel this.
>
> I went for a walk. A wondrous autumn morning, still, warm, the green shoots, the smell of leaves. And people, instead of this wondrous nature with fields, forests, water, birds, beasts, arrange for themselves in the cities another, artificial nature with factory chimneys, palaces, portable steam-engines, phonographs... Terrible, and there is no way at all to mend it..."

A few more extracts from the autumn diary give a full and vivid picture of L. N.'s mood at that time, with all the agitations that his great soul was living through.

> "There is very much in our, in my understanding of the meaning of life (religion) that is conditional, arbitrary, unclear, sometimes directly untruthful. I should like to express the meaning of life as clearly as possible, and if I cannot, then to introduce nothing unclear into this definition. Unclear to me is the concept of God. I have no right whatever to speak about God, about the whole of God, whereas I know only this, that within me there is something free, all-powerful — I wished to say good, but this quality cannot be attributed to this something, since the all-powerful and free and one cannot but be good. This consciousness I know and can live in it, and in this transferring into this consciousness of one's whole life lies the highest good of man."

> "Last time I set down that I continue to rejoice in the consciousness of life, but today I must set down precisely the opposite: I have weakened spiritually, chiefly in that I want, I seek the love of people, both near and far. Today I drove to Yasenki and brought back letters, all unpleasant. The fact that they could be unpleasant to me shows how greatly I have sunk. Two lady-arbitresses, unclear, muddled, and clinging (and toward them one could and should have related lovingly, as I decided, on reflection), and then a feuilleton in a Kharkov newspaper by that little student who lived here in the summer. An indubitable sign of decline, of the loss of communion with the Eternal, through the fact that it became painful to me to read his malicious and stupid printed lie. Besides, I was physically in a bad, gloomy mood and for a long time could not restore my communion with God."

On the following day he wrote:

> "In a very good spiritual state of love toward all. I read John's epistle. Astonishing. Only now do I fully understand it. Today there was a great temptation, which I did not after all fully overcome. Abakumov caught up with me with a request and a complaint that for some oaks he had been sentenced to prison. It was very painful. He cannot understand that I, the husband, cannot do things my own way, and he sees in me a villain and a Pharisee hiding behind his wife. I did not manage to endure it lovingly, I told A. that I cannot live here. And this is unkind. In general I am being more and more abused on all sides. This is good: it drives one toward God. If only one could hold fast to this. In general I feel one of the greatest changes that have taken place in me precisely now. I feel this by the calm and the joyousness and the kind feeling (I dare not say — love) toward people. Almost all my former writings of former years, except the Gospel and some, I do not like, on account of their unkindness. I do not wish to give them.
>
> Masha greatly agitates me. I love her very, very much. Yes, I want to draw a separating line under all my past life and to begin a new, even though the very shortest, but a purer epilogue."

"Masha greatly agitates me," says L. N. in his diary; this agitation was called forth by her grave illness, which carried her away into the unseen world.

This was a remarkable person, undoubtedly, by her spiritual qualities the one nearest of all to L. N. Ilya Lvovich, her brother, splendidly characterized the daughter's relation to her father in his reminiscences:

> "When I arrived at Yasnaya the day after her death, I felt a kind of heightened, prayerfully tender mood throughout the whole family, and there, perhaps for the first time, I became conscious of all the grandeur and beauty of death.
>
> I clearly felt that by her death Masha had not only not gone away from us, but, on the contrary, had drawn near forever and welded herself with us all, as could never have been during her life.
>
> This same mood I saw also in my father. He went about silent, pitiable, straining all his forces in the struggle with his personal grief, but I heard from him not one word of murmuring, not a single complaint — only words of tender emotion.
>
> When they carried the coffin to the church, he dressed and went to accompany it.
>
> At the stone pillars he stopped us, took leave of the dead woman, and went along the prospect homeward. I looked after him: he walked over the melting wet snow with a quick, old man's gait, as always sharply turning out the toes of his feet, and not once looked back.
>
> My sister Masha had an enormous significance in my father's life and in the life of our whole family.
>
> How many times in these latter years has one had to recall her and to say with sadness: 'If Masha were alive...,' 'if Masha had not died...'
>
> In order to explain Masha's relations to our father, I shall have to go far back.
>
> In our father's character — perhaps because he grew up without a mother, or perhaps innately — there was one distinctive and at first glance strange peculiarity: manifestations of the feeling of tenderness were entirely uncharacteristic of him.
>
> I say 'tenderness' as distinct from 'heartiness.' Heartiness he had, and a great deal.
>
> Characteristic in this sense is his description of the death of Uncle Nikolai Nikolaevich. In a letter to Sergei Nikolaevich, describing his brother's last day of life, our father tells how he helped him to undress.
>
> '...And he submitted, and became different... he praised everyone and said to me: thank you, my friend.' Do you understand what that means in our relations?'
>
> It turns out that in the language of the brothers Tolstoy the words 'my friend' were such a tenderness that one cannot imagine a higher.
>
> These words astonished our father even on the lips of his dying brother.
>
> In all my life I never saw from him a single manifestation of tenderness.
>
> To kiss the children he did not like, and, greeting them, did it only out of duty.
>
> It is understandable, therefore, that with regard to himself too he could not call forth tenderness, and that heartfelt closeness with him was never accompanied by any outward manifestations.
>
> To me, for example, it could never in any way have come into my head simply to go up to my father and kiss him or stroke his hand.
>
> This was hindered in part also by the fact that I always looked up at him from below, and his spiritual might, his greatness, hindered me from seeing in him simply a man, at times pitiable and tired — a weak old man who so needed warmth and peace.
>
> This warmth could be given to our father by Masha alone.
>
> She would come up, stroke his hand, caress him, say to him a tender word, and you saw that this was pleasant to him, and he was happy and even himself answered her in kind.
>
> It was as though with her he became a different man.
>
> And why did Masha know how to do this, while no one else even dared to attempt it?
>
> With any of us something unnatural would have come of it, but with her it came out simply and heartily.
>
> I do not mean to say that the other near ones loved our father less than Masha — no, but with no one were the manifestations of this love so warm and at the same time so natural as with her.
>
> And so with Masha's death our father was deprived of this one and only source of warmth, which, toward old age, was becoming for him more and more needful.
>
> Another, still greater strength of hers — this was her extraordinarily sensitive and responsive conscience.
>
> This trait of hers was for our father still dearer than caresses.
>
> How she knew how to smooth over all misunderstandings. How she always stood up for those upon whom some reproaches fell — whether just or unjust, it was all the same.
>
> Masha knew how to pacify everything and everyone."

Here is how this event was reflected in the diary of Lev Nikolaevich:

> "26 November. It is now one o'clock at night. Masha has died. A strange thing: I experienced neither horror, nor fear, nor the consciousness of something exceptional taking place, nor even pity, grief. I considered it necessary, as it were, to call forth in myself a special feeling of tender emotion, of grief, and I did call it forth, but in the depths of my soul I was more at peace than at another's act, to say nothing of my own — a bad one, one that ought not to be. Yes, this event is in the bodily sphere, and therefore indifferent. I looked at her all the time, while she was dying — astonishingly calm. For me she was a being disclosing itself before my disclosing. I followed its disclosure, and it was joyful to me. But now this disclosure in the sphere (of life) accessible to me has ceased, that is, this disclosure has ceased to be visible to me; but that which was being disclosed, is. Where? When? These are questions relating to the process of disclosure here and that cannot be referred to the true, extra-spatial and extra-temporal life."

> "29 November. Just now they have driven away, carried away to bury. Thank God, I keep up in my former good spirit. With my sons it is now easier."

> "1 December. Again and again I recall Masha, but with good, tenderly moved tears — not over her loss to me, but simply over the solemn minute lived through with her, out of love for her."

> "28 December. I live and often recall the last minutes of Masha (I do not want to call her Masha, so unsuited is this simple name to that being which has gone from me). She sits, propped about with pillows, I hold her thin, dear hand and feel how life is departing, how it is departing. These fifteen minutes are one of the most important, significant times of my life."

Of L. N.'s literary works of this year let us mention also his article "The Significance of the Russian Revolution." This article entered into the complete collected works of L. N. and was circulated as a separate brochure, and therefore we will not here set forth its content, well known to the reading public. We will say only that L. N. saw the chief significance of the Russian revolution in the possibility which it opened to the Russian people, and after them to all others — to turn off from the erroneous path upon which the Western peoples had set out, the path of urban industrial civilization, and to enter upon a new path — that of agricultural, peasant civilization, of drawing near to the "kingdom of fools."

This possibility is open even now, and the shift of Russia in this direction is indubitable.

At this time, occupied with the compilation of the second volume of L. N.'s biography, I often turned to him for various items of information, and the letters bore a businesslike, practical character. Finally, prompted by a desire for spiritual communion with him, I wrote him a letter in which I asked for nothing, but expressed my spiritual mood. I soon received an answer and adduce it here for the conclusion of this chapter and of the year 1906.

> "Thank you, dear friend, for the disinterested little letter. I was ill the other day. I was preparing to undergo a great change. Now I am recovering. I am occupied with a *Circle of Reading* for children and the people. Peasant children come to me, and the work confronting me appears terribly difficult, but I am trying.
>
> That which you mention is, probably, a little article which I did not send, under the title 'Believe Yourself,' addressed to youths of 14–16. How are your occupations with the unhappy young people?
>
> About Plotinus I know. Your extracts are very good.
>
> How vividly, in old age, one feels the enormousness of the work confronting one and the brevity of time. It is good that the 'I' is not in oneself alone, for otherwise one might fall into despair. I kiss you. I always think of you with love. Greetings to your wife."
