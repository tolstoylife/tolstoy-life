# Volume IV, Chapter 13. 1908 (continued). The Jubilee

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-13.md`](../ru/chapter-13.md).

As the end of August drew near, despite all the resistance on L. N.'s part, one felt that his triumph was approaching. The correspondence and the number of visitors kept growing. And he, as though on purpose, was ill. So that he was unable not only to answer everyone, but even to receive all who wished to see him.

L. N., having publicly declined a jubilee, arranged one for himself. His article *I Cannot Be Silent* had raised him to an unattainable height and had drawn to him the hearts of many.

The following page of L. N.'s diary shows us in their full extent his spiritual torments and his strained striving toward the common good:

> "11 August. It is hard, painful. These last days an unceasing fever, and I am in a bad way, I bear it with difficulty. Probably I am dying.
>
> Yes, it is hard to live in those absurd, luxurious conditions in which it has fallen to me to spend my life, and harder still to die in these conditions: of fuss, of medicine, of imaginary relief, of healing, when neither the one nor the other can be, nor is needed, but there can only be a worsening of the spiritual state.
>
> My attitude toward death is by no means fear, but a strained curiosity. About this, however, later, if I have time.
>
> Although it is a trifling matter, I want to say something of what I should wish to have done after my death. In the first place, it would be well if my heirs would give all my writings into common use; if not that, then without fail everything that belongs to the people, such as the *ABC Book*, the *Books for Reading*. Secondly, although this is the merest trifle of trifles — that no rites whatever should be performed at the burying of my body in the ground. A wooden coffin, and whoever wishes — let him carry it or convey it to the Zakaz, opposite the ravine, to the place of the 'Green Stick.' At least there is a reason for choosing that place and not another.
>
> That is all. By an old habit, of which I have nevertheless not freed myself, I think that I might still do this and that, and that... strange, predominantly one artistic design. Of course, this is a trifle, and I should not have had the strength to carry it out well.
>
> Yes, 'all is in thee and all is now,' as Syutaev used to say, and all is outside time. So what can happen to that which is in me, which is outside time? Nothing but good."

Here is the true testament. "I should wish to have it done after my death." What can a Christian add to this modestly expressed wish? Nothing. And had it remained in this form, a new bright ray would have been added to the halo of wisdom that adorns the great personality of Lev Nikolaevich. But fate willed otherwise, and we shall yet return to this question.

On 15 August N. N. Gusev records an interesting conversation with Lev Nikolaevich:

> "Having returned at 11 o'clock in the evening, I looked in on L. N. He was not yet asleep and was feeling, it seemed, better than during the day. I asked him, among other things, how he had found the sayings of Muhammad which he had read today in an English translation.
>
> — There is very much that is good, — L. N. answered.
>
> I said that, in my opinion, it would be important to include them in a *Circle of Reading* for Muslims.
>
> — Yes, yes, — L. N. agreed. — I move further and further away from the authoritativeness of Christianity. The foundation is one in all faiths. No believing man will dispute that there is a God, that there is a soul, that one must love.
>
> — These thoughts, — L. N. said then, — are the fruit of this illness. I feel that this illness has brought me great profit in the spiritual respect. Had it not been, I should have been riding on horseback, should have advanced in my works; but now I have advanced in the inward, the most important thing."

In this way L. N. once again declared, as he had in his reply to the Synod, that Christianity for him is not the single and final truth, but only one of its manifestations. This is very important to bear in mind in appraising L. N.'s works.

On 21 August L. N. dictates, among other things, to Gusev, for entry in the diary:

> "One feels the approach of the 28th by the increase of letters. I shall be glad when this is over, though I am glad also that I am completely indifferent to one or another attitude of people toward me, even while I am more and more not indifferent to my own attitudes toward them."

But this indifference was broken by the heartfelt warmth of the greetings received by L. N. on that day and in the days preceding and following the jubilee.

The gathering of guests at Yasnaya Polyana on the day of the jubilee was not especially numerous — that is, their number did not correspond to the significance of the event. The cause of this was, first, L. N.'s printed declaration that he desired no celebrations of any kind, and secondly, his illness just before the very day of the jubilee. And the more delicate did not wish to trouble L. N. with their presence. But nevertheless, through these obstacles a considerable group of L. N.'s admirers broke their way.

In addition, the Synod, for its part, took pains to repel its flock from any manifestation of sympathy toward L. N. on that day. John of Kronstadt even composed a special prayer in which he begged God to remove the blaspheming count as soon as possible.

The Ministry of Internal Affairs was not so frank, and sent round circulars to the governors stating that Tolstoy's jubilee might be celebrated, but only as that of an artist, and that assemblies and manifestations to Tolstoy as a public figure were not to be permitted.

All this had the effect that the jubilee lost its character of officialdom, became more intimate and closer to Lev Nikolaevich.

We borrow the description of this day from the reminiscences of several of his friends who were witnesses of this triumph and who marked it in one way or another in their articles, combining them in such a way as to take from each that which is more clearly set forth in him, and as far as possible to fuse all this into one connected narrative.

Iv. Iv. Gorbunov-Posadov describes thus the day preceding the jubilee:

> "The first waves of the ocean of greetings, rushing from all ends of the world with words of love and gratitude to the great apostle of love and to the gifted reader of souls and portrayer of the great drama of human life, are already knocking at Yasnaya Polyana; but quietly, serenely, in a kind of solemn calm, all is here today, just one day before the 80th birthday of Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy. Only just recovering after a grave illness that all but parted him from us, Lev Nikolaevich is from the morning already at his work, sitting in his armchair, in which, dividing his time between it and his bed, he had passed so many motionless, wearisome days. Before him is a reading-stand with a notebook open upon it, into which he sometimes enters his thoughts, but most of all he dictates to his secretary N. N. Gusev, who takes down stenographically after Lev Nikolaevich and then dictates the deciphered shorthand to Lev Nikolaevich's daughter, Alexandra Lvovna, who is constantly copying out for her father all his works.
>
> The main note running through the addresses — it is boundless gratitude for his gigantic, heroic struggle for the enthronement of the light of love over humanity, the struggle for the liberation of humanity from slavery to egoism and violence.
>
> 'A greeting from American friends,' says a telegram from Cincinnati, 'to the most humane, the greatest teacher, the defender of universal brotherhood, the enemy of all tyranny, the champion of the principles of Henry George, who is laying the foundation of economic freedom and bringing nearer the day of justice and peace.'
>
> Here is a deeply touching peasant message from the south of Russia, written in the original in a completely illiterate manner:
>
> Congratulation to Yasnaya Polyana, to Count L. N. Tolstoy.
>
> 'Lev Nikolaevich, accept from us, little, humble toilers, peasants, a deep heartfelt congratulation on the day of your jubilee of 80 years, celebrated by you and by your admirers. May God strengthen your aged powers for the joy of your family and of those around you, and of those admirers who cherish and love you as a great writer and a great Christian, who has given the world so much great Christian teaching. We always rejoice in our souls that it has pleased God to manifest to the world in your person a great Christian, who has given the world an immortal Christian great teaching and his own great example in a reasonable life. And if you have enemies, why, all the great teachers of the world had them, and Christ had them, and there are such even now, enemies of Christ, establishing hell; but your Christian teaching destroys their enchanted hell, and the light of Christ has again shone forth with a great light, thanks to your great truth, and people, having understood you and your great teaching, can only bow down before you and love you and rejoice that God has sent a great man for Russia. But the homeland is ungrateful: yet the world has understood you otherwise. Knowing that you will receive congratulations from all ends of the world, we too, little people, as your enthusiastic admirers, make bold to bring our most sincere, humble congratulation.
>
> Forgive us; as we have known how to express our love to you, so we beg you to accept it.
>
> Peasants (a row of signatures), Odessa.'
>
> And dozens of greetings imbued with these same feelings from the depths of the sea of the working, laboring people.
>
> Here are greetings from Germany, from the depths of the people whose leaders, brandishing weapons, proclaim one right only in the world — the right of the stronger, the right of the better armed — greetings sending heartfelt wishes of a long, long life still to him 'whose life is dear not for Russia, but for humanity,' to him who bears to humanity a teaching 'that gives the world peace.'
>
> And from another country, from England, whose leaders are arming for a struggle with the German people, ardent expressions of gratitude 'to him who has so helped in the following of Christ, in the realization in life of the Sermon on the Mount, which teaches us to love our enemies as our neighbors.'
>
> People of different classes, of different stations, standing in the conventional social sense at the top of the human ladder and at the very bottom of it (in Lev Nikolaevich's understanding, the very opposite), merge in one brotherly feeling of love toward him who summons them all to forgotten love.
>
> But here are letters saying that love without works is dead. Here is a letter from a former overseer-woman in a little town of Russia, who writes that she has not words enough to express her gratitude to Lev Nikolaevich, but that she wishes to undertake the care of a sick child only for the sake of 'gaining the possibility of taking part by a good deed in the rejoicing over your 80th birthday.'
>
> Here are the voices of humanity to come: a touching letter from Jewish youths, a sweet letter from a girl gymnasium pupil, thanking Lev Nikolaevich 'for all that you have done for us, the young.'
>
> Here are touching letters from lives burning down — a letter from an old man, a former military man, a general, thanking Lev Nikolaevich for all that he has done for the triumph of truth on earth, and a letter from a little old woman:
>
> 'Not in a bought and venal temple, but in the creation of your great genius, Count, do I see God and worship him and revere him.
>
> A humble old woman.'
>
> In this love, in this gratitude, the past and the future merge here, there merge people of tribes, nations, and states scattered over all the earth. In this union of theirs is the prototype of that future union of humanity for which, perhaps, no one in the world has done so much as Lev Tolstoy has done.
>
> 'Be well, dear grandfather, for the happiness of the peoples,' writes one of his readers, a workman. 'For me and for many other people, you, dear grandfather, will now never die.'"

But the very day of 28 August has come too. We continue to quote the splendid description of I. I. Gorbunov-Posadov:

> "Nature at Yasnaya Polyana surrounded the 80th birthday of Lev Nikolaevich with the joyously sparkling frame of an enchantingly beautiful golden autumn day. From early morning the doors leading from his study onto the balcony were thrown wide open, and the crystal-pure air poured in wide warm waves into the study where, having seated himself in his armchair, Lev Nikolaevich was looking over letters and telegrams.
>
> At this time his kindred were congratulating him; then V. G. Chertkov presented to him Mr. Wright, who had brought from England an address covered with many hundreds of signatures from the English admirers of Tolstoy. Among the signatures under this address stood the names of many of the best-known writers (among others, the well-known novelists Thomas Hardy, Meredith, Wells, the poet Edward Carpenter, Mackenzie Wallace, Bernard Shaw, the philosopher Frederic Harrison, Kennan). Among the signatories stood the names of scholars, literary men, public figures, members of parliament, members of the House of Lords, members of distinguished clubs of social reform and of the labor party, priests of the established Anglican church and of the nonconformist, that is, free confessions, the signatures of well-known artists, musicians, actors, representatives of labor, of industry, of cooperation, and so on, and so on. Among the signatures of grown people there were to be seen children's signatures too.
>
> Here is the full text of the English address:
>
> 'We, the undersigned, and a multitude of our compatriots, who for many years have found in your writings a source of noble feelings and high enjoyment, on the 80th day of your birth wish to express to you not only the affection which we feel toward you, but also our wonder before you as a writer and a teacher of morality.
>
> The boldness and sincerity with which you have set forth before humanity new and lofty ideals have made the world come to love you.
>
> We see that, after almost half a century, time wishes to sanctify the beauty and truth of your many-sided labor, and we rejoice that your writings are now read more than ever. They attract to themselves the sympathy and affection of people who differ considerably in their opinions, and these people delight in them from various points of view.
>
> And so we here sign our names, as your well-wishers and admirers, and some of us — as your grateful pupils.'
>
> Then Lev Nikolaevich began his working day. He worked this day astonishingly much for his strength, which was only little by little growing stronger after the illness. He dictated 17 new passages for the new *Circle of Reading*, intended for the broad popular masses. This *Circle of Reading*, by the author's design, was to be, as it were, a synthesis of his whole spiritual labor.
>
> During the most grievous days of his illness, in a voice failing from weakness, he had continued to dictate thoughts for this work.
>
> In the hall, on the tables, on the grand piano, lay packets of addresses that had just arrived: from the Society of Lovers of Russian Letters, read with especial interest by Lev Nikolaevich's family; from the Society of Workers of the Periodical Press; from the Society of Lovers of the Arts, with an album of drawings by well-known Russian artists, drawn especially for him; and others.
>
> On one of the windows stood an interesting offering to Lev Nikolaevich — a melchior samovar, ordered with their hard-earned money by the waiters of the 'Farce' garden, with sayings engraved on it: 'The Kingdom of God is within you,' 'God is not in might, but in truth,' 'Live not as you wish, but as God commands,' and then an enumeration of the names of those who had presented it. On the samovar hung an embroidered Russian towel with the same signatures. They had also sent a heartfelt address.
>
> Hundreds of greeting letters, addresses, telegrams from all ends of Russia and numerous letters from abroad already lay, examined, in the cabinet for correspondence. All the time, from Zaseka, Yasenki, Tula, new packets of letters and a multitude of telegrams were being brought.
>
> On the 28th about 500 telegrams were received, among which were a multitude of telegrams from municipal, zemstvo, scientific, literary, educational institutions, gymnasiums, schools, all kinds of assemblies, unions, groups, persons of all kinds of stations and professions, and, among others, a multitude of greetings from the working folk of very many factories and works, and from peasants from various ends of rural Russia. Only this morning, the 29th, about a thousand telegrams were brought from Tula, and among them, in enormous number, foreign ones, which, being more complicated to sort out, had been held back in delivery. But the matter, of course, is not in their number, which is not surprising in view of the enormous popularity of Tolstoy throughout the world.
>
> It goes without saying that the enormous majority of the greetings honor Tolstoy as a creator of genius in art as well; but Tolstoy as the apostle of love, the prophet of brotherhood, the great fighter with violence and with 'the power of darkness' (words especially often repeated in the telegrams) is honored first and above all. For this one need only cast the most cursory glance over these enormous packets of telegrams and letters.
>
> Around the house thronged the Yasnaya Polyana children, who had received in the morning a box each of sweets with views of Yasnaya Polyana, sent to them for this day from Petersburg by Georges Borman (the grown peasants, however, received a scythe each out of a hundred scythes sent for this purpose by a certain scythe manufacturer from the south).
>
> Having then gathered on the balcony of the house, the children with noisy merriment chose for themselves to read books from the heap of 'Posrednik' children's books brought out for them from the house.
>
> Before dinner there had gathered at Yasnaya Polyana all the sons of Lev Nikolaevich (with the exception of Lev Lvovich, who was at this time visiting in Sweden the father of his wife, who at that moment was ill). The elder daughter of Lev Nikolaevich, Tatyana Lvovna, had only recently come to Yasnaya Polyana to visit her ailing father and to spend with her mother the day of her birthday.
>
> Besides the family and the kindred, there were at Yasnaya Polyana only a few (very few) of the friends nearest to Lev Nikolaevich, who were spending the summer near him.
>
> For dinner Lev Nikolaevich came out in his mobile armchair, which was placed in such a way that he could see all those sitting at the table. Before the beginning of dinner he conversed in a friendly way with some of the guests.
>
> After he had, having stayed a little while alone in his study after dinner, again appeared to the guests, he was once more wheeled out into the hall, where he remained for two hours. At first, so as not to tire himself by talking much, Lev Nikolaevich played chess with M. S. Sukhotin, his frequent chess partner. Then he conversed on various subjects with several of the guests gathered around him.
>
> S. D. Nikolaev, the translator of the works of Henry George, who was among them, drew Lev Nikolaevich's particular attention to an address, remarkable in its content, which had come that day, containing a greeting to Lev Nikolaevich from the union of the Australian leagues of land reform (of the 'Single Tax'), signed by the chairmen and secretaries of all the land federations of Australia. Lev Nikolaevich, having in the daytime only glanced cursorily over this address, now asked that it be brought again, read it over with deep attention, and at once, there in the hall, amid the lively conversation going on all around, dictated to his secretary N. N. Gusev an answer, saying that he was glad of their kind attitude toward him, and that to his last days he would work for the cause, common with theirs, of the liberation of the land from private property.
>
> In the Australian address it was said:
>
> 'Deeply revered teacher. We, the pupils and followers of Henry George from all over Australia, who call ourselves adherents of the single tax, wish to join in those expressions of love and respect which will be borne toward you from all ends of the world on the day when you attain the venerable age of 80 years.
>
> History knows few men whom God has endowed with such genius as distinguishes you, and still fewer men who would devote their genius to the service of aims so noble. As the great moral force of the historical period through which we are living, you rule over kings and rulers and will direct human life at the time when they and their deeds are forgotten. Your love for your fellow-men, your readiness to come forward in defense of all the oppressed everywhere, has kindled an answering love in the hearts of people whose life has acquired meaning and whose desires have been ennobled thanks to your example and teaching.
>
> When we learned that you had also accepted the teaching of our dear late teacher Henry George, we began with greater boldness to stand up for those ideals toward which we strive, and with greater assurance to think of the coming of that kingdom of truth in which justice will be the law of social relations and love the law of personal relations among people.
>
> Not only to us, but to all who sincerely strive in manifold ways to better the world, to those who will come into it after us, your life and your teaching will be a source of inspiration and will remain so for all ages. And when the time comes, and you join your fathers, this inspiration and the memory of you will be preserved among humanity as its most precious possession.
>
> But may that day be far off, and may your years of joyous service to the loftiest interests of your fellow-men be prolonged.'
>
> Having then listened to two piano pieces played for him at his request by A. B. Goldenweiser, Lev Nikolaevich returned to his room."

A. M. Khiryakov sums up the great event and writes thus:

> "The fourth day after the jubilee. After the festival come the days of everyday cares, and though letters and telegrams are still being brought, yet no longer in the former quantity, and one can now sum up to some degree the responses with which the homeland answered the triumph of her great son. One can at least a little make sense of this stream of love.
>
> Countess Sofya Andreyevna will gather all the letters, telegrams, addresses, and gifts and will place them in the Historical Museum in Moscow, in the section of L. N. Tolstoy, which even now is becoming crowded. The future historian will thank her from his soul for the preservation of this precious material and will work it up from all sides. But for a newspaper worker this is too complicated a task, and I shall permit myself to share with the readers only some extracts from the mass of jubilee greetings, those extracts which most of all attracted my attention.
>
> I leave aside the greetings of foreigners, among which sparkle such names as Bjørnson, Hauptmann, Bernard Shaw, Meredith, and many others. I shall not quote the addresses of cities, zemstvos, societies, other institutions — these addresses, in spite of their sincerity, still bear an official character. I shall note chiefly the greetings of little, unknown people, reaching out toward the great as little blades of grass reach out toward the sun.
>
> A letter received from Kostroma breathes an extraordinary charm of direct feeling:
>
> 'Dear grandfather, Lev Nikolaevich. Do not be angry with us that our letter may perhaps take from you so much time in the reading of it. We shall say it all very briefly. We wish to tell you that we love you very, very much for your great teaching, for your truthful, bold word, for your unceasing call to good, to truth. This is all we wish to tell you, because we very much want to say it... On the day of your jubilee, to wish you many, many good days in the future. To you a youth and a maiden send their sincere, warm greeting; accept it.'
>
> An officer wounded near Liaoyang, who lay several hours on the field of battle, recalls how truly the spiritual state of Andrei Bolkonsky on the field of Austerlitz is depicted.
>
> A doctor who had been at Port Arthur speaks of the revolting phenomena of war.
>
> 'No forces,' writes a peasant of Vitebsk Province, 'can blacken or tear out of the people that great feeling which has been embodied in it. Every word of yours is known to the people, even if it were printed abroad. The people has become sensitive and loves you for truth, for intercession.'
>
> One of those who greet him reports that he had not wished to trouble him with his letter, but that the decree of the Synod compelled him to speak.
>
> 'Perhaps,' the author of the letter concludes, 'your example will have its effect and will compel us, the faint-hearted, to be bold and to speak and to act... And then will vanish that horror of life in which we now live.'
>
> An old man from Tyumen brings L. N. ardent gratitude for the deliverance of him 'by the holy words of love from the fetters of malicious vengeance.'
>
> Especially many greetings were sent by schoolmasters and schoolmistresses. A group of Moscow schoolmasters and schoolmistresses, in bringing their gratitude for the much that is useful which they have drawn from L. N.'s works, adds:
>
> 'But for your latest works, especially for your ardent protest against capital punishment, our respectful reverence before the genius-knower-of-hearts of Russia. More and more are we convinced that each of your latest works is the highest, the most precious and strikingly beautiful in the spirit of truth and of love for man.
>
> We are little, insignificant people. We are silent before the brutalities, we cannot express that burden, that horror before what is taking place, which makes us suffer; but the greater is the response in our hearts that is found by your courageous, vivid, and talented word of denunciation of the evil and of those frenzied in malice.
>
> ...May God then give you health and strength for what you have written, not fearing persecution.
>
> For in all of Russia you alone could speak your mighty word, and speak it as it has been spoken.'
>
> A group of zemstvo schoolmasters of G—— District writes:
>
> 'Your works have become a new Gospel, and your exemplary life will serve as a model for generations to come. Ages will pass, we shall long since be gone, but this day will never be forgotten in the hearts of new and ever-newer generations.'
>
> By the number of greetings received one cannot, of course, fully gauge the striving of L. N.'s admirers to express their feelings. Many did not venture to trouble him, especially taking into account the illness of the great old man; many were stopped by the apprehension that they would not be able to express their feelings properly; and many were even afraid that their greetings might become known, and that this would inflict substantial harm upon their material life.
>
> Thus, one extraordinarily heartfelt letter ends with a bitter postscript: 'Forgive me, dear Lev Nikolaevich, that I do not write my surname, since I am a family man and fear some kind of persecution.'
>
> What a characteristic postscript, worthy of being immortalized on the pages of history! In the 20th century, for a greeting to the most brilliant man, who constituted the pride of humanity, one could fear persecution. The inhabitant of 'constitutional' Russia of the 20th century feared persecution for thoughts expressed in a private, sealed letter.
>
> It is very hard to enumerate all the greetings. Under one are displayed several dozen signatures of workers and peasants and three crosses of an illiterate old man of 76 years.
>
> Many poems. There is even a poem by some policeman, and verses by a blind girl, accompanied by a portrait. There is a telegram sent with their last pennies by proletarians, and there is a telegram from Grand Duke Nikolai Mikhailovich.
>
> Some greetings are the cry of an agitated soul.
>
> 'One wants to weep and sob at the thought that, perhaps, these lines will reach him who is so dear, who is so needful to us, and who is still with us.'
>
> A village schoolmaster begs him not to grieve over the priests and hopes that L. N. will not give them the occasion to rejoice over a 'conversion,' because this would morally kill his admirers.
>
> One of Tolstoy's admirers prays that God may give him strength to bear all the attacks from people unscrupulous, and chiefly from the unknowing, the benighted, 'to whose number I too once belonged; but, having read over your *What I Believe*, I not only came to love you, but felt the awkwardness of my conscience for my embitterment against you.'
>
> Let us adduce one last extract from the letter of a gymnasium girl. At the gymnasium it had been proposed to honor the day of L. N.'s 80th birthday, and they had wished to arrange a literary morning, and suddenly — it was forbidden.
>
> 'How grieved we were,' writes the girl, 'to pass over in silence the day of your 80th birthday. Then I had thought of not going to school on the 28th, but afterward I understood that this ought not to be done: for you have all the time striven and strive that people should not be idle, but should labor, should work; and I resolved to go on the 28th to study and to work mentally as much as possible, so that at least at first I might little by little accustom myself to laboring more...
>
> ...You have many enemies, but still more friends, and I only pity all those who have caused and cause you so much evil. For they do not understand what they do, and such ones we must pity.'"

In our literature the view of Tolstoy as the greatest artist, weakening his fame by unsuccessful gropings in the sphere of philosophy and morality, is very widespread.

In setting about reading the multitude of greetings received by Lev Nikolaevich, I expected to find in them echoes of this widespread opinion, but I was mistaken. In the enormous majority the greetings note the significance of Tolstoy chiefly as a herald of moral ideas.

I think it will be interesting to the readers to learn the opinion of Tolstoy himself about the greetings he received.

Here is the opinion of Lev Nikolaevich, recorded by a stenographer during our conversation:

> "In the enormous majority of the letters and telegrams," remarked Tolstoy, "essentially one and the same thing is said. They express sympathy to me for the fact that I have contributed to the destruction of a false religious understanding and have given something that is of use to people in the moral sense, and this one thing alone is joyful to me in it all; namely, that public opinion has become established in this respect, that the majority simply join in what all are saying. And this, I must say, is in the highest degree pleasant to me. Of course, the most joyful letters are those of the people, of the workers."

At first Tolstoy read the greeting letters he received, but afterward they proved to be such a mass that, in order to avoid excessive fatigue, one could read over only the especially interesting ones; but here there proved to be a danger of another kind: the interesting letters agitated him too much. I can say from my own experience that it was hard for me to keep from tears in reading certain of the letters. So that even the chosen letters could be read only in small portions.

In speaking of the greetings, one cannot pass over in silence the censures uttered to Tolstoy — in other words, the abusive letters. It is characteristic that all those which I had occasion to look over are anonymous. They all produce the impression of having been written from someone else's words, without any acquaintance with Tolstoy's works. It must be confessed that these letters produce a very pitiful impression. There is neither poison nor wit. Nothing but foul language.

Behind the chariot of the Roman triumphator there ran a soothsayer-slanderer who reviled him, so that the triumphator should not grow too greatly proud. Tolstoy's slanderers are not fit even for this pitiful role. Their insignificant outcries sink without a trace in the worldwide stream of love that surged irresistibly toward Tolstoy on the day of his eightieth birthday."

I. I. Gorbunov-Posadov, who looked over the mass of greetings received, gives us a splendid selection of the most significant of them. We quote here the essential part of his remarkable article.

## From the Ocean of Greetings to Lev Tolstoy

Let us hearken, then, to the voices of lives young and old, of learned and barely literate people, of representatives of so-called intellectual culture and of those toiling popular masses, giving their whole life to labor on the earth, whom many are still inclined to consider unconscious, ignorant, inert, savage herds — whereas the very content of the greetings of the representatives of the toiling masses to Tolstoy shows the enormous work of thought in the people, lays bare with striking vividness not only that searching for truth which has always lived with such force in the people, but shows how far there is clarified in the people the consciousness of that truth whose embodiment in the surrounding life could turn our sorrowful world into a promised land for all who labor and are heavy laden.

Let us begin with the greetings that have a biographical significance. Here is a greeting recalling that Lev Tolstoy was once a military man.

> "The Fifth Battery of the 38th Artillery Brigade, formerly the Twenty-Fourth, esteeming it a happiness that you served in its ranks, congratulates you on the eightieth anniversary and sends up prayers to the Most High: may He grant you strength to work yet much for the good of humanity."

Here is a greeting from Kazan, where the years of Lev Nikolaevich's youth were passed. The old Kazan University, within whose walls he studied, is silent; but instead, the young, democratic one bears the light of knowledge into the midst of the working people. The Kazan Society of People's Universities honors "the tireless seeker of truth and of the meaning of life, the great artist and thinker, the apostle of light, of good, and of brotherly union."

Deeply touching are the greetings of the future Russia, of the representatives of the new life, the greetings of hearts bearing within themselves the seeds of a better time to come, the greetings of youth, in which, in spite of all the horrors and spiritual turmoil of our days, the holy ideals are alive. In these greetings, breathing at times the naïveté of a still inexperienced pen, there is vividly portrayed that which is most dear in the seeker and fighter for truth to the better part of our youth.

> "To the preacher of peace and love, the censurer of all that is base and vulgar, the great titan of the Russian word, all the pupils of the Smolensk gymnasiums send a heartfelt greeting."

> "We join in and send to you, the preacher of love, truth, and freedom, our wishes. You have taught us to despise hypocrisy, malice, and slavery. Live long, continuing your tireless work of correcting, purifying, and ennobling people. The Smolensk realschule students."

> "To the great teacher, the cherisher of peace, of love, untiringly leading us into the kingdom of light and truth. The Yekaterinburg realschule students."

A group of Mtsensk youth greets the writer "who has all the time served the cause of the liberation of the human spirit."

> "To the ever-young, from the young. A group of women students."

> "You are for us a source of water with which we quench our thirst for the knowledge of truth, which heals us from evil, the stream of which leads us out of darkness to the light. A schoolmistress."

> "The All-Russian Teachers' Union greets the great teacher of teachers and of peoples. May your mighty call to truth, love, and freedom long, long not fall silent."

> "The Society for the Promotion of Popular Education in Narva thanks you for that enormous educational and formative material by which the people lives and will live."

> "The Narva Society of Pedagogy and Hygiene greets in your person the pedagogue who, by founding the Yasnaya Polyana school, opened new pedagogical horizons and brought a bright stream into the work of educating and instructing youth — the writer who by his works has reared and is rearing whole generations of the intelligentsia and of the popular masses, especially strong through his preaching of the moral renewal of man."

> "The Kiev Society for the Promotion of Popular Education bows down before the spiritual might of the apostle of peaceful labor, of all-forgiveness, of love, truth, justice, and holds sacred the testaments of the great Yasnaya Polyana teacher, the spiritual leader of all who labor in the field of popular education. It is still dark in Russia, but the light shines even in the darkness, and the darkness comprehends it not."

> "The Riga Pedagogical Society greets one who serves the great ideas of good, truth, union, and the brotherhood of people. The school to come, founded on the principles of broad humanity, will in a fitting manner study your immortal creations and reverently honor your name."

The League of Education "honors in your person the writer who, like Homer and Shakespeare, has immortalized the country where he was born, the great pedagogue and teacher. Together with the whole world, the League of Education followed the struggle of your mighty spirit with physical illness, and now is joyfully assured that Russia will preserve you yet for long years, the defender of all that is bright and best, in spite of all the dark forces that dare to fight with you, the ruler of the feelings and thoughts of Russia."

> "In the gloomy years, when over our homeland have hung leaden clouds, begetting slavery of the spirit and poverty of thought, when everywhere the beast prowls and man wanders timidly, in these days your word, denouncing violence and falsehood, your mighty call to the service of the people and of truth, like the noise of the sea, sounds tirelessly, and all that is advanced in Russia, like all thinking humanity, is imbued with a feeling of reverence and deep gratitude to the old man great in his solitude and solitary in his greatness, for the ideals traced upon our glorious banner, which awaken conscience, enlighten the mind, and educate society. The Yelisavetgrad Public Library."

> "For more than half a century you have amazed the world with the artistic works of your genius, with the breadth and boldness of your ideals. Your mighty word works incalculable good, awakening the moral sense, stirring the conscience, and giving wings to thought. Your life is an inspired searching for truth, for verity, and for the paths to the happiness of humanity. To you, the pride of our homeland, we send ardent wishes. Live long for the struggle with the power of darkness. The Professors of the Petersburg Polytechnic Institute."

And, finally, from the usually impassive "walls of the Academy there comes this time also a brief but ardent greeting:

> "To the great artist of the Russian word, the fearless seeker of truth, the tireless preacher of faith in the power of good and love, the vice-president Nikitin brings congratulation in the name of the Imperial Academy of Sciences."

Representatives of literature greet Tolstoy with a multitude of addresses. Here are two or three out of the hundred addresses lying before us:

> "The great, mighty, truthful, free Russian writer, the embodiment of the world conscience" — thus the editorial board of the *Odessa Review* names Tolstoy in its telegram.

> "We join our ardent greetings to those which are being sent on this day from all ends of the world to the great artist, the tireless and fearless seeker of truth. The editorial board of *Russkoye Bogatstvo* (Russian Wealth)."

> "Valiant leader of the free, bright spirit, live yet many years, until the spirit of darkness and of worldwide malice shall be destroyed. The editorial board of the *Tsaritsyn Herald*."

The Literary Fund greets "the unbending and undaunted struggle for truth, in which all draw the assurance that victory is on the side of the light and not of the darkness."

> "Transporting ourselves in thought to the center of Russia," write the railway employees from the Amur, "where all the champions of a better future greet you, Lev Nikolaevich, we bring you our best wishes, as to a great artist and a great denouncer, formidable in his truth, of the evil that is being done. We believe that more than once yet your mighty word will resound, summoning to account the enslavers and oppressors of the people. With all our soul we wish that, during your glorious life, the tormented people, warmed by mutual love, may yet manage to breathe freely with its mighty breast. The employees of the technical department of the Amur Railway."

> "To him who has united the hearts of all peoples, the tireless fighter for truth. The employees of the board of the Moscow lace factories."

From the employees of Alafuzov's store in Perm: "May God grant you to see that time when people shall become humane, and the ideas preached by you shall triumph."

> "A group of commercial employees from the Mariinsky Market in Petersburg sincerely thanks the great teacher and artist of the word, who for several decades now has been and is the champion, defender, and friend of the people, has summoned to brotherhood, has served the weak, the oppressed, and the dispossessed, has by the force of his mighty heroic talent fought with the darkness, the ignorance, and the evil of mankind. We are proud in the consciousness of having the happiness to count you a great fellow citizen."

From the employees of a cheese shop: "Making use of the occasion of your jubilee, we, people of the everyday, of petty and unnoticed labor, send you our greeting and wishes, that your lofty mind and your deeply brilliant thought may have the possibility for yet long years, as hitherto, to be a beacon and a guiding star for all humanity on the path to the attainment of truth and to the gaining of brotherly love."

A shop assistant from Nikolsk-Ussuriysky writes: "We were kept in dark cellars, our path to the light and to the spiritual life was barred. But now we are going toward the light and, greeting the apostle of light, we wish him a long life for the joy and consolation of all the benighted working people."

With an extraordinary force of consciousness and of striving toward the light there sounds the greeting of the working people:

> "The workers of the Elworthy works send an earthly bow to the great apostle of truth, the preacher of love and compassion for one's neighbor, the immortal mourner over those who labor and are dispossessed, the great master of the word, to whom it is given to set hearts aflame by the word. Scourging falsehood and violence, tearing the masks from the Pharisees and obscurantists, you have raised high above the earth the torch of truth and justice, lighting with it for humanity the thorny path into the kingdom of universal brotherhood and happiness. Your mighty word, to which the seas and lands hearken with a keen ear, has penetrated to us too, the stepchildren of fate. In our hard working life, with its privations and adversities, we do not lose our faith in the triumph of light over darkness and of truth over falsehood."

The compositors of the printing house of the newspaper *Birzhevye Vedomosti* (Stock-Exchange Gazette): "A deep bow to the patriarch of Russian literature, the beacon of Russian thought and freedom, from the humble workers of the printed word, to the glorious teacher of good and preacher of the people's truth, from those who have come forth from the people."

> "On the day of the 80th anniversary of your beautiful life," says the telegram of the workers of the former shipbuilding works in Petersburg, "when before the grandeur of your image of genius the world bows down reverently, and your name is on all lips, only the country to whose lot has fallen the great pride of being your homeland does not dare to lift its voice aloud in greeting you. Out of the stifling workshops of the works, we, people of hard labor and a hard lot, the sons of the same unhappy mother as yourself, send you a greeting, honoring in your person a national genius, a great artist, a glorious and tireless seeker of truth. We, the Russian workers, are proud of you as of a national treasure, and would only wish that to the mighty builder of the new Russia too, the working class, nature would give its own Lev Tolstoy."

> "We send a greeting to the tireless laborer of thought, the artist of the word, the defender of all oppressed proletarians, who by the force of his great talent has fought with the power of darkness. The Petersburg workers of the Meltzer factory."

> "Unnoticed toilers wish a long life for the prolongation of light, warmth, and truth in the hard life of the homeland. A group of artisans."

> "Accept the deepest gratitude for the comfort drawn from your great creations by people of humble physical labor, the significance of which for the human happiness possible on earth you have so convincingly proved to the whole world. Live, then, for long years, defender of the lesser brethren. A group of Irkutsk journeyman tailors."

Especially touching in its humble simplicity is the greeting of the tavern waiters:

> "We congratulate you on the jubilee of your 80 years in literature; we, the waiters in service, together with all to whom your prophetic word is dear, wish to honor this day. Forgive us that in this letter of ours there is no learned verbal coloring. We are people of little enlightenment, but we too have read your work, in which you teach, as a teacher of the people, your brilliant idea — it is a tidings from the very truth, which brings man to repose and to spiritual delight and shows man the true path: whence he came and whither he should strive — toward God."

Equally deeply touching is the majestic simplicity of other peasant greetings:

> "Be not silent, old man inspired by God, and live many years. A peasant."

> "Live for the glory of literature and for the enlightenment of us, the blind. A peasant."

> "To the friend of nature I send upon nature," writes a peasant, inscribing his greeting on a piece of birch bark.

> "Great writer, today you have completed 80 years — we congratulate you on the long life which you have devoted for the good of the people, who have not all yet understood you. But the time will come when each one will preserve in his soul the word spoken by you. May your life be prolonged for many years.
>
> A peasant of Vyshny Volochok District."

> "May God grant that your life be prolonged, great sower of love and truth.
>
> Peasant wheelwrights."

Here are the beautiful lines of a peasant woman:

> "I send my gratitude for your labor and your love for the people. Gold I have none, and even if a mite should be found for the raising of your monument, I am sure that there would not be capital enough on the whole terrestrial globe to buy those living stones which you have forged for your monument, for these stones are living words, which will remain in the hearts of people. Your word will not die unto ages of ages. With respect I remain one who esteems you, a Christian by conviction and a peasant woman by calling."

In love, in gratitude toward Tolstoy, the non-Russian tribes of Russia unanimously merge with the Russians:

> "The organized Christian-Muslim employees of the city of Kazan greet one who has labored for the good of humanity and of the toiling masses."

> "The Russian and Tatar deputies of the Alushta town board unanimously greet the mighty, worldwide ruler of thoughts and feelings, the wise teacher of life, the convinced preacher of all-forgiving love, the glory and pride of our peace-loving people. May your light shine in the soul of each one of the people, may there reign among all people, by your word, a gracious peace and brotherly love."

From the Latvians: "Your great works have become the common possession of humanity, and our luckless Latvian people has long since found in them incomparable treasures. Our hearts hold you in reverence."

The Latvian Society in Riga "thanks you for all your love, for all that you have done for humanity. May your inexhaustible soul be yet for a long time our companion."

> "To the venerable old man, the enlightened teacher of the present generation, a group of Finnish youth and Finnish citizens sends its ardent greeting, full of deep respect and gratitude. May the living conscience of Russia not fall silent for many years."

Among the greetings there occur greetings of representatives of various churches, confessions, and sects.

Here is the greeting of several priests:

> "I congratulate the respected Lev Nikolaevich on the 80th anniversary of the day of your birth. May the Lord preserve you yet for many years. A priest of Moscow Province."

> "Great writer of the Russian land. I greet you. May peace be with you on the significant day of your jubilee. May the Lord forgive you your sins, voluntary and involuntary, and may the Lord keep you, dear Count, and have mercy on you in the days of your old age. A priest."

To the "God-seeker" a Catholic priest sends a greeting.

> "A greeting to a free Christian from free Christians," says the telegram of a free-Christian community.

> "We congratulate you," write sectarians from Siberia, "dear benefactor of humanity, intercessor for the exiles, those wronged by fate for their religious convictions. Sincerely grateful, we pray the Most High God to prolong your precious earthly life for long years."

Let us take yet at random a series of characteristic extracts from the telegrams of various separate persons of all kinds of classes and stations.

> "We are happy that we live in the epoch of the great Tolstoy."

> "We greet him who gives us our daily bread."

> "We greet you as a beloved brother who has pointed out the meaning of life. May your name live forever among people, to the glory of God, who sent you into the world."

> "Your great teaching, of which I am a follower, has given the whole world a clear concept of what a true Christian ought to be and toward what all humanity ought to strive. Prince Konstantin Golitsyn."

Here is a letter with the inscription "to the apostle, evangelist, and prophet": "Rejoice, the fruits of your noble sowings have reached the people, and it already values you excellently and knows who is its benefactor."

> "When my soul, after something accomplished, grows bright and joyful, I think to myself: surely I have done this as I ought, as Tolstoy would have advised me to do."

> "We wish you for many years to go on furrowing just as deeply the moist mother earth, tearing out of it the tares, to prepare the field on which struggle and hatred would be replaced by concord and love."

> "Live, shine, defend man."

> "You are Tolstoy. Better, more beautiful, more honest, more radiant than this name I know nothing. An old woman."

And, finally, the artless, deeply heartfelt verses of a blind girl:

> O genius of the Orthodox land,
> Writer of sovereign Russia,
> You sought the path to salvation.
> You hungered for the bread of heaven.
> You have found the road of salvation
> And toward the narrow gate you go,
> You labor, you pray to God,
> You live by God's truth.
> For this you shall receive a reward,
> When life's end shall pass.
> You shall receive what is fitting, genius,
> You shall receive a worthy crown.

With these sincere verses of a blind child we shall conclude our selections for now."

Two weeks later L. N. records in his diary:

> "Little by little I am recovering. Jubilees — much that is pleasant for the lower soul, but harder for the higher soul. But I cannot very much complain of myself. Little by little I am scrambling out of it all. Today I took up my notebook precisely in order to set down that which I felt for the first time in the morning and at night — that the center of gravity of my life had already been transferred from the bodily to the spiritual life: I felt my complete indifference to everything bodily and an unceasing interest in my own spiritual growth, that is, in my spiritual life."

When the whole mass of greetings had passed by, and one could sum up at least approximately all these expressions of sympathy, L. N. himself did this in a general open letter, which was printed and reprinted by many Russian newspapers. Here is this letter of thanks:

> "When, several months ago now, I heard of the intentions of my friends to celebrate my eightieth birthday, I declared in print that I should very much wish that none of this be done. I hoped that my declaration would be taken into account and that there would be no celebration of any kind.
>
> But that happened which I had by no means expected — namely, that, beginning from the last days of August and up to the present day, I have received and continue to receive from various quarters such flattering greetings that I feel the necessity of expressing my sincere gratitude to all those persons and institutions which have shown me such goodwill.
>
> I thank all the universities, town dumas, zemstvo boards, various educational establishments, societies, unions, groups of persons, clubs, associations, editorial offices of newspapers and journals, who have sent me addresses and greetings. I thank also all my friends and acquaintances, both in Russia and abroad, who remembered me on this day. I thank all the people unknown to me, of the most varied social positions, down to those confined in prisons and at hard labor, who all alike greeted me in a friendly way. I thank the youths, the maidens, and the children, who sent me their congratulations. I thank the persons of clerical rank — very few, indeed, but whose greetings are for that the more precious to me — for their kind wishes. I thank also those persons who, together with their congratulations, sent me gifts that touched me.
>
> I heartily thank all who greeted me, and especially those of them who (the majority of those who addressed me) quite unexpectedly for me and to my great joy expressed in their addresses to me their complete agreement, not with me, but with those eternal truths which I have striven, as best I could, to express in my writings. Among these persons, which was especially pleasant to me, there were most of all peasants and workers.
>
> I apologize for not having the possibility of answering separately each institution and person, and I beg you to accept this declaration of mine as the expression of my sincere gratitude to all the persons who in these days expressed their kind feelings, for the joy they have given me."

Let us take a little page of L. N.'s October diary. How beautifully there is expressed in it that rapturous mood of his which almost never left him during the last years of his life:

> "What a joy, comparable with nothing, astonishing, and I experience it — to love all, everything, to feel in oneself this love, or, rather, to feel oneself to be this love. How everything is annihilated that we, in our perversity, consider evil, how all, all become near, one's own... But one must not write, one will only spoil the feeling.
>
> Yes, a great joy. And he who has experienced it will not compare it with any other, will not desire any other, and will spare nothing, will do all that he can, to obtain it. And in order to obtain it, one thing is needed, small but difficult in our perverted world — to wean oneself from hatred, contempt, disrespect, indifference toward any man whatever. And this is possible. I have done in this respect so little, and already, as it were, I have received in advance an unmerited reward. With especial force I feel now, or rather, felt just now during my walk, this great joy of love toward all. Ah, how to hold fast to it, or at least from time to time to experience it. And that is enough."

One of the entries of L. N.'s November diary expresses that physical weakening of memory and of the sense of sight in L. N. in these years, which went on parallel with his spiritual growth:

> "I walk, I sit on a little bench and look at the bushes and trees, and it seems to me that on a tree there are two large, as it were bright-orange kerchiefs; but it is two leaves on a bush standing near. I refer them to the distant trees, and they are two large kerchiefs, and they are bright-orange because I refer this color to a distant object. And I thought: the whole world, such as we know it, is, after all, only — the product of our external senses."

Side by side with this bright wave of greetings, there rose also the dirty murk of the malicious hissings of the enemies of light, under the guidance, of course, of the servants of the church.

By one of the most cynical sallies against L. N. the bishop Hermogenes distinguished himself. Here is a sample of his "spiritual" eloquence:

> "The accursed one, despising Russia, a Judas, who has strangled in his spirit all that is holy, morally pure, morally noble, who has hanged himself, like a fierce suicide, on the dry branch of his own self-conceited mind and depraved talent."

This malicious abuse grieved L. N., and he was filled with sincere pity for its author and wrote him the following letter:

> "Dear brother Hermogenes. I have read your remarks about me in the press and was much grieved for you and for your co-religionists, who acknowledge you as their leader. Let us grant that I am in error, and that, as you say, I have by my error seduced many people from the path of truth onto the path of perdition. I am one in error, I am a harmful man; but, after all, I am a man and a brother to you. If you pity those whom I have ruined by my false teaching, then how can one not pity him who, being the cause of the ruin of others, will himself surely perish? For I am that man and a brother to you. It is understood that you, as a Christian possessing the truth, can and ought to address me with a word of admonition, of reproach, of loving instruction; but the only feeling which it is proper for you, as a Christian, to have toward me — is the feeling of pity, but by no means that feeling which guided you in your denunciations. I will not speak of which of us is right in the differing understanding of the teaching of Christ. That is known only to God. But one thing is certain, with which you too, dear brother, in calm moments cannot but agree — and that is, that the fundamental law of Christ and of God is the law of love.
>
> And so, following this law, I address you, as a brother to a brother, as an elder brother to a younger, with a loving word of reproach and of admonition.
>
> You did ill, dear brother, in giving yourself up to the unkind feeling of irritation.
>
> This is ill for any man-Christian, but doubly ill for the leaders of people professing Christianity. I write to you in order to beg you to extinguish in yourself the unkind feeling toward me, who have against you no other feeling than that of love and of compassion for an erring brother, and to restore in yourself the feeling, proper to people, of love for one another. If by these words I have grieved you, then forgive me. I desire nothing but good for you. I shall be very grateful if you answer me.
> Your loving brother Lev Tolstoy."

But he did not venture to send this letter directly to the addressee; instead he sent it to his sister, the nun Marya Nikolaevna, with the following letter:

> "I have read, dear friend and sister Mashenka, your letter to Dušan. It touched me very much, almost to tears, both by your love and by that true religious feeling with which it is imbued. I send you the letter to Hermogenes. Please, do not let it out of your hands, let it be read at your place, if you find it necessary, but do not give it to be copied. I did not send the letter because it is not worth it, and chiefly because *le beau rôle* (*) is too much on my side.
>
> It is as though I were boasting of my humility. I kiss you, dear friend.
> Your very loving brother Lyovochka."

(* "the fine role." *)

A little page of the diary of that time again points us to the deep inner work that was going on within L. N., and to that guiding principle with which his whole moral life was imbued.

> "3 December. A very good spiritual state. I slept much. I began by seeing in myself all my vileness, the predominance of human glory over the genuine demands of life. I saw this (which I had also long since sensed) both in the heavy feeling from the letter of some woman reproaching me for the letter, and from the interest with which, reading the newspapers, I sought with my eyes for the word 'Tolstoy.' How far I still am from even the slightest bit of decency, how bad it is! Now I write this and ask myself: and is it not for those who will read this diary that I am writing it? Perhaps, in part, yes; one must work upon oneself; now, at 80 years, to do that very thing which I did with especial energy when I was 14–15 years old: to perfect myself, only with the difference that then the ideals of perfection were others — both muscles and in general what is needed for success among people. Ah, if only one could accustom oneself to lay all, all one's energy upon the service of God, upon drawing near to Him! And drawing near to Him is impossible without the service of people. And even if I lived in the desert and died known to no one, I still surely know that my self-perfecting, my drawing near to Him, is needed. Help, help me to live by Thee. I write this, and tears come. It is good."

As L. N. says in his diary, that drawing near to God, that is, self-perfecting, is impossible without the service of people, he found time to serve people in the most strenuous manner, saving many from moral and physical sufferings.

L. N.'s friend and like-minded companion V. A. Molochnikov, in a letter to me from the prison where he was confined at this time for the dissemination of L. N.'s works, related the following story of how L. N. delivered from senseless and absurd sufferings several people doomed by the stupefied authorities to long-drawn-out torment.

> "I was already sitting in the Novgorod prison (since 30 June 1908), and at the same time as me there languished in the same prison 14 peasants of Krestetsk District, Paportniko-Ostrovskaya Volost. They had been seized on suspicion of organizing a village 'brotherhood of land and freedom.' There were no proofs, and therefore, without any charge being presented to them, they were listed, as they say in prison, 'under the governor.'
>
> It was felt certain that they would be exiled administratively. Moreover, they had probably even been forgotten about. All were placed in various cells. An old woman of 60, her son, and her 20-year-old grandson were placed so that, being in one and the same prison, they could neither see nor speak to one another.
>
> All had been torn from their family nests, had already been sitting for months, and knew no end of it.
>
> In his letter to me of 7 July Lev Nikolaevich asks, among other things, for 'commissions.' I wrote to him about the languishing peasants. It was hard to send the letter on, but it succeeded. My fellow-prisoners chuckled at my 'naïveté' and did not admit the possibility of the peasants' liberation from prison.
>
> About two weeks after my letter they were liberated, to the general joy. I remember how this unexpected joy called forth a universal rapture. For a long time we watched from the window, on a hot day, their radiant faces. Even the sentries were glad and allowed us to wave rags amiably from both sides. The joy was also egoistic: it had been very crowded. In the July heat, with the prison walls red-hot, in cells each calculated for 4 persons, there were kept 9–10 persons. Though from this side the joy was not very long-lasting..."

How much of this little-noticed good has been scattered through the world by L. N., "and it is precisely of it that the wreath of his glory is woven," adds to this story L. N.'s devoted pupil, the locksmith Molochnikov.

Let us conclude the description of this year of 1908, so remarkable in L. N.'s life, which raised him to an unattainable height of moral grandeur, by adducing yet one more interesting letter of his, addressed to one of his Eastern friends, a Hindu:

> "Recently I received a letter addressed to me in the newspaper *Aurore*, from the French, very witty writer Loyson. The letter concerns precisely this astonishing — I do not know how to name it — inner contradiction, or misunderstanding, or superstition, or simply a notion that has become established in society.
>
> This article was in August, but it reached me only a few days ago, and I was very glad of it, glad because this article, written by a very intelligent man, contains in itself, definitely expressed, all the usual arguments against non-resistance, and at the same time, by its naïveté, illustrates better than anything else that astonishing want of reflection which has become established in the scientific world regarding this question.
>
> The essence of the objections against non-resistance, voiced in all the discussions on this question in Loyson's article, which is in its way brilliant, consists in the following: 1) the law of love, if non-resistance be admitted, does not agree with, and is directly opposed to, the law of the struggle for existence and the selection that proceeds from it; and since this is a 'scientific' law, while the law of love is religious, then the scientific is true and the religious is false.
>
> The answer to this objection must present itself of its own accord to every man who is not under the influence of scientific superstition, and naturally consists in this: that if man has the qualities of reason and love, which are absent in animals, then the law of beings not possessing these qualities cannot be the guide of human life.
>
> The second objection is that, if the principle of non-resistance were accepted as the chief law of the life of people, the consequence of it would be the triumph and power of the evil over the good — that is, there would be that very thing which now exists and which is acknowledged by all thinking people; from which, naturally, follows the conclusion that, if the acknowledgment of a law contrary to love and resistance has led people to the triumph of the evil over the good, then all probabilities are that the acknowledgment of this opposite law would lead also to opposite consequences.
>
> The third objection, or rather, consideration, proceeding from the first two objections, is that for the guidance of humanity in its life there is needed not love (this is fit and necessary only for the personal perfection of certain individuals, somewhat as the occupation with some art or amusement), but justice is needed, that justice which manifests itself in law, in the civil statute.
>
> I will permit myself not to object to this objection, since it is too clear that if justice demands the killing of Louis XVI, then that same justice demands the killing of Marat and others, the killing of Alexander II and of his killers.
>
> Yes, it is time for people to understand that without the acknowledgment of love as the highest law of life there is not and cannot be any teaching or any conception of good and of morality, any movement forward of humanity. And without the acknowledgment of that which was uttered by Christ and which proceeds of its own accord from the conception of love, without the acknowledgment of non-resistance, there is not and cannot be any love.
>
> And nothing can replace for humanity the conception of love, of true love toward all."

Let us note yet one more significant phenomenon of this year in connection with L. N.'s jubilee. P. A. Sergeyenko, the author of the interesting book about L. N., *How L. N. Tolstoy Lives and Works*, conceived the idea of gathering the opinions of outstanding contemporary writers and thinkers about the jubilarian dear to him, and addressed an inquiry to a whole series of well-known persons. He received a large number of answers and published them in a collection, naming it *An International Almanac on Tolstoy*.

In this collection there are more than 50 various articles from the pens of outstanding writers of the Old and the New World. We shall adduce here several of the most characteristic, which will give us a notion of L. N.'s significance and influence beyond the bounds of his homeland.

Let us hear the voice of the wise Hindu-Muslim, Abdullah-al-Mamun-Suhrawardy. This evidently outstanding son of the East, having found satisfaction in the reading of L. N.'s works, wrote him a letter and received an answer. With that their personal communication ended, but an inner, spiritual bond was firmly established. Here is what he answered, among other things, to the inquiry addressed to him about his attitude toward Tolstoy:

> "I am a follower of Islam, the religion with which violence and bloodshed are usually associated. And yet I am a pupil of Tolstoy. I am a champion of peace and of non-resistance. This may seem paradoxical. But the paradox vanishes if one reads the Koran as Tolstoy reads and interprets the Bible — in the light of Truth and Understanding.
>
> The teaching of non-resistance, so tirelessly preached by Tolstoy, corresponds more to the East, and especially to India, which has grown akin to the teaching of Gautama Buddha. And the preaching of Tolstoy, merging with those teachings of the prophets and sages who were once renowned in that historic country, will manifest, perhaps, in our times also messiahs and mahdis who, crucified upon crosses, will bless those who crucify them.
>
> My dream — to express to Tolstoy personally my reverence before him — has not come true. And, it must be, will not come true in this life. We exchanged with him only one letter. And yet his charming personality is clear to me. Tolstoy, like Muhammad, is one of us, and not a superman who would look upon you from the height of his grandeur, as upon poor little people; he does not abuse his admirers and does not crush them.
>
> Light — is light from God, and not light from the East or the West. For light to shine, it is indifferent whether it burns in a golden, silver, or earthen lamp; whether it is Chinese, Russian, or Arabian. This Russian count, this teacher and prophet — is the object of my veneration. I feel the kinship of my soul with his soul. I too have passed through the valley of doubts and trials, of dejection and despair. And, not seeing it, I walked the same path as Tolstoy. And although I am only thirty years old, yet I bear within me those same experiences which gave to the world Christs, Buddhas, and Tolstoys."

How touching is this union of souls between persons so heterogeneous in their outward semblance. And how consoling it is that there exists among people this inner homogeneity.

Let us now transport ourselves from the East to the West and hear the voice of an American woman, outstanding in mind and in moral qualities. Her name is Lucy Mallory. L. N. was in correspondence with her. He placed many of her thoughts in the *Circle of Reading*. Here is what she wrote about L. N.:

> "There is no doubt that Lev Tolstoy is a great leader, teacher, and reformer of the contemporary epoch. Before him, humanity had never yet had a leader whose influence embraced the whole world.
>
> There have been other so-called leaders who, within the bounds of a known locality and for a time, by means of bloodshed and pillage, by force compelled people to follow them. But no people has ever yet attained an enduring welfare by the path of violence.
>
> But the only force which Tolstoy used was the force of love and of wisdom. And therefore his influence will never weaken, but will continue more and more to develop the beauty and harmony of life; for love creates itself, exists in itself, and contains within itself the beginning of eternal growth — of development and of perfection."

Lucy Mallory noted a very characteristic trait of Tolstoy's genius: that he, denying all violence, conquers the whole world. His force is love and wisdom. And this force determines his worldwide significance.

Let us turn to the more modest opinion of the well-known French writer Paul Margueritte. His opinion is brief, elegant, and profound; he expresses his opinion of Tolstoy thus:

> "A profound moralist, he renewed the Christian feeling and restored in the contemporary conscience the sense of the just and the unjust. He is the moral light of elect humanity, and we bow down with deep reverence before the writer who was an apostle, and the apostle who was a man."

As in the opinion of the Hindu Mohammedan, so in the opinion of the French novelist we see an indication of Tolstoy's humanity as his characteristic attractive peculiarity. This trait it was that gave his genius the quality of universal humanity.

The Belgian writer Charles Sarolea visited Tolstoy in 1905, during the previous revolution. And here the perceptive Belgian already sees the role which Tolstoy must play in the worldwide revolution. To the Belgian writer, who had already seen the glow of the spreading conflagration, the significance of Tolstoy presents itself in all its grandeur, and he says:

> "Under this ominous glow of conflagrations, all of Tolstoy's creative work presented itself to us as clothed with a new significance. Life itself explained and confirmed it. That which had seemed the dream of a poet was becoming a historical reality. That which had appeared contradictory now fell into place and occupied its corresponding position in the harmonious whole of his entire creative work. Tolstoy, as an apostle of the idea, rose to his full height and stood high above the warring sides, above the rebels against crude force. Having risen high above the perpetrators of the destroying revolution, Tolstoy is the prophet of the creating revolution."

This creative role of L. N. must with especial force manifest itself in the present great Russian and worldwide revolution. One cannot imagine Tolstoy at a machine-gun, a participant in civil war; but neither can one imagine him a counter-revolutionary, destroying the values gained by the revolution. His creative role must give a moral foundation to the new social order.

Tolstoy's influence penetrated also into the land of the Rising Sun, into the Far East. The Japanese Naoshi Kato also speaks of the revolutionary significance of Tolstoy. And he did, indeed, accomplish a spiritual revolution in that astonishing country. Kato describes this upheaval thus:

> "When in 1902 and 1903 there appeared in Japan translations of Tolstoy's new works, it was interesting to observe how Tolstoy's religious thoughts penetrated into every winding of the Japanese mind and, like gunpowder hidden in the crevices of rocks, exploded with great force, shaking to their foundations all existing theories and principles. It was almost a revolution. Not only the Christians, who are progressive enough to be on the level of contemporary thought, came to the knowledge of the terrible real truth lurking in the religion they profess, but even the Buddhists found a source of inspiration in the books of Count Tolstoy. Much indicates that the renewal which has shown itself in recent years in Buddhism has here its beginning.
>
> Were Tolstoy's influence confined to the religious world of Japan, it would be saying too much that his thought shook the spiritual world of Japan to its foundations. But the fact is that his books found zealous readers and followers among the younger generation of Japan, which stood outside religion. To tens of thousands of young Japanese there was revealed the religion of Christ in bold relief and in the simplest form, which, consciously or unconsciously, had been concealed from their gaze under the covering of various dogmas and vain conventions. The light cast by Count Tolstoy upon the realm of reason was like radium penetrating as many layers as lie in its path. The thick armor existing for protection against the contagion of religious epidemics proved too thin for the bright light of understanding. Thanks to this light people found their own religion, issuing from the depths of the soul, and not grafted on by the external world under the name of church and dogmas. 'Religious consciousness' — these are the most popular words soon after the appearance among us of Tolstoy's books."

To kindle in indifferent souls the flame of religious consciousness is a superhuman work, the work of a prophetic genius.

And yet Tolstoy did not even create a teaching of his own. And there were people who clearly recognized this unsystematic quality of his and at the same time bowed down before his worldwide might. We adduce in conclusion the words of the American writer Carus. Here is how he expresses it:

> "The teaching of Tolstoy is far from representing any harmonious theory or system. The religious ideas of Tolstoy, his moral principles, non-resistance to evil, his views on war, the state, money, and so forth — all this serves as the subject of the liveliest discussion, but only a few thinkers venture to defend this teaching, bold to the point of audacity. But there is no necessity whatever to agree with Tolstoy in order to be filled with wonder at the man who is such a vivid embodiment of the eternal demands of the spirit, which find their highest expression in these noble impulses. They, these impulses, do not bear a personal character, but are the expression of the world consciousness of the Universal Spirit by which we were created, of God Himself, with whom we live and move."

However modest the jubilee was, it undoubtedly established the greatness and worldwide significance of Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy.
