# Volume IV, Chapter 12. 1908. *I Cannot Be Silent*

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-12.md`](../ru/chapter-12.md).

---

N. N. Gusev in his diary gives the following picture of the first New Year's evening at Yasnaya Polyana:

> "When L. N., having already taken leave of everyone, intended to withdraw to his own room, a small general conversation started up. Little by little everyone rose from the table and surrounded L. N. A circle formed, like a round dance.
>
> — Come, strike up, what are you waiting for, — said L. N.
>
> — As along the se-e-ea-shore... — began Andrei Lvovich.
>
> All took one another by the hand and went round the circle. All of us, people of various social positions and various views on life, L. N. united for several minutes in the one feeling of carefree merriment, which always brings people together."

This picture of the Yasnaya Polyana manners is very characteristic; I myself had more than once to take part in the manifestation of the feeling of merriment at a sign given by Lev Nikolaevich. This points to his joy in life, to the absence in him of that gloomy, "monastic" trait which so often draws hypocrisy after it and repels sincere people. Sincerity was one of the most precious qualities of L. N., and in association with him it was easy to be sincere. One could not even be otherwise.

And this joy in life harmonized wonderfully in him with the very deepest seriousness, with which his whole life was pervaded, especially the last years. On this same day he wrote in his diary:

> "1 January. For the first time, with an extraordinary new clarity, I became conscious of my spirituality: I am unwell, I feel a weakness of the body, and so simply, clearly, easily there presents itself a liberation from the body, not death, but liberation from the body; so clearly there became evident the indestructibility of that which is the true 'I,' that this I, it alone, really exists, and if it exists, then neither can it be annihilated, like that which, as the body, has no real existence. And it became so firm, so joyful. So clear became the perishableness, the illusoriness of the body, which only seems to be.
>
> Can this new spiritual state really be a step forward toward liberation? I think that it is, because just now I called Ivan and felt something especially joyful and near in association with him. God grant, God grant. As though I felt the liberation of that which alone exists: love. Ah, if only it would remain so until death and would so be transmitted to people-brothers."

By L. N.'s own testimony, in this year his correspondence increased considerably, as he supposed, in view of the approaching jubilee and his growing fame. In N. N. Gusev's diary there is an interesting specimen of such correspondence of L. N. with his correspondents. Here is what Gusev sets down on 3 January:

> "More than once I have heard from L. N. that when he receives letters and sees on the envelope an address written correctly and clearly, then such letters interest him less than letters with an address written illiterately and by an unaccustomed hand. The letters of working people are more interesting to L. N. than the letters of the intelligentsia. Here is one of such illiterate, in the highest degree pregnant letters, received recently:
>
> "To His Excellency, Lev Nikolaevich, Mr. Tolstoy. Lev Nikolaevich, we turn to You for help, as You are a great and worldwide writer, and especially a religious guide, and so we too have resolved to turn to You, Lev Nikolaevich, the factory workers of the Yartsevo manufactory. And especially do we turn to You, great worldwide writer, excuse us please that we are illiterate and uneducated, in the dark, and have resolved to turn to the great teacher to resolve for us the difficult questions, especially of religion.
>
> Since we do not know whence we are to find the true path to salvation, we have therefore resolved to turn to You. Please, Lev Nikolaevich, resolve for us the difficult questions about the church sacraments: baptism, chrismation, unction, marriage, communion. We have resolved to turn to You to resolve for us these sacraments, especially marriage and baptism: we beg, please, write us an answer, with what else to replace these sacraments and how to receive them in our actions.
>
> And further we turn to You, send the translated Gospel, and as for its cost, we will at once send the money.
>
> And further we too cannot, and again, although the truth is met with in the Gospel, yet we shall understand nothing whatever, so we too turn to You, tell us, how one must pray to God, must one perform any actions or not; please, Lev Nikolaevich, explain to us these mysteries and send an answer. And further we beg You to send, if possible, any instructive books, especially religious ones. Goodbye, Lev Nikolaevich. Excuse us for turning so impolitely to your excellency, but our darkness undoes us; herewith we sign."

How joyful it is for L. N. to receive such letters is evident from his answer to this letter, written on 30 December:

> "Kind friends and brothers, it was very pleasant to me to receive your letter, because from it I see that you are occupied with the most important questions in the world, namely — how to live, in order to fulfil the will of Him Who sent us into the world. It will be still more joyful to me if the books I am sending you answer your questions. I believe in that which is set forth in these books, *The Christian Teaching* and *The Gospel*, and, trying to live by this teaching, the more I live and draw near to death, the more I feel joy and tranquillity.
>
> Your brother, Lev Tolstoy."

Let us close this month with an extract from the diary of Lev Nikolaevich of 31 January:

> "We, like animals, wish to do good to those who do it to us, and evil to those who do us evil. As reasonable beings we ought to do the contrary. Good is more needful than anything else to the one who does us evil, who is evil. Good is especially needful to those who do evil, not to us, but to anyone whatever."

And further he adds:

> "To love enemies, to do good to those who have done us evil, is not a heroic feat, but only the natural inclination of a man who has understood the essence of love. To do good to those who love, to love those who love, is not love, and does not give that particular, unique, greatest good proper to love. This good is given only by love toward people who do us evil, who in general do evil."

After the revolution of 1905, by the manifesto of 17 October a certain religious freedom had been granted, permitting the formation and registration of sectarian communities.

Many of our like-minded companions considered such registration a criminal compromise; others, on the contrary, saw in it the practical realization of their social ideals.

To the number of the latter belonged I too, with a small circle of friends who sympathized with me. And so we resolved to write a declaration concerning the registration of our community.

It was proposed to me to draw up this declaration according to the established form, which I hastened to do. The difficulty and responsibility of such a document consisted in this, that it was necessary to satisfy at once brevity and clarity of exposition, and at the same time to set forth those important foundations which defined once for all our relation to the authorities.

In view of the importance of this matter I resolved to turn for advice to Lev Nikolaevich. He, with the wisdom and goodness proper to him, understood the enormous significance of this act and edited it with his own hand. The original autograph of this redaction has been deposited by me for safekeeping in the Tolstoy Museum in Petrograd; here I adduce a copy of the chief part of it. At the beginning of this declaration there followed formal answers to the questions about the place, composition, name of the community, and so forth, and then there followed a brief exposition of the fundamental views. It was precisely in this part that L. N.'s corrections occurred.

He almost re-made my exposition anew, and after his corrections it took the following form:

> "We, the undersigned, members of the community of 'Free Christians,' unite upon the common foundations of the Christian teaching, recognizing as its essence the teaching of love not only toward those who love us, but also toward our enemies. Alien to political aims, our society, uniting in unity of beliefs, leaves to the conscience of each of its members his relation to the existing order and the powers that be, although the relation to the government that flows from our belief is full submission to all its orders that do not contradict the fundamental demands of the Christian teaching of love toward God and one's neighbour.
>
> "He that loveth not knoweth not God; for God is love. He that saith, 'I love God,' and hateth his brother, is a liar: for he that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?" (1 Epistle of John 4, 8, 20.)
>
> In these words of the evangelist John the very essence of our religion is excellently expressed.
>
> Setting as the aim of our life the fulfilment of the will of the Father of life who has sent us, we do not consider obligatory for ourselves the letter of the Holy Scripture recognized by the church, and we are guided in our life by the religious-moral wisdom of all times and peoples.
>
> Holding that the one true manifestation of faith is a life conformable to it, we, neither establishing nor recognizing any external religious rites whatever, consider obligatory for ourselves only a pure moral life and love toward one's neighbour.
>
> In communicating all these items of information, we beg that our community be registered and that an order be made for the keeping at the town council of books for the registration of the civil status of the members."

Unfortunately, the law of that time required for registration no fewer than 50 members, and we, in our scatteredness, could not for a long time collect this number of signatures, and therefore our community was not finally registered; but by that same law, having begun the matter of registration, we received the right to assemble freely and to discuss our economic and religious questions, and our assemblies took place regularly in Petersburg and served for many as a guiding little light.

This document, however, remained a testimony to the social wisdom and breadth of views of Lev Nikolaevich.

To Ivan Mikhailovich Tregubov, who had turned to Lev Nikolaevich with a similar question and had sent him his own exposition of faith, he likewise answered with a friendly letter, which began thus:

> "I received your letter, dear I. M., and the project of the community, and I hasten to answer you. In general I will say that I rejoice at your good activity and am glad to assist you in whatever I can."

From the month of February the first signs of jubilee bustle had already appeared in Russian society. In this year, in August, Lev Nikolaevich was to complete his 80th year. Many out of deep sincerity, others out of idleness, a third sort out of vanity, but there arose a whole wave of initiatives for the celebration of L. N.'s 80th birthday. These preparations brought L. N. not a few painful minutes. Let us hear the witness of all these experiences, N. N. Gusev:

In his diary of 27 February he sets down, among other things:

> "From the very beginning of January there has been talk in the press of the necessity of celebrating the 80th birthday of L. N., which falls on 28 August of the present year. In Petersburg a special 'Initiative Committee' was formed, as the people who took upon themselves the initiative in the matter of this celebration called themselves. To Lev Nikolaevich all these preparations for his exaltation are grievous by their artificiality, their pompousness, their insincerity and falseness, so little corresponding to the ever-present naturalness, sincerity, modesty, and simplicity of his life and views.
>
> However, up to the present day L. N. has not protested against all these preparations. But today S. A. received a letter from the aged Princess Dundukova-Korsakova, a contemporary, it seems, of L. N., in which she writes of how this honouring of a man who has violated their beliefs will offend all the believing Orthodox. This letter touched Lev Nikolaevich very much, and in a voice trembling with tears he dictated into the phonograph an answer to it:
>
> "Dear Maria Mikhailovna — I would say sister in spirit, if I knew that you would permit me to call you so. I have just read your letter to my wife, which deeply touched me. You have disclosed to me that which, in my levity and egoism, I had not thought of, and that which you have disclosed to me is very important.
>
> The jubilee exaltations being prepared for me are in the highest degree — I will not say grievous — agonizing to me. I am so old, so near to death, so much desire to go thither, to go to Him from Whom I came, that all these vainglorious, pitiable manifestations are to me only grievous. But all this is for me personally; whereas I had not thought of that of which you write to me: of that grievous impression which will be produced upon people who believe just as you do, who believe sincerely and deeply — what impression will be produced upon them by these exaltations of a man who has violated that in which they believe.
>
> Of this I had not thought, and you have reminded me of it. I shall try to rid myself of this bad business, of my participation in it, of the offence to those people who, like you, are far, incomparably nearer to me than all those unbelieving people who, God knows for what, for what aims, will exalt me and speak those vulgar words that no one needs. Yes, dear Maria Mikhailovna, the older I become, the more I am convinced that all of us who believe in God, if only we believe sincerely, all of us are united among ourselves, all of us are sons of one father and brothers and sisters among ourselves. Whether we wish it or not — we are all one. So then, farewell, dear Maria Mikhailovna. Thank you for remembering me. Communion with you is very joyful to me. If I were with you, I would ask permission simply to kiss you, as a brother a sister. But now, farewell. I thank you for your love and beg you not to deprive me of it."

In dictating the last words, L. N. could no longer hold back the tears that kept rising to his throat and that interrupted his words.

Following upon this letter, as if rejoicing that there was now a fully sufficient occasion to ask for the cessation of the preparations for the exaltations being readied, L. N. dictated into the phonograph a letter to M. A. Stakhovich, one of the members of the "Initiative Committee":

> "Dear Mikhail Alexandrovich, I know that you truly love me not only as a writer, but as a man as well, and, besides, you are a sensitive man and will understand me. For this reason I turn to you with a great, great request. My request is that you put a stop to this jubilee that has been started, which, besides suffering, and worse than suffering — a bad act on my part — will bring me nothing else. You know that always, but especially in my years, when one is so near to death — you will learn this when you grow old — there is nothing dearer than the love of people. And it is this very love that, I fear, will be violated by this jubilee. Yesterday I received a letter from Princess Dundukova-Korsakova, who writes to me that all Orthodox people will be offended by this jubilee. I had never thought of this, but that which she writes is perfectly just. Not in these people alone, but in many others as well, it will arouse an unkind feeling toward me. And this is to me the most painful of all. Those who love me, I know them and they know me, but for them, for the expression of their feelings, no external forms whatever are needed. So then, my great request to you is this: do what you can to abolish this jubilee and to set me free. Forever shall I be very, very grateful to you. Yours lovingly, Lev Tolstoy."

The same lamentations are met with in other contemporary letters written by Lev Nikolaevich to his friends: thus he writes, among other things, to Nazhivin:

> "That which you write about my dreadful jubilee is, surely, not so grievous for you as it is grievous for me. I am doing everything I can to put a stop to it, but I see that I am powerless."

At last, to N. V. Davydov, the chairman of the Moscow jubilee committee, who had come to him, he dictates yet another letter and asks that it be read at a session of the committee. Here is this letter:

> "Gracious sir, Mr. Editor. I am sending you the enclosed letter. Such letters from people who relate negatively to my impending jubilee I have received several; this letter, however, I very much beg you to print, as its author desires. I, on my own part, should likewise wish it printed, since in connection with this letter I have something to say regarding this impending jubilee of mine.
>
> What I have to say is precisely this, that this jubilee being prepared is exceedingly grievous to me. The causes of this are many. One of the first is that I have never looked with sympathy upon honourings of this kind: it seemed to me that the expression of sympathy and love for the activity of a man can be expressed in no way by external means, but by a close union in thoughts and feelings with the one to whom these thoughts and feelings refer. I recall how long ago already, about thirty years back, during the honouring of Pushkin and the erection of a monument to him, dear Turgenev called on me, asking me to go with him to this festival. However dear and pleasant Turgenev was to me then, however much I prized and highly valued (and value) the genius of Pushkin, I refused. Knowing that I distressed Turgenev, but I could not do otherwise, because even then honourings of this kind seemed to me something unnatural and — I will not say false — not answering to my spiritual demands. Now, however, when this concerns me personally, I feel this in a far greater degree still.
>
> But this is the last consideration. The other, the most important, is that which is expressed in this letter and in other letters of the same kind, namely, that these honourings being prepared, even in their very preparation, arouse in a great number of people the most unkind feelings toward me. These unkind feelings might have lain without expression, but they are forced out and developed in consequence of this. I know that these unkind feelings have been called forth by myself: I myself am to blame for them, to blame by those incautious, harsh words with which I permitted myself to discuss the beliefs of other people. I sincerely repent of this, and am very glad of the occasion to declare it. But this does not change the matter itself. In my years, standing with one foot in the grave, the one thing that is desirable is to be in love with people, as far as is possible, and to part with them in these very feelings. This letter, however, and others like it that I receive, show precisely that the preparations for the jubilee arouse in people — and perfectly justly — the most contrary feelings toward me. And this is very grievous to me. If on one pan of the scales there lay the approvals, most pleasant and flattering to me, of people whom I respect, and on the other — the hatred aroused even of a single man, I think that I would not hesitate to renounce the praises, only so as not to increase the dislike of that one man. Now, however, I feel that this jubilee being prepared arouses unkind, unloving feelings toward me, which I have deserved, not of one, but of many and many, of very many. This is agonizingly grievous to me, and therefore I would beg all those good people who love me to do everything possible in order to abolish all attempts at honouring me.
>
> I will not speak of the fact that I quite sincerely do not acknowledge myself as deserving those honourings which are being prepared: all this would seem to be some sort of false coquetry. But I cannot help saying what I think, and would be happy if people would leave this matter alone and do nothing in this direction."

N. V. Davydov, in handing over a copy of this letter to the Tolstoy Museum, furnished it with the following note:

> "This letter was dictated by L. N. Tolstoy and handed over on 25 March 1908 at Yasnaya Polyana to N. V. Davydov for the reading of it at a session of the Moscow committee for arranging Tolstoy's 80th jubilee. It was being prepared for print, but afterward Lev Nikolaevich changed his mind. N. Davydov."

Original in this same sense is L. N.'s letter to his old friend A. M. Bodyansky.

N. N. Gusev tells of this letter thus:

> "Today (12 March) I received from A. M. Bodyansky a letter in which he, among other things, writes:
>
> "I wrote my opinion as to how Lev Nikolaevich's jubilee ought to be celebrated, but the newspapers did not place it. I wrote that, in accordance with the laws, and therefore with the accepted truth, Lev Nikolaevich ought to be put in prison for the day of the jubilee, which would give him a deep moral satisfaction. This thought I developed somewhat and supported with proofs."
>
> Having read this letter, I, while L. N. was still out on his walk, laid it, together with the letters received today addressed to him, on his table, supposing that it would be interesting to him.
>
> Indeed, at his lunch, L. N. said to me:
>
> — How Bodyansky delighted me! Truly, that would be a satisfaction to me. The other day I was thinking what I desire, and I answered: I desire nothing, except that they should put me in prison. I dictated him an answer into the phonograph.
>
> How touching is this answering letter of L. N.'s! He says in it (and one must hear with what sincere suffering this was said by him): "Truly, nothing would so fully satisfy me and give me such joy as precisely this, that they should put me in prison, in a good, real prison, stinking, cold, hungry.""

The same mood is reflected in L. N.'s diary of that time as well; thus, on the 10th of March he sets down:

> "Exactly a month I have not written. I was occupied at the writing-table with an article. It does not go, yet I do not wish to leave it. The inner work, however, thank God, goes on without ceasing and better and better. I wish to write that which is being accomplished within me, and how that is being accomplished which I have told no one and which no one knows. Many letters, visitors. There were none especially important; they have started up a jubilee, and this is doubly grievous to me: both because flattery is stupid and disagreeable, and because, by old habit, I slip into finding in it not pleasure, but interest. And this is repugnant to me. Ch. was here. It was especially good for me with him. About a week ago I fell ill: I had a fainting fit. And it was very good for me. But those around me make of this something important. Yesterday I read a wonderful article by a Hindu in Nazhivin's translation — my own thoughts, unclearly expressed."

And so, in spite of all these counteractions, not only on the part of L. N. himself, but also on the part of the authorities, the jubilee nevertheless took place. Out of the abundance of the heart the lips of the people spoke, and, perhaps, precisely thanks to all this resistance it came out especially cordial. But of this further on.

In the diary, in the above-cited extract, L. N. mentions a fainting fit.

N. N. Gusev tells of this fainting fit thus:

> "Today (2 March) there was a fainting fit with L. N. This happened at about 4 o'clock in the afternoon. Before this he had dictated to me his translation of Victor Hugo's story *Un Athée*. This story, unknown, it seems, to L. N. and read by him now for the first time, produced upon him a very strong impression. The content of the story is that a young man, who has left the priesthood because he has come to an atheistic world-view, sets forth in detail to his interlocutor his materialistic views, according to which there is no God, no soul, no ideal; the aim of life is to live for oneself alone. But when, five months after this conversation, the wreck of the ship on which he was sailing occurred, he, forgetting all his reasonings, by which it came out that enjoyment is the one aim of life, throws himself into the sea to save perishing women and himself perishes. At the last words of this story Lev Nikolaevich wept, and, having finished dictating his translation to me, sobbed aloud.
>
> Upon the conclusion of the recording — adds Gusev — I did not leave at once, but began to put the phonograph in order. L. N. walked several times up and down the room. Suddenly I ceased to hear his steps. I instinctively glanced in his direction and see — he is slowly, slowly sinking onto his back. I ran up to him, supported him by the back, but had not the strength to halt the falling of his body, and in my arms he slowly sank to the floor.
>
> At my cry there came running S. A., who was in the dining-room, called the manservant, and we lifted up L. N.; he sat down on the floor, but evidently had not yet come to himself and uttered disconnected words: 'Leave me... I shall fall asleep presently. There was a pillow somewhere here... Let be, let be...'
>
> We laid him on the divan. After about 5 minutes he came to himself and remembered nothing of what had happened to him.
>
> In the evening L. N. got up, went out into the dining-room, and asked for dinner, but ate very little. He seemed to have forgotten everything — forgot what his near ones and relatives were called and the very places best known to him. He could not recall where Khamovniki is... What does this mean?
>
> The physicians Nikitin and Berkengeim, summoned by telegram from Moscow, arrived."

Such fainting fits afterward recurred several times and pointed to the stages of the weakening of his physical powers, toward which L. N. related with a religious-philosophical calm.

At this time the elder daughter of Lev Nikolaevich, Tatyana Lvovna, was living abroad, in Switzerland, and, of course, kept up an active correspondence with her father.

In March of this year L. N. wrote her an interesting and substantial letter, an extract from which we adduce here with the permission of T. L.:

> "First of all, the carrying out of your commissions: the signed cards I enclose. To *Good Health* answer the following: I gave up eating meat about 25 years ago, felt no weakening whatever upon giving up meat food, and never felt the slightest privation, nor any desire to eat meat. I feel myself, in comparison with people (with the average man) of my age, more strong and healthy. But I cannot, I have no ground to ascribe this to the experiment of not eating meat. I think, however, that the non-use of meat is useful for the health, or, rather, that the use of meat is harmful, because such food is immoral: and all that is immoral is always harmful, both for the soul and for the body. The ancients said: 'mens sana in corpore sano'; one ought to say the reverse: the health of the soul, that is, the following of its laws (the moral ones), gives health to the body. That is all. If you wish, translate this and send it with my signature.
>
> My health, both bodily and especially spiritual, is very, very good; it seems that it could not be better, yet with each day it becomes better. I try in the best way possible to bear (since it is impossible to stop it) that noise which they make around my signboard, and little by little I try to express that which may, perhaps, be needful to someone and which it seems to me that I know."

And here is a little page from his diary of that time, pointing to his unceasing inner work.

> "...I meet with people, I remember, and for the most part forget that which I wished to remember: that he and I are one. It is especially difficult to remember during conversation. Then the dog Belka barks, hinders my thinking, and I grow angry and reproach myself for being angry. I reproach myself for being angry at the stick over which I stumble... Returning from my walk, I take up the letters. Begging letters irritate me. I remember that they are brothers, sisters, but always too late. Praises are grievous. Joyful is only the union that is expressed. I read the newspaper *Rus*. I am horrified at the executions, and, to my shame, my eyes seek out a T. and an L. N., and when I find them, it is rather disagreeable. I drink coffee. I always fail to abstain — it is superfluous — and I sit down to letters, articles."

An original, profound thought:

> "Christianity in no way consists, as some erroneously think, in not obeying the government, but in obeying God."

It is interesting to set this thought beside another, which N. N. Gusev adduces in his diary.

On 5 April he sets down thus:

> "Only today, — said L. N., — was I thinking of how impossible it is for us to foresee the consequences of one or another social order — monarchical or republican. Could the French Revolution have foreseen Napoleon? To us now all this seems clear, but at the time people did not at all foresee it."

Continuing on this theme, L. N. gave a new, interesting definition of socialism:

> "Socialism, — he said, — is the realization of the ideas of Christianity in the economic sphere."

At the beginning of April there recurred with Lev Nikolaevich a fit of loss of consciousness, though not going so far as a fainting fit. The fit expressed itself in a loss of memory; he ceased to recognize and could not recall the names of his near relatives sitting at the table.

This forgetfulness continued on the next day as well, and then, after a good sleep, it all passed off, leaving no trace.

Fate had prepared for Lev Nikolaevich a special kind of sufferings and persecutions. They persecuted his friends and like-minded companions, and he suffered for them and employed all his powers and all his influence to lighten their lot.

Thus, in April of this year his like-minded companion Molochnikov, a locksmith from Novgorod, was subjected to persecution. He was committed for trial for the dissemination of the works of Lev Nikolaevich. He sent the bill of indictment, consisting of extracts from the incriminated articles; in this way, this bill of indictment represented a sort of proclamation for the propaganda of these ideas. This circumstance occupied Lev Nikolaevich very much. He took this case very much to heart, and, telling of it to Gusev, said:

> — I, sinful man that I am, want to go to Petersburg and to present myself at the trial and to say: "here he is, the accused."

— L. N. wished to write to Molochnikov, that he should put him forward as his defender; "that surely ought to have an effect," he said.

As in other judicial matters, L. N. turned to his friend N. V. Davydov for advice and wrote him the following letter:

> "Dear Nikolai Vasilyevich. Again to you with a request. I enclose the bill of indictment, drawn up against a man near to me; I enclose also his letter, to give you an idea of the man himself. What am I to do? My plan is twofold: either to go myself to Petersburg, to offer to be his defender, or to submit a declaration in which to express that the books were received by him from me, that if anyone is to blame, it is I, and if anyone is to be tried, it is precisely I; the books I receive from the publishers, and when people ask them of me, I give them to those who ask. How am I to act in this case? Instruct me, or draw up, if possible, such a declaration, or advise me to go myself to Petersburg and be the defender. I await an answer. The bill of indictment and the letter, please, return.
>
> Yours lovingly, Lev Tolstoy.
>
> 1908, 11 April."

N. V. Davydov dissuaded L. N. from going to defend Molochnikov, considering that such a noisy demonstration would rather harm him. Molochnikov was condemned, and L. N. felt himself to blame in this; he wrote to N. V. Davydov:

> "I have just received a piece of news that has greatly grieved me, dear Nikolai Vasilyevich, that Molochnikov, of whom I wrote to you, has been sentenced to confinement in a fortress for a year.
>
> I cannot express to what a degree this has agitated me. I cannot understand that which is going on in the heads and, chiefly, the hearts of the people occupied with the drawing up of such sentences. I am sorry that you dissuaded me from the defence. I, of course, would not have defended, but would have tried to appeal to the voice of conscience of those unhappy people who do such deeds. Can anything be done now? Very, very much do I thank you for what you sent. Goodbye.
>
> Lev Tolstoy."

Lev Nikolaevich expressed his indignation in a special article, printed in the newspapers: "On the Trial of Molochnikov." But Molochnikov nevertheless had to serve out his punishment.

At the end of April L. N. writes in his diary: "They treat me assiduously. Shchurovsky was here. Great zeal, but, like all of them, he wishes to know and believes that he knows, but knows nothing. For several days, and indeed almost always, I have been unwell. Yesterday it seems that I finished the article. Today, lying in bed in the morning, I lived through a feeling long not experienced, of doubt in everything. In the end there remains all the same one thing: good, love — that good which no one can take away. Yesterday I received a reproachful letter, point by point, from a youth-Marxist, and, to my shame, it was grievous to me. Still it is far from living only for the soul (for God), and still human glory disturbs me. Yes, as Pascal truly says, there is only one true good: that which no one can either take away or give. If only one could learn to acquire it and to live for it."

What boldness and sincerity one must have, in the 80th year of life, for a preacher of a new religion to confess before all (L. N. knew that his diary was being read and would be read) that doubts in everything sometimes come upon him. The firmer for that is the remnant which did not waver even amid these doubts. Good, love — doubt did not dare to touch these foundations.

On 21 May L. N. had interesting and pleasant guests — children. N. N. Gusev describes their visit thus:

> "Yesterday, in the fifth hour of the day, there were from Tula 120 children, pupils of the railway school, with 6 teachers. All of them had bouquets of flowers in their hands. As they were leaving for home, some 6 or 7 of them, when L. N. was no longer there, offered us (M. A. Schmidt and me) their bouquets. It was touching how they all took off their caps and cried: 'good day,' when M. A. came out (the meeting of L. N. by them I did not catch). Lev Nikolaevich distributed booklets to all of them: to the younger ones — *For the Little Ones*, to the older ones — his folk stories, and to the teachers — his *Thoughts on Enlightenment and Education*. He set the phonograph going for them, putting on an arrangement of a story by Leskov. It was joyful and touching."

L. N.'s sufferings from the lack of correspondence of the life around him with his world-view became the more acute the more he himself rose spiritually and made of himself ever greater demands. And the thoughts called forth by these sufferings he, as always, entered in his diary. Here are some of these thoughts of that time:

> "My life is good in this, that I bear all the burden of the rich life that I hate: the sight of those who labour for me, the requests for help, the condemnation, the envy, the hatred — and do not make use of its advantages, even by way of loving that which is done for me, of helping those who ask, and so on.
>
> The day before yesterday I received a letter with reproaches for my wealth and hypocrisy and the oppression of the peasants, and, to my shame, it pains me. Today the whole day I am sad and ashamed. Just now I rode on horseback, and it seemed so desirable and joyful to go off as a beggar, thanking and loving all. Yes, I am weak, I cannot constantly live by the spiritual 'I.' And when one does not live by it, then everything touches one to the quick. One thing is good, that I am dissatisfied with myself and ashamed — only let me not be proud of this."

At the end of May Lev Nikolaevich finished his article entitled *I Cannot Be Silent*, called forth by the unceasing Stolypin terror.

N. N. Gusev tells thus of the circumstances accompanying the appearance of this article:

> "The death sentences carried out daily, for nearly two years now, in great numbers, had long already made him suffer agonizingly. In some of his latest articles L. N. had already written of the madness of the bloody reprisal being carried out by the government against its conquered enemies. But that which he wrote had no effect upon those who had it in their power to put a stop to these horrors, and the executions continued. The news, printed in the newspapers, of the execution on 9 May at Kherson of 20 peasants struck L. N. especially painfully, as the most cruel and insolent manifestation imaginable of that enslavement and outrage against the best estate of the Russian people — the peasantry — which is unceasingly carried out by a minority of idle and depraved people. Under the oppressive impression of this news L. N. began to write his article 'On the Executions.' I remember with what a joyful expression of face, barely holding back his tears, on that day when he began this article, he silently showed me the sheets of paper covered with his sweeping handwriting, and when I asked him: 'Is this something new?' — he, with the same significant and joyful expression of face and with the same tears in his eyes, silently nodded his head. As soon as L. N. began to write this article, from the very first day that hopeless, depressed state in which he had been before this gave way to a vigorous, confident one. I remember how, several days after this, at lunch, to S. A.'s words that nothing could be done to bring it about that the executions should cease, L. N. in a firm and confident voice retorted: 'How can nothing be done. Very much can.'"

How L. N. himself looked upon this article and why he wrote it is evident from what he told me three days ago:

> — I simply want to print it as quickly as possible, simply want to shake it off myself. There, come what may, but I have done my part.

In order to write this article, L. N. carefully collected material through competent persons, so that the facts adduced by him in the article are taken from real life. It fell to me to take an indirect part in the gathering of these materials, as is testified by the extracts adduced below from L. N.'s letters to his friend N. V. Davydov. Thus, in the first letter he wrote:

> "I have a request to you: if it is tiresome to you to carry it out, do not do it, but if you do carry it out, I shall be very grateful. I need to know the details about capital punishment, about the trial, the sentences, about the whole procedure; if you can furnish me with the most detailed of them, you will very much oblige me. My questions are these: by whom the case is initiated, how it is conducted, by whom it is confirmed, how, where, by whom it is carried out, how the gallows is set up. And how the executioner is dressed, who is present at this... I cannot say all the questions, but the more there are of details, the more needful this is to me."

And in the following letter he writes:

> "Very, very grateful to you, dear Nikolai Vasilyevich, for the two notes about capital punishment received by me today through P. I. Biryukov. You promise me the protocols. I shall also be grateful, if this does not burden you. The notes are very interesting and important. I should like to be able to make use of them.
>
> Forgive me for burdening you. I am very grateful to you. And how I should wish to be able, thanks to your help, to express even in the hundredth part and to call forth in people the horror and indignation which I experienced in reading your note."

This article was sent out to all the Russian newspapers and to the chief agents for the translation of L. N.'s works abroad. The German translator sent it out to all the chief German newspapers, and on the appointed day it appeared at once in all languages, throughout the whole cultured world. In Germany alone it appeared in 200 different editions.

As soon as this article appeared in the Russian newspapers, there followed repressions against those who had printed it; the greater part of the newspapers resolved to print only fragments.

*Russkiye Vedomosti* was fined 3,000 rubles for printing fragments of *I Cannot Be Silent*. Provincial newspapers that reprinted fragments of this article from the metropolitan ones were also fined.

In Sevastopol the publisher of a newspaper printed *I Cannot Be Silent* and posted the newspaper up about the town. He was arrested.

Then there began to be received sympathetic, and afterward also abusive, letters from readers of this article.

Here is a specimen of a sympathetic letter: a lady, a theosophist from Kaluga, writes:

> "NN, already almost an old man, in deep agitation wrote you a few words about his impression from your article. To us he told how, having met an acquaintance of his, he asked him whether he had read your article. And at the affirmative answer he involuntarily said:
>
> — Do you know what, I felt that I too wish that they should put on my neck a soaped rope...
>
> — And I too wish this, — answered the acquaintance."

But there were also embittered letters.

On the very day of the jubilee L. N. received a parcel from a certain lady. The parcel consisted of a box in which there was a rope and a letter of the following content:

> "Count. An answer to your letter. Without troubling the government, you can do this yourself, it is not difficult. By this you will render a benefit to our homeland and to our youth. A Russian Mother."

N. N. Gusev was so struck by this parcel that for some three days he did not venture to tell L. N. of it. But he received it perfectly calmly and dictated to Gusev the following answer:

> "M. M. I very much regret that, surely without wishing it, I have aroused in you such grievous feelings, probably for yourself as well, which are expressed in your letter. You will gladden me very much if you will explain the cause of your unkind feeling and will try to extinguish it within yourself. I fear that you will take this for an empty word, but I say quite sincerely: condoling with you, Lev Tolstoy."

This famous rope, with the box in which it arrived, and with the address of the recipient and of the sender, is now in the Tolstoy Museum in Moscow.

In this way this denunciatory word flew round the whole world. Abroad they called it "Tolstoy's Manifesto," pointing thereby, as it were, to that significance of a spiritual government which L. N. had acquired by his bold pronouncements.

Of course, such pronouncements of L. N. attracted to him the best, the most daring minds of the civilized world. One of such sensitive people was the then still young English writer Bernard Shaw, who sent L. N. his book. L. N. answered him with a cordial and substantial letter:

> "Dear Mr. Shaw.
>
> I beg you to excuse me for not having until now thanked you for the book sent by you through Mr. Maude.
>
> Now, re-reading it and having turned my especial attention to the places you indicated, I especially appreciated the speeches of Don Juan in the Interlude — 'the Scene in Hell' — (though I think the subject would have gained much from a more serious attitude toward it, and not in the form of a casual insertion in a comedy) and *The Revolutionist's Handbook*.
>
> In the first I, without any effort, fully agreed with the words of Don Juan, that the hero is the one 'he who seeks in contemplation to discover the inner will of the world... and in action to do that will by the so-discovered means' (*) — that very thing which in my language is expressed by the words: to know within oneself the will of God and to fulfil it.
>
> (* "He who seeks in contemplation to discover the inner will of the world, and in his actions to apply that will by means of the method discovered by him." *)
>
> In the second, however, I was especially pleased by your attitude toward civilization and progress, that quite just thought, that however much the one and the other may continue, it cannot better the condition of humanity if people do not change.
>
> The difference in our opinions is only in this, that according to you the betterment of humanity will be accomplished when simple people become supermen or new supermen are born; according to my opinion, however, this very thing will be accomplished when people cast off from the true religions, including Christianity, all those excrescences which deform them, and, having all united in that understanding of life which lies at the foundation of all religions, establish their reasonable relation to the infinite origin of the world and follow that guidance of life which flows from it.
>
> The practical advantage of my method of freeing people from evil over yours is that it is easy to imagine that very large masses of the people, even little or not at all educated, may accept the true religion and follow it, whereas for the formation of supermen out of those people who now exist, as also for the birth of new ones, such exceptional conditions are needed as can as little be attained as the correcting of humanity by means of progress and civilization.
>
> Dear Mr. Shaw, life is a great and serious matter, and all of us in general, in this short interval of the time given to us, must try to find our destination and as far as possible to fulfil it the better. This relates to all people and especially to you, with your great talent, your original thinking, and your penetration into the essence of every question.
>
> And therefore, boldly hoping not to offend you, I will tell you about the defects that have appeared to me in your book.
>
> The first defect of it is that you are insufficiently serious. One cannot speak jestingly of such a subject as the destination of human life, and of the causes of its perversion and of that evil which fills the life of our humanity. I should have preferred that the speeches of Don Juan should not be the speeches of an apparition, but the speeches of Shaw, and likewise that *The Revolutionist's Handbook* should be ascribed not to a non-existent Tannery, but to a living Bernard Shaw, responsible for his words.
>
> The second reproach is that the questions you touch upon have so enormous an importance, that for people with such a deep understanding of the evils of our life and such a brilliant capacity for exposition as you, to make of them only a subject of satire may often harm more than promote the resolution of these important questions.
>
> In your book I see a desire to astonish, to strike the reader with your great erudition, talent, and intelligence. And yet all this is not only not needful for the resolution of those questions which you touch upon, but very often distracts the reader's attention from the essence of the subject, attracting it by the brilliance of the exposition.
>
> In any case, I think that this book of yours expresses your views not in their full and clear development, but only in an embryonic condition. I think that these views, developing more and more, will come to that single truth which we all seek and to which we all gradually draw near.
>
> I hope that you will forgive me if you find in that which I have told you anything disagreeable to you. I said what I said only because I recognize in you very great talents and feel toward you personally the most friendly feelings, with which I also remain.
>
> Lev Tolstoy."

Let us close this chapter with an extract from the diary of that time, in which there sounds that note of sorrow which in the last years became habitual in L. N.'s mood.

> "I have lived through very grievous feelings. Thank God that I have lived through them. A countless quantity of people, and all this would be joyful, were not everything poisoned by the consciousness of the madness, the sin, the vileness, of luxury, of servants, and — of the poverty and the over-strained tension of labour all around. Without ceasing, I suffer agonizingly from this, and alone. I cannot but desire death. Although I wish, as far as I can, to make use of that which is left."
