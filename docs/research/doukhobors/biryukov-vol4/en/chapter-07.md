In this year L. N. was especially occupied with the elucidation of the world-view that had newly taken shape in his mind.

Already at the beginning of January we see in his diary an entry that clearly defines his attitude toward life; he gives a new definition of the concept of life and points to those philosophers who come near to his understanding of the world.

He speaks thus:

> "In order for my understanding of life to be intelligible, one must take the standpoint of Descartes, that man surely knows only this — that he is a thinking, spiritual being — and one must clearly understand that the most strictly scientific definition of the world is that the world is my representation (Kant, Schopenhauer, Spir). But what then is this spiritual being which I call myself, and what is the cause of my representation of the existence of the world? To these questions, in defining life, I answer thus: life is the consciousness of a spiritual being, set apart from all the rest, existing in unceasing communion with the All. The limits of the separateness of this being from the All present themselves to me as my body (matter) and as the bodies of other beings, which make up the All. But the unceasing communion of this separate spiritual being with the All presents itself to me in no other way than in time. The limits of my spiritual being, appearing in space, I cannot apprehend otherwise than through my own body and the bodies of other beings. And the communion of this being with others I cannot apprehend otherwise than through the movement of my own and of other beings.
>
> Were there no separateness of my spiritual being from the All, there would be no body of mine, nor bodies of other beings. And in just the same way there would be no movement of my separate being. There would be no movement of all the other beings either. So that life is the consciousness of the separateness of my spiritual being, bounded by limits, from some other, boundless spiritual being, which makes up the All and the Beginning of all."

In this same year L. N.'s correspondence was especially abundant; he corresponds with the whole world in various languages, and many of his letters constitute whole articles setting forth his world-view.

Such is his letter to the French writer Hyacinthe Loyson, son of the well-known Father Loyson, who had abandoned Catholicism.

This letter is especially interesting in that L. N. speaks in it himself of the significance of his writings and rejects every attempt to exalt him to the rank of an apostle, a reformer — a thing to which his superficial admirers are so prone. Here is this letter in translation:

To Monsieur Loyson (son of Father Hyacinthe).

> "Dear Sir. I have received your letter, and also your article, which I read with great attention. To my regret, I cannot tell you that your appraisal of my writings is wholly correct.
>
> You shower me with praises, and at the same time you reproach me with strange errors and even with the absence of foundations. All my critics — to my regret, you too are no exception — reproach me for my attacks either upon the church, or upon science, upon art, and especially upon every kind of violence employed by governments. And some of them call this simply stupidity or madness, others inconsistency or extravagance. They give me all sorts of flattering names: genius, reformer, great man, and so forth — and at the same time they do not allow in me the simplest common sense, that is, they maintain that I do not see that the churches, science, art, governments are necessary for societies in their present form. This strange contradiction arises from the fact that my critics, in judging me, do not wish to abandon their own point of view and to take mine, which, by the way, is very simple. I am not a reformer, not a philosopher, and still less an apostle. I am simply a man who has lived a bad life, and who has understood that the true life consists solely in the fulfilment of the will of him who sent me into this world. Having found in the Gospel the foundations for the true life, I cast off the illusory life and have lived a life only in accord with these foundations of mine. From this point of view it is clear that if I attack the churches, the governments, the sciences, the arts, this is not from any pleasure in attacking, not because I do not understand what significance men ascribe to them, but solely because, having found these institutions contrary to the fulfilment of God's will, which consists in the establishment of the Kingdom of God on earth, I cannot but reject them. For those who judge of things objectively, on the basis of observations and reasonings, the existence of the churches, of science, of art, and especially of governments, must seem necessary and even inevitable. But for such a man as I, who knows the inner certainty that flows from religious consciousness, all these observations and reasonings have no weight whatever, when they are in contradiction with the certainty of religious consciousness. I am not a reformer, not a philosopher, not an apostle. But the one small merit which I allow in myself is logic and consistency.
>
> The reproaches that are made to me, in considering my ideas objectively, that is, from the point of view of their application to the life of the world, are like the reproaches that would be made to a husbandman who, having sown grain in a place where there had been little trees, grass, flowers, and pretty paths, did not take care to preserve all this. These reproaches are just from the point of view of those who love the trees, the greenery, the flowers, and the pretty paths, but these reproaches are quite mistaken from the point of view of the husbandman, who ploughs and sows his field in order to feed himself. The husbandman is quite consistent and logical, and cannot but be so, because, in doing what he does, he does it with a clear and definite aim in view, that of feeding his body. Were he not to do what he does, he would risk dying of hunger. So it is with me too. I cannot but be consistent and logical, because, in doing what I do, I pursue a clear and definite aim — that of feeding myself spiritually. Were I not to do what I do, I should risk dying spiritually."

Of the same weighty character is his letter of that time to his old friend Al. Andr. Tolstaya, of 23 February. In this letter L. N., with peculiar delicacy, turns aside from himself every attempt at "conversion" and sets forth, as it were, his own creed, which is love.

> "I too, dear friend Alexandrine, did not want to answer your letter, and I too have no thought of entering into polemics. But I want to talk about how a difference of religious convictions neither can nor ought, not only to hinder the loving drawing-together of people, but neither can nor ought to call forth in people the desire to convert a beloved person to their own faith. I write of this because I have lately come vividly to understand it — to understand that every sincere religious person, such as I consider you and myself to be, must have his own faith, answering to his mind, his knowledge, his past, and, above all, his heart — his own faith, out of which he cannot pass; and that for me to wish that you should believe as I do, or for you to wish that I should believe as you do, is just the same as to wish that I should say I am hot when I am shivering with cold, or that I am cold when I feel that I am burning with fever. This truth has long been known to all, and I have only of late felt it with my heart, and understood how a man's faith (again, if it be sincere) cannot lessen his merits, nor my love for him. And from that time I have ceased to wish to impart my faith to others, and have felt that I love people quite independently of their faith, and attack only the insincere, the hypocrites, who preach what they do not believe — those whom alone Christ condemns. For one need only think of those millions, those milliards of people — Hindus, Chinese, and others — who for generations live and die without even having heard of that which constitutes the object of my faith. Can it be that they are not my brothers, children of one Father, God, because they believe quite otherwise than I, and that I must dissuade them from their faith and persuade them into mine? No, I think that we must first of all love one another, and strive to draw together as closely as possible. The more we love one another, the more we shall feel ourselves at one in our hearts, and the more insignificant will the disagreements of our minds and words appear to us. You, no doubt, have long known this; but I have only of late understood it with my whole being, and it has become very good and easy for me from this. This is the only thing I wished to say. I received your third note also, about the death of N. P. (*) I am very grateful to you. There is much else there of great importance. Farewell, brotherly and tenderly I kiss you and your hand without a finger."

(* The Emperor Nikolai Pavlovich. *)

At this time there took place in Europe an event which, it would seem, should not have touched L. N. at all, but which nonetheless caused him a great deal of trouble.

The Duchess Louisa of Tuscany abandoned her husband and went off with the tutor Giron.

Someone set going a rumour that she had done this under the influence of Tolstoy's writings, and so there were sent to him inquiries as to what he thought of it. Idle people divided into parties for and against the flight, and L. N. happened to express himself incautiously against it, and thereby to call forth the reproaches of the sympathizing party, so that he was barely able to extricate himself from this tangle.

Here is his first letter about this event, addressed to the editorial office of the newspaper "New York World" and written in answer to their inquiry:

> "I do not intend to condemn the conduct of the unhappy woman of whom you wrote to me. It is said: 'Judge not, that ye be not judged.' I only maintain that in all that I have ever written there is not a single word by which such conduct could be justified. I profess the Christian teaching, and it requires of us, above all, that we sacrifice our pleasures, our happiness, for the good of our neighbour. In the case of which you write, just the opposite has occurred. This woman sacrificed the peace and happiness not only of her husband and her father-in-law, but of her children as well. Especially keenly does the eldest son, probably, suffer, and indeed he will suffer always, all his life, from the shame of a mother who sacrificed everything for the pleasure of an unhindered cohabitation with the seductive Monsieur Giron. Such is my view of this dirty story, with which the whole world busies itself in vain."

This letter appeared in the newspapers, and protests against his cruelty began to pour in upon L. N. He had in fact become sorry for the unhappy duchess, and he held it his duty to come forward in print with a confession of his blunder. He did this through the editorial office of the newspaper "Yuzhny Krai" (The Southern Region), where the protest had been printed; here is his confession:

To the editor of "Yuzhny Krai," 23 Feb. 1903.

> "Mr. Editor! Today I received the enclosed letter:
>
> 'Count! having read in the newspapers your letter concerning the Saxon Crown Princess Louisa — a letter in which you say that you profess the Christian teaching — I was left in perplexity: which teaching of Christ do you acknowledge, the one offered us by the church dominant in the state, or else the pure teaching of Christ, which acknowledges God as spirit, Truth, and Love? If you acknowledge the first, then how is it that you attack its dogmas? If the second — then how can you speak in such an ironical and proudly contemptuous tone of an unhappy woman, who has already paid so dearly and is redeeming her transgression by deep suffering? You say: "judge not, that ye be not judged," and there and then you pronounce your judgment, the judgment of a Pharisee over a publican: "I thank thee that I am not as this publican," forgetting that Christ, whom you so revere, would have said in such a case: "He that is without sin, let him cast the first stone at her."
>
> If you will deign to resolve my perplexity, I beg you to place your answer in one of the issues of "Yuzhny Krai" or in some other newspaper, since "Yuzhny Krai" will, of course, reprint your answer. As a matter of fact, I do not count on receiving an answer, since the answer could and ought to be your self-accusation of inconsistency at the very least, and this you will not do… out of self-love!'
>
> The history of my letter about the princess is the following: having received from Berlin a letter from an Englishman, who asked me how far it might be true that the views I had expressed could have influenced the princess in her act, I in an evil hour dictated to my daughter my answer. Usually my daughter gives me the letters that are sent off to look over, and I intended to look over, to correct, or to destroy this letter altogether. But it happened that the letter was sent off together with the others. This was so unpleasant to me that soon afterward I wrote to Chertkov in England that, in the event of my letter's being printed — which I did not expect, but which might nonetheless happen — I asked him to print my letter to him, in which I acknowledge my letter to the Berlin correspondent to be coarse, cruel, and un-Christian. After this I received a letter from a certain Saxon, who, just like the Kharkov correspondent, quite justly reproached me with the inconsistency, and above all the coarseness and cruelty, of my letter, and also an open letter of similar content in the 'Petersburger Zeitung.' To the Saxon I replied, describing to him the circumstances under which the letter had appeared, and expressed in it my repentance for having permitted myself, even though in a private letter, to utter so cruel and un-Christian a judgment about an unhappy woman; moreover, I gave him the right, if he should find it necessary, to publish my letter to him. Up to now, so far as I know, neither in the English nor in the German newspapers has there appeared either my letter or my answer to the Saxon, and therefore, taking advantage of the occasion offered by the Kharkov correspondent, I beg you, Mr. Editor, to print my letter in your newspaper."

At this time L. N. rises to a new moral level of complete religious tolerance. He expresses this in his diary. Thus, on 13 March of this year he records:

> "God is the whole infinite world. We men, however, are within a sphere, not at its centre, but at some place or other (everywhere is the centre) of that infinite world. And we men make in our sphere little windows, through which we look upon God — one from the side, one from below, one from above; but we all see one and the same thing, although it presents itself to us differently and we call it differently. And the conclusion drawn from what is seen through the little windows is, for all, one: let us live all in concord, in friendship, in love. Well, and let each man look through his own little window and do that which flows from this looking. Why, then, push men away from their little windows and drag them to one's own? Why even invite a man to abandon his own — it is a bad one, forsooth — and invite him to one's own? This is even discourteous. If anyone is dissatisfied with what he sees through his own, let him of himself go up to another and ask what is seen from there, and let him who is satisfied with what he sees relate what he sees. This is useful and is permissible.
>
> I am very happy that I have become quite, really tolerant in matters of faith. And it was the intolerant who taught me this."

As an illustration and an application of these thoughts to life there may serve the following letter, written a week earlier to a man who evidently diverged far from him in his convictions — in all probability an Orthodox believer:

> "I quite believe that your reasoning, having confirmed in you your faith, has strengthened you in it and given you peace. Judging by this, you must think that any man who has seriously and sincerely set before himself the question of the meaning of his life cannot but establish his attitude toward life and toward its Beginning — an attitude consonant with the degree of his development and the sincerity of his search for truth. For more than 20 years now I have established my attitude toward God and the demands that flow from such an attitude, and with this attitude I have lived ever since, and the longer I live, the more I am strengthened in it, and, drawing near to death, which I expect every day, I experience complete peace and an equal joy both in life and in death. My belief is not in agreement with yours, but I neither tell nor advise you to abandon yours and to adopt mine. I know that this is just as impossible for you as it would be to change your physiological nature: to find a relish in what is repugnant to you, and the contrary. And therefore I not only do not advise you to do this, but advise you to hold to your own and to work it out further, if it is susceptible of perfecting and development. A man can believe only in that to which he has been brought by the totality of all his spiritual powers. Each of us looks upon the world and upon its Beginning through that little window which he has himself made or freely chosen, and therefore it may happen that a man who sees dimly and whose little window is unclear may pass of himself, by his own will, to another's window; but to call a man who is satisfied with what he sees away from his little window to one's own is quite groundless and, at the least, discourteous.
>
> We all see one and the same God, all live by His will, and all can, looking upon Him from different sides, fulfil His chief law — to love one another, notwithstanding the difference of our view of Him.
>
> I wish you the most important thing in life: to find in your faith that peace, that invulnerability and freedom, which a true faith in God and in His law gives."

6 March 1903.

The following letter is, as it were, a new confession of faith, and clearly points to the spiritual development of the personality of Lev Nikolaevich:

> "I received your letter and am very glad that I can answer your question. I have come to the conviction — not by way of reflection, but by the experience of a long life — that human life is spiritual. Man is a spirit, a particle of the Deity, enclosed within certain limits which we apprehend as matter; but the life of the spirit is not subject to any distortion, still less to suffering. It grows always evenly, expanding the limits within which it is enclosed. Nevertheless it is natural to men to fall into the error of thinking that the essence of life lies precisely in the bounds that limit it, that is, in matter. Under the influence of this error we look upon material sufferings, and in particular upon sickness and death, as a misfortune, whereas sufferings (always inevitable, like death itself) only destroy the bounds that confine our spirit, and bring us back — by annihilating the delusion of materiality — to that understanding of his own life as a spiritual, and not a material, being which is natural to man. The stronger the material suffering, the nearer the suffering that seems to us the greatest, death, the more easily, the more inevitably is man freed from the delusion of the material life, and the more surely does he come to know himself in the spirit. True, in coming to know himself in the spirit, man does not receive those keen enjoyments which the animal, material life gives, but in return he feels complete freedom, invulnerability, indestructibility, he feels his union with God, the foundation and essence of everything. Then death no longer exists, or presents itself as a liberation and a rebirth; he who has experienced such a state would not exchange it for any material enjoyment. I speak thus because I myself experienced this with extraordinary force during my illness.
>
> In recovering, I experienced two opposite feelings: the one — the joy of an animal returning to life; the other — the regret of a spiritual being at the loss of the clarity of the spiritual consciousness that was present in me during the illness. But, notwithstanding all the temptations of the temporal life, awakened with new force in convalescence, I believe, I know, that the illness was for me the highest good. It gave me that which neither my own reflections nor the reflections of other men could give me, and that which it gave me I shall now never lose, I shall take it with me. But, illness aside as well, in calling to mind my life, I see clearly that much that caused me torment was for me a true good, because it removed me from the pursuit of material good and directed me toward the acquisition of the true good — the spiritual. Not for nothing does the wisdom of the people say, concerning sickness, fires, and all that does not depend upon the human will: 'The Lord has visited.'
>
> There is nothing worse, in the sense of the acquisition of the true good, than that which men wish for themselves and for others, namely: health, wealth, fame. God grant that you may feel all the beneficence of suffering and of the approach of inevitable material death. True, for this it is necessary to believe in one's own spiritual essence, in a particle of God, which is subject neither to change nor to diminution, still less to sufferings or to annihilation. But, judging by your letter, I have ground to think that you do believe in this, and if you do not yet believe, then you will nonetheless come to it.
>
> May the Lord help you, and above all He who is within yourself."

Interesting are his new thoughts on progress and civilization, recorded by him in the diary on 14 April of this year:

> "Usually the progress of mankind is measured by its technical, scientific successes, on the supposition that civilization leads to good. This is not true. Both Rousseau and all who admire the wild, patriarchal state are just as right, or just as wrong, as those who admire civilization. The good of men who enjoy the very highest, most refined civilization and culture, and of the most primitive, savage men, is exactly the same. To increase the good of men by science, civilization, culture is just as impossible as it is to make the water on a watery plane stand higher in one place than in others. The increase of the good of men comes only from the increase of love, which by its very nature makes all men equal; whereas scientific, technical successes are a matter of age, and civilized men surpass the uncivilized in their well-being just as little as a grown man surpasses a child in his well-being. The good comes only from the increase of love."

Life often drew the attention of L. N. to the sad facts of the madness and cruelty of the coarse mob and of its no less coarse leaders, the representatives of the ruling class.

Such an event at that time was the calamity caused by the Kishinev pogrom.

It was hard to struggle against this, hard to heal the wound that had been inflicted, but nonetheless L. N. did everything to express his abhorrence of it and his pity both for the victims and for the tormentors in their delusion.

There was sent to him by Professor Storozhenko an address of protest for his signature. L. N. answered thus:

27 April 1903.

> "Dear Nikolai Ilyich. I am very glad to put my signature to the telegram. The only thing I do not like is the expression 'of burning shame for Christian society.' Could not these words be struck out, or the whole telegram be altered thus: 'Deeply shaken by the villainy committed at Kishinev, we express our grievous compassion for the innocent victims of the mob's bestiality, our horror before this bestiality of Russian people, our inexpressible loathing and abhorrence for those who prepared and incited the mob, and our boundless indignation against the connivers at this dreadful deed.' In any case, if only the expression about shame be left out, I am glad to sign, and I thank you for your appeal to me.
>
> To Petersburg I am not going. My health is good. I should wish that you too be not ill. And, judging by your letter, I hope that this is so. In friendly fashion I press your hand."

From the American United States he received from the newspaper "North American Newspaper" a telegram of the following content: "Is Russia to blame for the Kishinev slaughter? An answer of 30 words is paid for." L. N. answers thus:

> "The government is to blame: first, for the exclusion of the Jews, as a separate caste, from the general rights; secondly, for the violent inculcation upon the Russian people of idolatry in place of Christianity."

To an invitation to take part in the compiling of a literary miscellany for the benefit of the Jews who had suffered, L. N. answered with the following letter:

> "The dreadful villainy committed at Kishinev has painfully stricken me. I have expressed in part my attitude toward this matter in a letter to a Jewish acquaintance, a copy of which I enclose.
>
> The other day we sent from Moscow a collective letter to the mayor of Kishinev, expressing our feelings on the occasion of this dreadful affair.
>
> I shall be very glad to lend my aid to your miscellany, and I shall try to write something suited to the circumstances.
>
> Unfortunately, that which I have to say, namely, that the author not only of the Kishinev horrors, but of all that discord which is being sown in a certain small part — and not the popular part — of the Russian population, is the government alone — this, unfortunately, I cannot say in a Russian printed publication."

We give also the letter mentioned above, in answer to the appeal of a Jewish acquaintance who had asked him to declare himself in print on the occasion of this event.

> "I have received your letter. Of such letters I have already received several. All who write, just as you do, require of me that I express my opinion about the Kishinev event. It seems to me that in these appeals to me there is some misunderstanding. It is supposed that my voice carries weight, and therefore I am required to express my opinion about so important and, in its causes, so complex an event as the villainy committed at Kishinev.
>
> The misunderstanding consists in this, that there is required of me the activity of a publicist, whereas I am a man wholly occupied with one very definite question, having nothing in common with the appraisal of contemporary events: namely, the religious question and its application to life. To require of me a public expression of opinion about contemporary events is just as groundless as to require this of any specialist whatever who enjoys a certain reputation. I can — and I have done so — make use of some contemporary event for the illustration of a thought I am setting forth, but to respond to all contemporary events, however important they may be, as the publicists do, I can in no wise do, even were I to consider it necessary. Were I to act thus, I should have to express opinions unconsidered or trite, repeating what has already been said by others, and then — evidently — there would not exist that significance of my opinion on the ground of which its expression is required of me.
>
> As for my attitude toward the Jews and toward the dreadful Kishinev event, it ought, it would seem, to be clear to those who have interested themselves in my world-view. My attitude toward the Jews cannot be other than an attitude toward brothers, whom I love, not because they are Jews, but because we and they, like all men, are sons of one Father, God, and this love demands no effort of me, since I have met and know very good men who are Jews.
>
> My attitude toward the Kishinev crime, too, is of itself determined by my religious world-view. Even without knowing all the dreadful details which became known later, I understood from the first newspaper report the whole horror of what had taken place, and experienced a heavy, mingled feeling of pity for the innocent victims of the mob's bestiality, of bewilderment before this work of these men, supposedly Christians, a feeling of loathing and abhorrence for those so-called educated men who incited the mob and sympathized with its deeds, and, above all, of horror before the real author of it all, our government, with its clergy that stupefies and fanaticizes men, and with its robber band of officials. The Kishinev villainy is only a direct consequence of the preaching of falsehood and violence which is carried on with such strain and obstinacy by the Russian government.
>
> The attitude of the government toward this event, too, is only a new proof of its coarse egoism, which stops at no cruelties when the matter concerns the suppression of a movement that seems to it dangerous, and of its complete indifference — like the indifference of the Turkish government toward the Armenian massacres — to the most dreadful cruelties, if only they do not touch its interests.
>
> This is all that I could say on the occasion of the Kishinev affair, but all this I have long ago said.
>
> But if you ask me what, in my opinion, the Jews ought to do, then my answer too flows of itself from that Christian teaching which I try to understand and which I try to follow. The Jews, like all men, need one thing for their good: to follow, as much as possible in life, the universal rule — to act toward others as you wish that they should act toward you — and to struggle with the government not by violence (this means must be left to the government), but by a good life, excluding not only all violence toward one's neighbour, but also any participation in violence and any use, for one's own advantage, of the instruments of violence established by the government.
>
> This is all, very old and well known, that I have to say on the occasion of the dreadful Kishinev event."

Yasnaya Polyana, 27 April. 1903.

To his daughter Marya Lvovna he wrote at this time:

> "…The Jews are positively besieging me — some 20 letters — demanding that I declare myself on the Kishinev horrors. I have written an answer about this to Shor, who wrote to me — perhaps he will print it — and besides I have signed the collective letter to the mayor of Kishinev composed by Storozhenko. It is awkward for me to declare myself, because the cause of it all is our government, which torments the Jews and stupefies the Russians.
>
> Besides the definition of life, which I should like to express well and of which you know, I have a terrible longing to show clearly the whole deception and horror of governments of every kind, even your Swiss one.
>
> …Yesterday, while out walking, I met a youth who had left the technical college, the son of wealthy parents, and was on his way to me, and then wherever God might lead, in order to go down to the people and to work. Although one knows how it is — either quite impracticable, or terribly hard — one cannot see this without being moved. He happened to meet Bulygin at my house, and today went off to him."

On 18 June he makes an interesting entry about his literary projects.

> "I have conceived three new things:
>
> 1) The cry of the present-day men gone astray — the materialists, the positivists, the Nietzscheans — the cry (Mark 1:24): 'Let us alone; what have we to do with thee, thou Jesus of Nazareth? Art thou come to destroy us? I know thee who thou art, the Holy One of God.' (Very good it would be.)
>
> 2) For the Jewish miscellany: A merry ball at Kazan; I am in love with a beauty, the daughter of a Polish military commander, I dance with her; her handsome old father takes her tenderly and goes off to dance the mazurka. And in the morning, after a love-struck, sleepless night, the sounds of the drum, and a Tatar is being driven through the ranks, and the military commander orders him to be beaten more painfully. (Very good it would be.)
>
> 3) To describe myself in all truth, as I now am, with all my weaknesses and follies, interspersed with what is important and good in my life. (That too would be good.)
>
> All this is far more important than the foolish *Hadji Murad*."

Unfortunately, of these three projects only the second was carried out — *After the Ball*.

At the same time he already feels himself no longer able to struggle with the surroundings about him, and submissively gives himself up to the cares of his family for his welfare; conscious of this compromise, he repents of it in letters to his friends; thus he wrote at this time, among other things, to me:

> "I have wholly submitted to the temptations of fate, and live in the luxury that surrounds me, and in the physical idleness behind which I do not cease to feel the reproaches of conscience. I console myself with the fact that I live very amicably with all my family and others not of the family, and write something or other that seems to me important. There is very much of such. Farewell, dear friend, I kiss you, your good wife, and your capricious children. L. T."

2 September 1903.

Let us cite one more interesting letter of L. N.'s to a naive correspondent who had wished to convince him that one need not believe in God:

> "You do not understand what the word God means, and the mention of this word always irritates you. From this you conclude that 'it is time for mankind to cease speaking of God, whom no one understands.'
>
> That you are irritated by the use of a word whose meaning you do not understand is very natural. It is always so. But your conclusion, that no one understands what God is, because you do not understand it, is at the least strange. The fact that all mankind has always used this word, having need of this concept, ought to have led you to the thought that to blame is not mankind, but you yourself, in that you do not understand what all mankind, or the vast majority, understands, and that therefore you ought not to advise mankind to cease speaking of God, but to try yourself to understand that which you do not understand. Every man, just as you do, cannot but be conscious of himself as a part of something infinite. It is just this infinite, of which a man is conscious of being a part, that is God. For unenlightened people, to whom belongs the vast majority of the so-called learned, who understand nothing but matter, God will be infinite matter in space and time. And such a representation of God will be very absurd, but still they will have their own God, absurd though he be, still a God. But for enlightened people, who understand that the beginning and essence of life is not in matter but in spirit, God will be that infinite, unbounded being which a man is conscious of in himself within limits bounded by time and space. And such a God mankind has been conscious of and has acknowledged, and acknowledges, and will always acknowledge, if only it does not turn into a brute condition."

On 28 August 1903 Lev Nikolaevich completed his 75th year. This round number is reckoned a jubilee. It did not pass, of course, unnoticed in the life of L. N. either, although he himself attached no significance to it.

The "Russkie Vedomosti" report that L. N. Tolstoy met the day of his seventy-fifth birthday as vigorous, as full of spiritual animation and physical strength, as he had been all the latter time. This day was passed at Yasnaya Polyana in the close circle of L. N.'s children who had gathered with their families, and of a few near friends and acquaintances. In the morning Lev Nikolaevich was greeted by the representatives of the editorial office of "Posrednik" (The Intermediary), who presented L. N. with a copy of the miscellany "Thoughts of Wise Men," compiled by Lev Nikolaevich and issued in honour of his 75th birthday. At the same time there was handed to him a deeply heartfelt address, signed by numerous Moscow admirers, and an address from the men of letters, representatives of educational societies, *zemstvo* and other public men, who had gathered at Yaroslavl during the exhibition of the Northern Region. This address is of the following content:

> "We send ardent wishes of long life to him who, piercing the darkness with the light of his genius, points out the path to the attainment of truth and good."

Having conversed a little with all the guests, L. N. went off, as was his wont, to his study to work. In the third hour, having finished his occupations, he went out riding, as he did constantly all summer. Then, having returned, he passed the whole time of dinner and the evening in lively conversation with all who had gathered at Yasnaya Polyana. The whole day and the whole evening there came telegrams of greeting from various places, beginning with Petersburg and Moscow and ending with Manchuria, from persons of every possible class and condition. A heartfelt telegram was sent by the St. Petersburg Imperial Public Library. The writers who had come out of the peasant milieu presented the following address:

> "Dear and deeply respected Lev Nikolaevich! Permit us too, who have come out of the simple people and, by the path of literacy and of love for literature, of which you are the pride and the ornament, have attained to the understanding that men live not by material bread alone, to join all the others who greet you on the occasion of your 75th birthday, and to express to you, who all your life have burned with the fire of the spiritual life, the flame of which has warmed and will always warm millions of people, our ardent wish for many years of life, of vigour, and of fruitful activity."

Interesting too is the telegram from Kharkov:

> "To the preacher of the holiness of labour, the persecutor of prejudices, of slavery, and of the injustices of human relations, the workers of the city of Kharkov send greeting."

There were many sympathetic articles, especially in the provincial newspapers; thus, let us note the "Kievskaya Gazeta" (Kiev Gazette), where there were placed two interesting articles, one by Voloshin, on the occasion of "The Radiant Life," and an article by V. Lvov, "The Great Old Man."

L. N. himself, it appears, was burdened by this celebration, and notes it thus in his diary:

> "The 28th passed heavily. The congratulations are simply burdensome and disagreeable — insincere: of the Russian land and all manner of foolishness. The tickling of vanity, thank God, none whatever. Perhaps there is nothing to tickle. It is time."

The day after the completion of his 75th year a small misfortune befell L. N. Having set out on 29 August on his usual ride on horseback, he was obliged to dismount and to lead the horse by the bridle in crossing a certain ditch, and in doing so the horse somehow stepped on his foot. At first L. N. felt no particular pain, but then the foot began to swell, and he was put to bed for several days.

At the end of September he wrote, among other things, to V. G. Chertkov:

> "You know that I always have thoughts dear to me — because needful for the life of men — by which I guide myself, by which I am sustained, as by a spiritual medicine. And it always so happens that, as with a material medicine, the organism grows accustomed to the medicine and it no longer acts. But of late, for more than a month, I have a medicine that helps wonderfully and acts to this day. This medicine is meant against the very thing from which you suffer — separation from God. With me it expresses itself — I meant to say, is prepared — thus: I remind myself, as often as I can, that within me is the divine beginning — there is God — such a being as can neither be grieved, nor angered, nor ashamed, nor proud, but can only do what God does — 'what the Father does, that the son does also' — only do good to people, love. And so, often reminding myself of this in quiet moments, when one is alone and can pray, one trains oneself to recall this, to be conscious of God within oneself, in the hard moments of life, when you are grieved, offended, irritated, frightened. And one need only recall who you are, and such a peace establishes itself — if not always love toward him who has offended or grieved you, then at any rate surely the absence of irritation, of unkindness. One even experiences a sort of peculiar, almost physical feeling of joy and tranquillity. Upon me this acts wonderfully, but then we all go by separate paths. Try it; perhaps it will suit you too. Upon me not one of my spiritual medicines has acted so beneficially and so lastingly."

Wonderfully varied was his correspondence. After this letter, full of inner, moral significance, he writes to Grinevskaya, the author of a book about the Babis, and expresses interesting thoughts about this teaching.

> "Dear Madame Isabella Arkadyevna, I am very glad that V. V. Stasov conveyed to you the good impression which your book made upon me, for the sending of which I offer you my thanks. Of the Babis I have long known and have long been interested in their teaching. It seems to me that this teaching, like all the rationalistic social, religious teachings that have arisen of late out of the primitive teachings disfigured by the priests — Brahmanism, Buddhism, Judaism, Christianity, Mohammedanism — has a great future, precisely because all these teachings, having cast off all those deformed accretions that divide them, strive to merge into one common religion of all mankind. Therefore the teaching of the Babis also, in the measure in which it has cast off the old Mohammedan superstitions and has not set up new superstitions separating it from others (unhappily, something of the kind is noticeable in the exposition of the teaching of the Bab), and holds to its chief fundamentals — brotherhood, equality, and love — has a great future. In Mohammedanism there is taking place of late an intensified spiritual movement. I know that one such has its centre in the French possessions in Africa and has its name (I have forgotten it) and its prophet. Another is in India, in Lahore, and has likewise its prophet and publishes its journal. Both these religious teachings contain nothing new, and at the same time do not set their chief aim in the changing of men's world-views, and consequently of the relations of men among themselves, that which I see in Babism, not so much in its theory (in the teaching of the Bab) as in the practice of life, so far as I know it. And therefore with my whole soul I sympathize with Babism, in the measure in which it teaches men brotherhood, equality, and the sacrifice of the carnal life for the service of God."

He follows European literature too, and gives it an appraisal of his own. Thus, in a private letter of that time he characterizes the German writer Polenz, just deceased and, in L. N.'s opinion, little appreciated:

> "I was very grieved by the news of the death of Polenz. He was a great writer, who united in himself in equal degree all three qualities needful for a writer: always important content, excellent technique, and great sincerity, that is, love for what he described. These qualities showed themselves in the three novels of his which I have read: a peasant novel, a landowner novel, and a religious novel — *Der Pfarrer von Breitenhof*. This last novel is excellent both in form and in the weightiness of its content.
>
> It is a great pity that the public did not appreciate this remarkable writer at his worth. But if he is not appreciated by his contemporaries, then future generations will appreciate him. He is one of those writers who, like Dickens, Hugo, will outlive several generations and will be appreciated not by their compatriots alone."

To the Russian translation of Polenz's novel "The Peasant" L. N. wrote an interesting preface, in which he underlines the significance of this novel and points out the merits of the author himself.

Let us return again to L. N.'s diary and cite several interesting and weighty thoughts from various spheres of life. On 13 November he records:

> "It is usually thought that progress lies in the increase of knowledge, in the perfecting of life: but this is not so. Progress lies only in the greater and greater elucidation of the answers to the fundamental questions of life. Truth is always accessible to man. It cannot be otherwise, because the soul of man is a divine spark, truth itself, and the only thing is to take off from this spark of God (this truth) all that obscures it. Progress lies not in the increase of truth, but in its liberation from its coverings. Truth is acquired, like gold, not by its being added to, but by everything that is not gold being washed away from it.
>
> I know of only one sinless and greatest good in the world: this is the love of people, when you are loved. But this good cannot be got by seeking it, by seeking the love of people. The only means of getting it is the fulfilment of the law of life, of the will of God, of perfecting. This greatest good is that 'rest' which shall be added unto you if you truly seek the kingdom of God.
>
> We know in ourselves two lives: the spiritual life, apprehended by us through inner consciousness, and the bodily life, apprehended by us through external observation.
>
> Usually men (to whom I belong) who acknowledge as the fundamental life the spiritual life deny the reality, the need, the importance of the study of the bodily life, which evidently cannot lead to any final results. And in just the same way men who acknowledge only the bodily life deny altogether the spiritual life and all conclusions founded upon it, deny, as they say, metaphysics. But to me it is now quite clear that both are wrong, and that both kinds of knowledge, the materialistic and the metaphysical, have their great significance, only one must not wish to draw inappropriate conclusions from the one or the other kind of knowledge. From materialistic knowledge, founded upon the observation of external phenomena, one can deduce scientific data, that is, generalizations of phenomena, but one cannot deduce any guidance for the life of men, as the materialists — the Darwinists, for example — have often tried to do. From metaphysical knowledge, founded upon inner consciousness, one can and must deduce the laws of human life: how, and to what end, to live? — that very thing which all religious teachings do; but one cannot deduce, as many have tried to do, the laws of phenomena and their generalizations. Each of these two kinds of knowledge has its own appointment and its own field of activity."

By a peculiar tenderness and intimacy are always distinguished L. N.'s letters to his sick friend G. A. Rusanov. We borrow from one such letter of that time a new definition of life.

> "My chief, fundamental thought is that life is only in consciousness. Without consciousness we have no right to speak of life.
>
> For the understanding of life it is inevitably necessary to choose one of two things: either to acknowledge as life one's temporal existence (of which we learn only at the awakening of consciousness), which has no limits in the past, so that existence in the mother's womb, in the seed of the father, of the grandfather, in the material particles composing our body and the bodies of our forefathers, and in their interrelations, and in the sun and its beginning — that is, to acknowledge as life the greatest absurdity, and consciousness as only one of the manifestations of this absurdity; or to acknowledge that which at first seems strange, but which is quite clear, exact, and reasonable — that our life is our consciousness of ourselves as an eternal, infinite, that is, timeless and spaceless spirit, bounded by the conditions of temporal and spatial phenomena.
>
> The more a man comes into contact with the true life, the more life he has. In the striving toward the greatest contact lies the task of perfecting. The best life is that when it merges with the eternal life, and death is annihilated. In this striving lies the essence of man's life. To what end is this? I do not know. He knows, who is the lord of life, who is life itself.
>
> Forgive me for all this nonsense. All this, in such an embryonic and deformed shape, I permit myself to write only to you."

In conclusion to this chapter let us cite several remarkable letters of Lev Nikolaevich's of the end of 1903 on several extremely important questions.

1) The question of a general strike, from a letter to I. M. Tregubov:

> "A general strike, in order to attain those results for which it is organized, must have at its base a unity of convictions among all the people taking part in it. Unity of convictions is that highest visible aim to which they aspire. This unity is only in truth, in that truth which can at a given time be accessible to all men. Such a truth is only religious truth. Where, then, do you now see the possibility of such a uniting? Men not only do not come together in their religious superstitions, but the majority of men, especially those who present themselves as the foremost participants in a strike, have no religious convictions whatever, deny the very necessity of religion, deny that beginning upon which alone they could come together, and therefore are in even greater discord, as we see among all the political workers. To gather a general strike out of men such as they now are is impossible, just as it is impossible to bake bread out of unkneaded flour. However much you try, there will be no bread. The dough must be kneaded. It is for this reason that I think that, since a certain store of powers is given to each of us, it is reasonable to employ these powers upon an activity that can have results. If there are men either very short-sighted, or very carried away by the struggle, who do not see, do not wish to see, the consequences, in case of failure, of the struggle they are waging, then it is understandable that they may welcome and desire any activity that will contribute to the attainment of the nearest aim set before them, independently of what may and must come about after the attainment of this nearest aim. But men who see that a real improvement in the life of men can come about only from the improvement of the men themselves, from their uniting in the name of truth, not only cannot sympathize with the arranging of general strikes or revolutionary attempts, but cannot but see the vanity of such occupations. The business of the life of each of us is to employ the powers given us in the service of God and, by His will, of all men. And therefore a man who sees clearly that, however much he may sift the flour, making of it little heaps that look like loaves, there will be no bread, cannot but cease to occupy himself with this business, and will involuntarily try to employ his powers upon at least kneading the dough a little."

In another letter to I. M. Tregubov L. N. summed up his thought about the strike thus:

> "…A general strike cannot succeed, because men are not ready for it. But when men are ready for it, that against which strikes are needed will no longer exist."

I. M. objected to L. N., wishing to convince him of the utility of a general strike.

L. N. again answered him, on 1 June 1904, with these words:

> "About the strike I have nothing new to say, except that there is no reason not to sympathize with a strike, if it does not violate the foundations of the Christian faith, just as one cannot but sympathize with any means that contribute to the liberation of men."

An old friend of L. N.'s, Mitrofan Semyonovich Dudchenko, who lives on the land and persistently applies moral principles to life by the labour of his own hands, having heard that L. N. had ceased to work, writes to him, asking him whether his views on this subject have changed. Besides this, he is interested in the question of the religious education of his children. And so L. N. answers both these questions:

> "I was very glad to receive your letter, dear Mitrofan Semyonovich. I have long been thinking about you and about those very subjects of which you write — the most important subjects in the world. I not only have not changed my view as to the necessity of oneself satisfying one's very first needs, but more keenly than ever I feel the importance of this and my own sin in not fulfilling it. There were many causes that drew me away from this fulfilment, but I will not enumerate them, because the chief cause is only my own weakness, my sin. And therefore the receipt of your letter was for me a spiritual joy: a reproof and a reminder. One thing consoles me — that, living badly, I do not deceive myself, do not justify myself, that I can free myself from this labour because I write books, while always being conscious of that which you say, that 'just as I need to read a good book, so does he who will work in my stead,' and that in just the same way, if I can write a good book, there are hundreds and thousands of men who would write better books, were they not crushed and beaten down by labour. So that I not only agree with you, but more strongly than ever feel my sin, and, suffering from it, acknowledge the first-rate importance of the denial of the right to use for oneself the forced labours of another man. Thinking and hearing of you, from Yevgeny Ivanovich (*) and being conscious of the whole burden of your situation, I have at the same time not ceased to envy you.
>
> (* Yevgeny Ivanovich Popov, a friend of Lev Nikolaevich's. *)
>
> Do not lose heart, dear friend. 'He that endureth to the end shall be saved' — this applies precisely to your situation. I think that no bustle can hinder right thinking (which I see by your letter). What hinders right thinking is only idleness and luxury, and I feel this often in myself. However strange and unkind it may seem that I, living in luxury, permit myself to advise you to continue to live in want, I boldly say this, because I cannot for a moment doubt that your life is a good life before your own conscience and before God, and therefore the most needful and useful to people, while my activity, however useful it may seem to certain people, loses — I want to think, not all, but surely the greatest part of — its significance, owing to the non-fulfilment of the chief sign of the sincerity of that which I profess. The other day there was with me an intelligent and religious American, Bryan, and he asked me why I acknowledge simple manual labour to be necessary. I told him almost the same thing that you write: that, in the first place, it is a sign of the sincerity of the acknowledgment of the equality of men; in the second place, it draws us nearer to the majority of working people, from whom we are fenced off by a wall, if we make use of their need; in the third place, it gives us the highest good of a peace of conscience, which there is not and cannot be in a sincere man who makes use of the services of slaves. So this is my answer to the first point of your letter. Now as to the second, the most difficult — religious education. In education in general, both physical and intellectual, I hold that the chief thing is not to force anything upon children, but, biding one's time, to answer the demands that arise in them; the more so is this needed in the chief subject of education, the religious one. Just as it is useless and harmful to feed a child when it does not want to eat, or to force upon it knowledge of subjects which do not interest it and which it does not need, so still more harmful is it to instil into children any religious notions about which the child does not ask, and, mostly by formulating them coarsely, to disturb thereby that religious attitude toward life which at this time is perhaps unconsciously arising and establishing itself in the child. It is necessary, it seems to me, only to answer, but to answer with complete truthfulness, the questions put by the child. It seems very simple — to answer truthfully the religious questions of a child. But in reality this can be done only by him who has himself already answered truthfully for himself the religious questions about God, life, death, good and evil — those very questions which children always put very clearly and definitely. However strange it may seem, the education of oneself is the most powerful instrument of the influence of parents upon children. And that first paragraph which your future neighbours have made their own — 'perfect thyself' — is the highest and, however strange it may seem, the most practical, in the sense of serving other people, of influencing other people, activity of a man.
>
> So it is in education too. The external conditions of your austere life, which you surely do not value at its worth, are the most advantageous for education. Your life is a serious one, and the children see it and understand this.
>
> And here is just where there is confirmed that which I always think about education and which you say in your letter — that the essence of the education of children consists in the education of oneself. But if you want from me more definite directions as to what precisely to read or to give into the hands of a child for religious education, then I think that one must not confine oneself to the religious writings of one faith, with us the Christian, but, on a level with the Christian instructive literature, make use also of the Buddhist, the Brahmin, the Confucian, the Jewish.
>
> Very, very glad was I of the communion with you. I should wish that it might be the hundredth part as useful to you as it is useful to me, and therefore I should wish that it might be more often repeated."

Finally, let us end with a fine little page from the diary, written at the end of this year:

> "Men have never lived without religion. We, a little particle of men, the one that takes upon itself to teach the majority, live without religion and think that it is not even needed. From this come all the calamities of men. And yet, it would seem, it is clear that without religion one cannot live. One cannot live, because:
>
> 1) only religion gives the definition of the good and the bad, and therefore a man only on the basis of religion can make a choice out of all that he may wish to do, in those moments when his passions are silent;
>
> 2) without religion a man can never know whether what he does is good or bad;
>
> 3) only religion destroys egoism; only in consequence of religious demands can a man live not for himself;
>
> 4) only religion destroys the fear of death; not that a man can go to the danger of death or even deprive himself of life, but that he can calmly await death;
>
> 5) only religion gives a man the meaning of life;
>
> 6) only religion establishes the equality of men;
>
> 7) only religion fully frees a man from all external constraints."
