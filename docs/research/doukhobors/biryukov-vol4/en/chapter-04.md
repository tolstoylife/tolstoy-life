# Volume IV, Chapter 4. 1901 (continued). A Plan for a Free School. Illness

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-04.md`](../ru/chapter-04.md).

L. N. still spent April 1901 in Moscow, now ailing, now recovering, but on the whole his health was moving toward improvement, which allowed him to work. At this time, while I was living in Switzerland, I received from him a most important letter that greatly stirred me, written at the very end of March. I give it in full:

> "I wrote you a letter not long ago, dear friend Posha, and this very letter called forth in me a whole series of thoughts which I want to communicate to you.
>
> It comes to me ever more and more insistently that the work of educating children in a free country, which has chanced to take shape around you, is a matter of enormous importance, the most important matter in life. I wrote this introduction about a month ago, and since then I have not gone on — partly because of ill health (weakness, a feverish condition, pains in the muscles and, as always, the stomach and liver; now it is better), partly because of the bustle caused by our social events, of which let others write to you. But I want to continue what I have begun, and what greatly occupies me — the thought that the ordering of society, of the relations of men among themselves, even if only a little less brutal than now, and even if only a little nearer to that Christian — not even ideal, but quite realizable conception which has formed and grown firm in us, that such an ordering of the whole of society is unattainable not only to our generation, mine, but to yours as well, but that it must in part or in full be attained by the next generation, by the children who are growing up now. But in order for this to be, we, our generation, must work to deliver the next generation from those deceptions, those hypnotizations, out of which we extricated ourselves with such difficulty, and not only to deliver them, but to give them all the help we can to go along the one true path — not some special path of our own, but the path of freedom and reason, which inevitably leads all men to the uniting truth. But in order for this to be, there must be such schools. In order for there to be such schools, there must be models, attempts at models. And here you and Pasha (*) are placed by fate itself, and by the conditions of your characters, in such a position that I do not know who can do this better than you, who as it were have been bidden to do it, to devote to it all the powers of your life. You are Russians, with that ideal striving toward full, radical freedom, bound by nothing, and with a clear, definite world-view that flows from this freedom. And you live in a free country, where nothing will hinder you from applying your principles to life. And you are both capable, healthy, effortlessly moral people. And you are loved. And around you a little cluster of children has gathered. The other day they were speaking of this, and two men, Bulygin and Mikhailov, were present, and both desire nothing so much as to find a place where they can save their children from deception. And there are hundreds, if not thousands, of such parents and children. Besides this, though it is immodest, you have a man who has long loved the work of education and now attaches the greatest significance to it and is ready to give it his last powers, a man who can be useful to you — and that is I. Only appreciate the whole importance of this matter, surpassing all others, and give yourselves to it, and I, as far as I can, will lay all my powers upon it.
>
> (* My wife, Pavla Nikolaevna, née Sharapova. (P. B.) *)
>
> For in this matter everything must be begun from the beginning: 1) religious education, or rather not education, but the guarding from false education; 2) the education of a way of life — the abolition of the perverted habits of being waited upon; 3) the subjects of instruction — the methods, no compulsion; 4) the artistic: a) drawing as a means of conveying knowledge and thoughts, b) music, not instruments, but above all the use of one's own voice.
>
> 5) Labour — a system of labour.
>
> 6) Hygiene.
>
> For all this must be begun from the beginning. And work without end. If only something could be done.
>
> Write what you think of this. I kiss you all three, brotherly, and the children. L. T."

21 March 1901.

We, of course, hastened to answer that we were ready to serve the cause of establishing a free school, if only our powers should suffice, and to this we received a new letter, begun while still in Moscow, before his departure, and finished a month later, after his arrival at Yasnaya; this interruption in the writing was again caused by illness.

This letter is very long and is already known to the public from the complete collected works; we convey its main thoughts in summary:

As the fundamental principle of his new programme of a free school, L. N., in his letter to me, lays down the thought that education and instruction are accomplished by suggestion. There is conscious suggestion; it is accomplished in the teaching of any subjects. The other suggestion is unconscious; it exerts a powerful influence on education in the form of the example of the educators' lives. If this influence is good, it may be called enlightenment. L. N. says that the bourgeois setting of our middle class hinders such enlightenment. He expresses this in these words:

> "Some family of a rentier, of a husbandman, of a clerk, even of an artist or a writer, lives a bourgeois life — lives without drunkenness, without debauchery, without quarrelling, without offending people, and wishes to give its children a moral education; but this is just as impossible as it is impossible to teach children a new language without speaking that language and without showing them books written in that language. The children will listen to the rules about morality, about respect for people, but unconsciously they will not only imitate, but will assimilate as a rule the fact that some people are called to clean boots and clothes, to carry water and refuse, to prepare food, while others are to soil their clothes and rooms, to eat the food, and so forth. If only one seriously understands the religious foundation of life — the brotherhood of men — then one cannot but see that people who live on money taken from others, and who compel these others to serve them for this money, live an immoral life, and no sermons of theirs will deliver their children from the unconscious, immoral suggestion which will either remain in them all their lives, perverting all their judgments about the phenomena of life, or, with great efforts and labour, after many sufferings and errors, will be destroyed by them."

Then L. N. passes on to the content of instruction, very closely bound up with education and accomplished by means of conscious suggestion. In former times this suggestion was turned into a hammering-in and even a beating-in by every means, even to the use of sticks and rods; at present we stand upon the principle of free instruction, which nonetheless is suggestion. As one of the chief conditions for the success of instruction L. N. considers the good will of the learners. He says that knowledge is only then successfully received and bears fruit if there is a thirst and hunger for knowledge, a need for it, just as food is only then beneficial when a man feels physical hunger.

And therefore one must never compel a child to learn, and still less punish him for failure or for so-called laziness and the shirking of lessons. The very subjects of instruction L. N. divides thus:

1) philosophical-religious subjects, on the meaning of life;

2) experimental subjects, natural science;

3) mathematical subjects.

Then follow 3 divisions of the arts:

a) verbal art (here belongs also the study of language);

b) plastic art;

c) music.

Finally, as a 7th division he considers the teaching of crafts (physical labour, agriculture, in general the production of the material objects necessary for life).

All these thoughts are sketched by him in the letter, in his own expression, as a programme of a programme. He does not insist on their completeness; he leaves freedom for their elaboration in detail in practice; but one cannot but agree with the boldness and fruitfulness of his framing of this question of a free working school.

Afterwards L. N. worked over this letter to us a little more, and in this form it was published first by *Svobodnoe Slovo* ("The Free Word") abroad, and then also entered the complete collection of his works under the title "Letter on Education to P. B."

Notwithstanding his weak health, L. N.'s activity was manifold. His diaries continue to gleam with wondrous thoughts. Thus, on 8 April he records:

> "There are religious people who treat with disdain, and even deny, men's care for their own bodies, and likewise all professional activity. This is not right. The best of men cannot always serve God. There are periods of indifference, of weariness. And then a man can and must perform his professional work — sew, plane, teach, and so forth, only so that this work be not repugnant to God. Nor can a man always serve people by professional work: there are periods of weariness, and then let him serve himself: eat, sleep, make merry. Only let this serving of himself not hinder, and not be repugnant to, the service of God. One must not despise these periods of weariness, of incapacity for the service of God, but organize them so that they should in no way hinder the service of God."

About this same time he records the following thought:

> "The happy periods of my life were only those when I gave my whole life to the service of people. These were: the schools, the arbitration, the famine-stricken, and religious help."

This thought is very interesting in that it convicts L. N. of the so-called "contradictions" which people who did not understand L. N. deeply enough were so fond of pointing out.

Indeed, there is nothing easier than to convict L. N. of contradiction on this score.

In his "Confession" L. N. says that it was very hard for him to "dodge" in his pedagogical activity, to war with the landowners during his "arbitration," and that he grew so weary, in body and in spirit, of all this falsehood, that he threw everything up and went off to take the *kumys* cure, to rest and to live an animal life.

Of his activity for the benefit of the famine-stricken he wrote to his friends that it was dreadful, full of sin; and in one letter he even expressed himself thus: "I am occupied with distributing 'the vomit of the rich.'"

And here, after many years, he writes that only these occupations were the happy time of his life.

And this is quite understandable. These activities are indeed hard, and there is much in them that is difficult to bear for natures as impressionable as Lev Nikolaevich. But he himself entered upon them under the influence of the most noble feelings and worked with self-abnegation. With the passing of time the hard impressions were smoothed away, and there remained the impression of happy days spent in labour for the common good.

In May and June L. N. continued to ail, which, however, did not prevent him from occupying himself with his usual affairs. One of his usual affairs was intercession for the persecuted. And so, on 6 May he writes to Svyatopolk-Mirsky a letter "on the easing of the lot of Maxim Gorky." This letter is very characteristic.

> "Your Excellency, the wife and friends of A. M. Peshkov (Gorky) have turned to me, asking me to intercede before whomever I can and may find it possible, that he, sick and consumptive, should not be killed before trial and without trial by being held in the Nizhny Novgorod prison, which is, as I am told, dreadful in its unhygienic conditions. I personally know and love Gorky, not only as a gifted writer valued even in Europe, but also as an intelligent, kind, and sympathetic man. Although I have not the pleasure of knowing you personally, it somehow seems to me that you will take part in the fate of Gorky and his family and will help them as far as it is in your power.
>
> Please, do not deceive my expectations, and accept the assurances of the perfect respect and devotion with which I have the honour to be your humble servant. Lev Tolstoy."

On 8 May L. N. and his family moved to Yasnaya. His diaries gleam with wisdom just as before. The frequent so-called "disorders" of that time lead him to the following thought:

> "When I sit upon another and ride upon him, that is order. But when this other wishes to free himself from under me, that is disorder."

Further on he recalls the historical course of his thoughts:

> "Seeking the cause of evil in the world, I kept going deeper and deeper. At first I pictured to myself the cause of evil as evil men, then as a bad social order, then as the participation in violence of those very men who suffer from it, the army, then as the absence of religion in these men, and, finally, I came to the conviction that the root of everything is religious education. And therefore, in order to set evil right, one must not change the men, not alter the order, not break up the violence, not dissuade men from participation in violence, and not even refute the false religion and expound the true one — but only educate the children in the true religion."

The general state of his health makes his wife express, in a letter to her sister T. A. Kuzminskaya, the following anxious thoughts:

> "...You ask about Lyovochka. It grieves me even to answer you on this, since I must write you the truth. This year he has suddenly grown quite old, has wasted away, has lost strength, is constantly ailing with something. Now his legs hurt, it is painful to rise from his seat; now his arms hurt, his fingers cramp; now his stomach will not digest. Sometimes he groans the whole night through: whether this be rheumatism, or a degeneration of the arteries, or poor circulation — it is hard to tell. They are now giving him hot salt baths, and he drinks the waters. He does not ride horseback, walks little, eats very carefully."

In June he went off again to stay with his daughter Tatyana Lvovna at Kochety, and, returning thence, felt a still greater weakness. His friend P. A. Boulanger relates the details of this journey thus. P. A. was employed on the railway and, when L. N. travelled, always tried to ease his way by providing some convenience or comfort, which was not always easy to do, since L. N. always stubbornly refused all privileges. So it was this time too:

> "L. N.'s journey," P. A. Boulanger related in his reminiscences, "from Kochety to the railway station was very hard and tormenting. In view of the fact that to travel in a carriage was very painful, L. N. preferred to go to the station on foot, setting out in good time. He refused to take a guide, not wishing to constrain others, and, having asked the way, he set off. But, having walked an hour and a half, he grew tired and, besides, wishing to take a direct line by which he would shorten his way by some 3 or 4 versts, he lost his road. Twilight was coming on. Lev Nikolaevich clambered from hill to hill, was losing his strength, saw that he was straying altogether from his original direction. Night fell, and not far from himself L. N. heard the barking of dogs; he made his way thither and found shepherds at an abandoned farmstead. Here he learned that he had deviated considerably from the road, that it was still some six versts to the station. Then he began to ask that a horse be got from somewhere — there was no horse. Would not someone undertake to guide him to the station, or at least to bring him out onto the road? No one agreed; they were afraid — in this locality there are many wolves, and no one wished to risk going out into that darkness. They pointed out the direction, and God speed.
>
> In the dark night, already weary, not knowing the road, but relying on his old hunting habits, Lev Nikolaevich set off again, climbing once more up and down the hills. At last his feet felt out a beaten road. He stopped to get his bearings in the darkness. It could be seen that he had come upon the crossing of several roads. Which way was he to go now? Knowing that the *zemstvo* in this locality set up at the crossings of roads posts with inscriptions of the directions, he felt out a post, but the inscription could not be read. By good luck there proved to be matches in his pocket, and, having struck a match, Lev Nikolaevich learned at last which way he had to go.
>
> Having walked a little along the road he had found, Lev Nikolaevich heard the rattle of a carriage along the road and began to wait, hoping that the one who was driving would give him a lift to the station. It proved that it was his own luggage that was being carried to the station, and, having sat down on the box, he 'safely' got to the station in half an hour. He was dreadfully exhausted. His stomach had begun to ache from the jolting; everything ached. Going into the lavatory at the station, he, moreover, somehow awkwardly leaned upon a door with a pulley-weight, his finger got into the crack of the door, and the door with its heavy weight slammed shut and crushed the finger. To all his weariness and former pains there was added the tormenting pain of the injured finger as well. The dressing was made only several stations later, at Orel.
>
> As I had supposed, Lev Nikolaevich did not travel in first class, and he was persuaded that, after all the difficulties he had borne on the way, he should travel at least in second class. But here too it was very uncomfortable to travel, as Igumnova, who was accompanying him, related. The carriage was full, the seat-backs for sleeping had already been raised, there remained a few places below, and Lev Nikolaevich somehow squeezed himself into one of these places at the feet of a lady lying on the divan, hunched in this hole with the divan's back raised above him. Of rest, of course, there could be no question. Moreover, the lady who lay there, elderly but trying to seem young, was flirting in the most vulgar fashion with the gentleman sitting opposite. It was very smoky, stuffy, and disgusting.
>
> 'But,' Igumnova related, 'we bore it patiently, having received your telegram at Orel and knowing that we had only some hour and a half more to endure.
>
> When Lev Nikolaevich entered the carriage that was waiting for him, I was struck by the change that had taken place in him. Evidently he was suffering greatly, but, as always, did not show it. With pleasure he undressed, the injured finger was washed and bandaged again, and at once he went and lay down in his compartment.
>
> Our carriage was uncoupled at Yasenki, and Lev Nikolaevich was able to pass the rest of the night quietly, though, as it turned out, he did not sleep. Early in the morning we conveyed him, ill, to Yasnaya Polyana."

Whether this particular incident told upon him, or whether illness was in general already creeping up on Lev Nikolaevich, the subsequent news of him from Yasnaya Polyana was of the most alarming kind.

And soon L. N. took to his bed.

Here is what S. A. wrote about this illness to her sister on 11 July:

> "...Lyovochka is very ill. He has come down with a fever; for two evenings he had a high temperature. And this had such a bad effect on his heart that it quite refuses to work. With quinine they stopped the fever for today; it is now 8 o'clock in the evening and there is no fever. But this morning his temperature was 35.9, and his pulse 150. This is considered a very bad sign. The doctor stays without leaving until tomorrow evening, and says that tomorrow will decide everything. If the heart quiets down, then Lyovochka may this time get up; but in any case he cannot live long. The children are gathering little by little...
>
> ...I do not believe, and cannot yet believe, that Lyovochka is in a bad way," Sofya Andreyevna continues. "How many times have I been frightened, and each time everything turned out well. But a sort of numbness has come over me; I go about as though stunned, everything in me has grown dull and stopped. Sometimes I sit or lie at night beside him, and I so long to tell him how dear he is to me and how I never loved anyone in the world as I loved him. That if ever outwardly — by some sort of delusion — I was guilty before him, yet inwardly there sat firmly within me toward him alone a serious, steadfast love, and never, by a single movement of a finger, was I unfaithful to him. But one can say nothing, one must not agitate him, and I must settle these accounts with myself alone, of 39 years of a married life that was in essence very happy and pure, but with the guilt that, after all, we did not make each other happy fully, completely, to the end.
>
> All this I write to you as to a friend, as to one of those who both loved and understood us, and I wanted simply to pour out my heavy heart to someone. If all goes well — there will be no word of it; but if it is the end — then the news will fly swiftly round the whole world."

Marya Lvovna, in a letter to me, describes in detail the course of this illness:

> "When papa woke, he called me to him and said that he had not slept all night from pains in his chest and side, and that he felt unwell. Papa nevertheless got up, dined with us, was in one of his wonderful moods — you know — that peculiar, heartfelt talkativeness. We spoke of many things, of the most interesting and important, and it was so good for us all to be together, quiet and joyful. In the evening papa came down with a fever. He slept well that night and in the morning got up quite fresh and said that he was completely well. But here in the morning, taking his temperature, I noticed that, while speaking with me, he was as it were gasping for breath. But I ascribed this to the fact that the subject of conversation could have agitated him. I felt his pulse and here saw that the pulse was very rapid and irregular. But papa was so fresh after a good night that he paid no attention to it and went downstairs to dress. After breakfast I went to the village to the sick; with me went Kolechka Ge, my husband, and a girl who lives with us. Going back, we met papa and followed him at a distance, so as not to disturb his solitude and yet to be near him. He went in the direction of the highway and, having reached the first little hill, suddenly stopped. We caught up with him, and he said that something very unpleasant had happened to him, his heart was beating, the sweat had broken out, and his pulse here was already making some extraordinary leaps and stops. We walked slowly with him, and at the corner he sat down to rest, and he felt very bad all the while. He reached the house with great difficulty and lay down. Toward dinner it again grew better, and he came out to us on the terrace to dine. Here the Tula priest arrived, who often comes to him, a very disagreeable man, cunning, it seems (it seems to me that he is something of a spy). Papa began to talk with him, grew agitated, and began to tell him that he does wrong in coming to him, that he has probably been sent, and so forth. This conversation was hard for him, and he again felt himself worse. The evening, nevertheless, he again passed with us. In the night mama returned; he was again unwell, had a fever; and early in the morning they sent for the doctor in Tula, then for the Kaluga one, and then for the Moscow one. Here began those three days of dying. The whole time the pulse was 150, such weakness that he had to be moved in our arms. We summoned them all: Seryozha (Ilya happened to be here), Tanya, Misha, sent word to Lyova in Sweden — all sat and simply waited for the end, and at this time he was so excited with thoughts that it even oppressed him — he kept asking us to write down separate thoughts about illness, about death, about space and time, about eternal life, and so forth. He said that he felt very well. He said that the horses had been brought round, to set off, and that the carriage was very comfortable, because the consciousness was clear. He was so kind, gentle, and touching with everyone. These three days they gave him caffeine, strophanthus, coffee, wine, quinine. Today is the first day he has taken no medicine, and for about 5 or 6 days he has had a normal pulse. The doctors consider that this is an attack of angina pectoris, brought on by the illness with which he was ill in winter and which he had here in spring as well. The return of such an attack may always lead to the end."

During the illness, feeling the approach of death, L. N. thus characterized his condition: "The carriage is at the door." Another time he said: "I have stopped at a crossroads. And it is equally pleasant to me, whithersoever they may take me: into the continuation of this life or to the beginning of a new one." He used a similar comparison in a letter to his brother Sergei Nikolaevich as well, to whom he wrote, among other things, having recovered from the very gravest attack of the illness:

> "When, as it is with you and me, one is so near to the crossing over the main pass, all relations in this world lose their importance, and important only are the relations with God, ever more and more being established. So it is at least with me, and it was especially strong during the illness. This I wish very strongly for you as well. Surely it is so indeed.
>
> During my whole illness it was very, very good with me; one thing troubled me, and troubles me — whether it would have been so had I not had such care, easing the illness, the pain. Had I been lying full of lice on the stove with cockroaches, amid the cries of children and peasant women, with no one to give me a drink. Now I am quite well, only weak. I walk, but do not go downstairs, and continue to write what seems to me needful.
>
> ...I now have a feeling as though I were at the last station on the way from the place to which I am travelling — not without pleasure, or at least, surely, without displeasure. There are no horses, and one must wait until the return horses arrive or are fed. And the station is not bad, and I try to pass the time with profit and pleasure. By the way, you rest, you tidy yourself up, and it will be merrier to travel the last stage."

Interesting is the entry of that time in the diary of S. A., in which is vividly expressed L. N.'s striving to maintain a harmonious, loving atmosphere in the family. And with what joy Sofya Andreyevna answered this:

> "Today he said to me: 'I am now at a parting of the ways: forward (to death) is well, and back to life is well. Even if it passes now, it is only a reprieve.'
>
> Then he grew thoughtful and added: 'There is still much, and I should like to say it to people.' When daughter Masha brought him today the last article of Lev Nikolaevich's that N. N. Ge had just copied out, he rejoiced over it as a mother would rejoice over a beloved child brought to her bedside in illness, and at once asked N. N. Ge to insert certain corrections, and asked me to gather together downstairs in his study all the rough drafts of this article, to tie them up and to inscribe them: 'rough drafts of the last article,' which I did.
>
> Yesterday morning I am tying onto his stomach a warming compress; he suddenly looked at me intently, wept, and said: 'Thank you, Sonya. Do not think that I am ungrateful to you and that I do not love you...'
>
> And his voice broke off from tears, and I kissed his dear hands, so familiar to me, and told him that it is happiness for me to nurse him, that I feel all my guilt before him, if I did not give him enough happiness, that he should forgive me for what I was not able to give him, and we both, in tears, embraced each other, and this was what my soul had long desired — it was a serious, deep acknowledgment of our close relations of all our 39 years of life together... All that disturbed them temporarily was some sort of outward delusion and never altered the firm inner bond of the very best love between us."

At last I received comforting news from S. A. She wrote to me, among other things, on 19 July: "The life dear to us all all but went out. But now, thank God, L. N. is recovering well and is working again. For the autumn the doctors are sending us to the Crimea, whither all three daughters will go with us. Warmth has a good effect on Lev Nikolaevich, and 4 doctors advised him to prolong the effect of warmth a little longer."

It is interesting how L. N. himself thought about his illness. Having recovered, he records in his diary:

> "16 July, Y. P., 1901. I have not written for more than a month. I was gravely ill from 27 June, though even before that, for two weeks, I had been unwell. The illness was one continuous spiritual festival, with heightened spirituality and tranquillity at the approach of death, and the expression of love on all sides... I finished *The Only Means*. Not especially good, weak."

The Synod, by its excommunication, considerably broadened L. N.'s popularity. His views penetrated even to the East, and here he receives a letter from the Hindu Ramazeshna, to whom he replies. L. N.'s letter is so characteristic that we shall give it here in full.

> "I thank you for your interesting letter. I am entirely in agreement that your nation cannot accept the solution of the social question which Europe offers it and which, in essence, is no solution. A society, or assembly of men, founded on violence, is not only in a primitive state, but in a very dangerous position. The bond uniting such a society can always be broken, and a great misfortune may befall the society. All the European states are precisely in such a position. The only solution of the social question for reasonable beings, endowed with the capacity to love, lies in the abolition of force and in the organization of a society founded on mutual respect and on reasonable principles voluntarily accepted by all. Such a state can be attained only by the development of true religion. By the words true religion I mean the fundamental principles of all religions, the essence of which is: 1) the consciousness of the divine essence of the human soul, and 2) respect for its manifestation.
>
> Your religion is very ancient and very profound in its metaphysical definition of man's relations to the spiritual. All — toward Atman; but I think that it is distorted in its moral, that is, its practical application to life, owing to the existence of caste. This practical application to life, so far as I know, was made by the Jains (Jainism), the Buddhists, and certain other sects, such as the Kabir Panthis, in which the fundamental rule is the holiness of life and, consequently, the prohibition against depriving any living being of life, especially man.
>
> All the evil which you experience — famine, and, still more important, the humiliation of your people by factory life — will continue so long as your people consent to go for soldiers (sepoys). Parasites feed only on unclean bodies. Your people must preserve moral purity, and in the degree to which it is pure from killing or from the readiness for it, in that degree it will be free from the régime under which it now suffers. I entirely agree with you that you ought to be grateful to the English for all that they have done for you, for your welfare, and that you should help them in all that leads to the civilization of your people; but you ought not to help the English in their rule by violence, and on no account to participate in an organization founded on violence. Therefore it seems to me that the duty of every educated Hindu consists in destroying all the old superstitions which conceal from the masses the principles of true religion, that is, the consciousness of the divine essence of the human soul and respect for the life of every living being without exception, and in spreading them as widely as possible.
>
> It seems to me that these principles are implied, if not actually contained, in your ancient and profound religion, and require only to be developed and to have removed from them that veil which conceals them. It seems to me that only such a course of action can free the Hindus from the misfortunes to which they are subjected, and can be the most effectual means for the attainment of that aim toward which you are striving."

And so, while from far-off lands there fly to Tolstoy expressions of sympathy, his own home-grown guardians strive by their zeal to outdo even the resolution of the "humble ones." It was with such a somewhat strange boldness that the 1st Moscow Temperance Society distinguished itself.

At the annual meeting of the society, after the usual reading of reports and the elections, there was read a declaration by one of the members, the tailor Vorsunyak, demanding nothing more nor less than the expulsion from the society of its honorary member, Count L. N. Tolstoy. Paragraph 4 of the society's charter runs: "Only persons of the Orthodox faith may be members of the society." But Count Tolstoy — it was pointed out in the declaration — has, in accordance with the resolution of the Holy Synod, been temporarily excommunicated from the church and therefore cannot be considered Orthodox. Vorsunyak was supported by 2 or 3 men, and among them the petty trader Zamyatin.

> "We do not know Tolstoy," he declared, "and we do not wish to know him! And why have the gentlemen intellectuals foisted him upon us at all?"

In this way the temperance society entered into a struggle not only with drunkenness, but with the intelligentsia as well. The chairman's conclusion was as follows:

> "Gentlemen," he said, "we are not a missionary brotherhood! It does not fall within the bounds of our peaceful work for the good of people to be obliged to criticize the religious convictions of a fellow member sincerely devoted to one and the same task as ourselves: the eradication of drunkenness. True, there have been cases when the higher administration or the police indicated to the society that it was necessary to expel some notoriously unreliable member. With regard to Count L. N. Tolstoy we have had no such indications. And in our very charter, with the exception of the disputed paragraph 4, there are no indications of any possibility of expelling a fellow member on account of any religious convictions of his."

Thus the chairman was on the side of the law, but some members took a different stand. After the chairman's speech the priest Father Lebedev rose and declared:

> "If Lev Tolstoy remains among the members of the present temperance society, I and all the other persons of the clerical estate withdraw from its membership, since we consider it impossible to remain in communion with a man condemned by the highest spiritual authority."

This resolute declaration gave the debate a sharp turn, and the result of it was the following resolution:

> "Having heard the declaration of the members of the 1st Moscow Temperance Society — the tailor Vorsunyak, the shopkeeper Zamyatin, the priest Lebedev, and others — concerning the expulsion of Count L. N. Tolstoy from among the honorary members of the society in view of the resolution passed by the Holy Synod on the temporary excommunication of the said L. Tolstoy from the Orthodox church, the general meeting, having taken into account paragraph 4 of its charter, came to the conclusion that Count L. N. Tolstoy, after the above-cited resolution of the Holy Synod, cannot fit into the membership of the society indicated in paragraph 4. In view of this, the further continuance of L. N. Tolstoy among the members of the society is undesirable. But there are likewise no indications in the charter regarding the procedure for expelling honorary members of the society from its membership. Therefore it is resolved: to submit the present matter to the consideration of His Imperial Highness, the August Governor-General of the city of Moscow, the Grand Duke Sergei Alexandrovich, with the appending of the written declaration of the tailor Vorsunyak and the most humble request: since the members of the society have, on the basis of the articles of the charter, no actual right to expel by their own authority the honorary member L. N. Tolstoy, to permit a petition to be made for the appropriate order for the expulsion of L. N. Tolstoy by the administrative authority." This resolution was signed in their own hands by all those present.

In the summer, in July, L. N. was visited by his French friends, Charles Salomon and Paul Boyer.

L. N.'s correspondence keeps growing.

The Romanian Queen Natalya, who, as is well known, bore the literary pseudonym of Carmen Sylva, sends him her works together with an enthusiastic letter. L. N. answers her thus:
To Carmen Sylva.

> "Madame, I am very grateful to you for the letter which you were so kind as to write me. The approval of people standing at the two opposite ends of the social ladder is especially precious to me, since such approval, more than anything else, allows me to believe that the Christian ideas of which I have tried to be the interpreter are true ideas, since they answer the needs of the human soul, notwithstanding the difference of the conditions in which it finds itself.
>
> I have not yet received the book which you are sending me. I thank you in advance for it. Knowing the lofty and humanitarian ideas of Carmen Sylva, I am sure that I shall read it with great interest.
>
> Repeating to you my gratitude, I beg you, Madame, to accept the assurance of my perfect esteem. Lev Tolstoy."

The excommunication not only drew to L. N. the sympathy of the whole enlightened world, but also troubled and stirred many of the best representatives of that same church milieu.

In August of this year L. N. received a sympathetic letter from the Orthodox priest Tikhon, expressing agreement with L. N. in his understanding of the teaching of Christ. Answering him, L. N. says that this is already the letter of a fourth priest, and rejoices that the spiritual light is penetrating into their milieu as well. At the same time L. N. points out to him that, if he agrees with him in his understanding of the teaching of Christ, then he cannot remain a priest. And then he indicates two ways out for a priest who has come to know the truth:

> "The best way out of this position, the heroic way, in my opinion, would be for the priest to gather his parishioners, to come out to them upon the ambo, and instead of the service and the bowings before the icons to bow down to the ground before the people, asking forgiveness of them for having led them into error. The second way out is the one chosen some 10 years ago by a remarkable man, now deceased, known to me, the priest Apollov, from the Vyatka seminary, who served in the Stavropol diocese. He declared to the bishop that, owing to his changed views, he could not continue to be a priest. He was summoned to Stavropol, and the authorities and his family so tormented him that he agreed to return to his place. But, having stayed less than a year, he could not endure it and again refused, and was unfrocked. His wife left him. All these sufferings so affected him that he died like a saint, not having betrayed his convictions and, above all, his love."

The chief thing for L. N. is sincerity, the acknowledgment of one's own weakness, one's sin, without justifying it by any contrivances of the mind whatsoever.

"A priest," he says further, "who understands the truly Christian teaching and remains a priest, acts badly, and this he ought to know and feel and suffer from. But how he acts is his affair with God, of which we, outsiders, cannot judge."

After the Orthodox priest, a Protestant pastor from France also turns to L. N. with an expression of sympathy.

L. N. answers him:

"Dear Sir, I have just received your letter, and I thank you for the feelings which you express to me in it. I am also grateful to you for the quotations which you make from Auguste Sabatier. I greatly regret that I know this remarkable man only by name and by hearsay. The quotations which you cite about his manner of understanding Christianity prove to me that I must be in complete union of thought and feeling with him and with you and with all those who share these ideas. There is, however, a point on which I differ from you. This is your idea of the necessity of a church, and consequently of pastors, that is, of men invested with a certain authority. I cannot forget verses 8 and 9 of chapter XXIII of Matthew — not because it is a Gospel text, but because it is for me a truth quite self-evident, that there should be neither pastors, nor teachers, nor leaders among Christians. And that it is precisely this violation of the Gospel law that has almost annihilated the significance of the preaching of true Christianity down to the present time. For me the fundamental idea of Christianity is the restoration of direct relations between God and man. Every man who wishes to set himself in the place of an intermediary in these relations hinders him whom he wishes to lead from entering into immediate communion with God and, what is worst of all, himself withdraws entirely from every possibility of the Christian life. In my opinion it is the height of pride, that is, the sin that most removes one from God, to say to oneself that I can help others to live well and to save their souls. All that a man striving to follow the Christian teaching can do is to strive to perfect himself as far as possible (Matt. V. 48), to direct upon this perfecting all his powers, all his energy. This is the only means of influencing one's neighbours, of helping them on their path toward the good. If there is a church, no one knows its bounds, nor whether he is a member of it. All that a man can wish and hope for is to be a part of it, but no one can ever be sure of this, and still less can one presume in oneself the right and the possibility of directing others toward it.

I beg you, dear Sir, to forgive me for the frankness with which I set forth to you an opinion contrary to your own, and to believe in the feelings of sympathy and respect with which I remain ready to serve you. Lev Tolstoy."

In printing this letter, the pastor gives briefly the content of his own letter to L. N. as well:

> "Not having kept a copy of the letter which I sent to this great Christian, to congratulate him on having been excommunicated for his fidelity to the Gospel, I will only tell you that in the letter I informed him about Auguste Sabatier and about the sympathy he had for the excommunicated. I cited to him several extracts from letters received by me from my late teacher, who was my spiritual father, and among others the following passage:
>
> "One may be a Christian without believing in much of what the church believes or what is in the Bible; but one may be a Christian only in so far as one is conscious in oneself of the spirit of Christ. It is this spirit that changes the conscience and produces the moral conversion, the new birth. If the new man has been engendered in you, strive to grow and to magnify him in yourself freely, because he is a son of God, and lovingly, since he must, on this side too, be a son of God."

At this same time L. N. expresses, in a letter to the Englishman Maude, an interesting characterization of the writer Ruskin:

> "The other day I read an excellent book about him, *Ruskin et la Bible*, by Hugues, I think. The chief trait of Ruskin is that he could never fully free himself from the churchly-Christian world-view. At the beginning of his works on social questions, when he wrote *unto the Last*, he freed himself from dogmatic tradition, but the misty churchly-Christian understanding of the demands of life, which gave him the possibility of uniting these ideals with the aesthetic, remained with him to the end and weakened his preaching; it was weakened also by the artificiality, and therefore the obscurity, of his poetic language. Do not think that I have denigrated (*dénigrer*) the activity of this great man, quite rightly called a prophet — I have always admired and admire him — but I point out the spots that are even in the sun. He is especially good when an intelligent writer of the same disposition as himself makes extracts from him, as in *Ruskin et la Bible* (read it), but to read Ruskin, as I read him, straight through, greatly weakens the impression."

Infinitely varied were L. N.'s correspondents: now a Romanian queen, now an Orthodox priest, now a sympathizing Englishman, now a Bulgarian sitting in prison for refusing military service. There too the good tidings penetrated. The Bulgarian Shopov writes to L. N., and he answers him:

> "Dear friend Georgi. I received your letter long ago and was very glad and grateful to you for it, but did not answer because of ill health and a multitude of affairs. Please continue to inform me of your situation. How do you bear the imprisonment? Is it strict? Are visitors admitted to you, are books given? Inform me also of your family situation. Have you parents? Who are your kin, and how do they regard your act? Can I not be of some use to you in some way? If it is possible, translate your letters to me into Russian, and if that is not possible, then write as legibly as you can, so that every letter can be made out. Then I get at the sense. Perhaps it is just as hard for you to read my letters. But I think that you must understand Russian better than we understand Bulgarian. As for your having been tried not for the reason of your refusal, but for the non-fulfilment of military orders — that they always do. They have nothing else to do. And I truly pity them. And you, who are in their power and deprived by them of freedom, must nevertheless feel pity for them. They feel that the truth and God are against them, and they clutch at everything in order to save themselves, but their days are numbered. And that terrible revolution which you are making — not by storming the Bastille, but by sitting in prison — destroys and will destroy all the present godless ordering of life and will give the possibility for a new one to be founded. I have devoted all my powers to serving God in this, and if it is possible to send it to you, I should be glad to forward to you what I have written about this. I kiss you in brotherly fashion. Lev Tolstoy."

10 August 1901.

In September L. N. had occasion to intervene in international politics and to express his opinion on the Franco-Russian alliance. To this question he had already earlier devoted a long article entitled "Christianity and Patriotism"; now he answered an inquiry of Pietro Mazzini, the Paris correspondent of the Italian newspapers. L. N.'s answer is simple, brief, and clear:

> "My answer to your first question, as to 'what the Russian people thinks of the Franco-Russian alliance,' is the following: the Russian people — the real people — has not the slightest notion of the existence of this alliance; but even if it did know of this alliance, I am sure that, since all nations are to it entirely alike, its sound sense, as well as its feeling of humanity, would indicate to it that this exclusive alliance with one nation, in preference to every other, can have no other aim than to draw it into enmity, and perhaps even into wars, with other nations, and therefore this alliance would be in the highest degree disagreeable to it.
>
> To the question 'whether the Russian people shares the enthusiasm of the French people,' I think I may answer that not only does the Russian people not share this enthusiasm (if it really exists at all — which I greatly doubt), but, if the people knew of all that is being done and said in France in connection with this alliance, it would experience rather a feeling of distrust and antipathy toward that nation which, without any reasonable ground, suddenly begins to display toward it a sudden and exclusive love.
>
> With regard to the third question, 'what the significance of this alliance is for civilization in general,' I think I have the right to suppose that, since this alliance can have no other aim than war or the threat of war directed against other nations, it cannot but be pernicious. As for the significance of this alliance for the two nationalities composing it, it is clear that both in the past and in the future it has been a positive evil for both peoples. The French government, the press, and all that part of French society which extols this alliance have already gone, and will be forced to go, to still greater concessions and compromises against the traditions of a free and humane people, in order to appear — or actually to be — in agreement in intentions and feelings with the government most despotic, most backward, and most cruel in all Europe. And this has been and will be a great injury to France. Whereas for Russia this alliance has already had, and, if it continues, will have, an influence still more baneful. Since the time of this ill-starred alliance, the Russian government, which once was ashamed of the opinion of Europe and reckoned with it, now no longer concerns itself about it and, feeling behind it the support of this strange friendship on the part of a nation reckoned the most civilized in the world, becomes with each day more and more reactionary, despotic, and cruel. So that this savage and unhappy alliance can have, in my opinion, no other influence than the most negative, both upon the welfare of the two peoples and upon civilization in general."

Yasnaya Polyana. 9 September 1901.

Finally, on 5 September L. N., accompanied by Sofya Andreyevna, his daughters, and friends, set out from Yasnaya Polyana for the Crimea.
