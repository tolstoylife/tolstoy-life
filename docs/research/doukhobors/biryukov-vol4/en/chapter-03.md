# Volume IV, Chapter 3. Expressions of Sympathy on the Occasion of the Excommunication

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-03.md`](../ru/chapter-03.md).

---

Alexander Nikiforovich Dunaev, one of Lev Nikolaevich's friends, now deceased, who collected the letters and telegrams received by L. N. on the occasion of his excommunication, gives the following explanation of the selection he made from these letters; in a letter to V. G. Chertkov he says:

> "The copies I am sending you represent one tenth of all the letters received by L. N. on the occasion of his excommunication. The abusive, reproachful, and unsympathetic letters are almost all included; what has been left uncopied are those that are simply dull and colourless.
>
> All that I am sending has been picked out as characteristic of the attitude of people of differing intellectual and moral levels toward a man who has become the centre of the spiritual life of mankind.
>
> The sympathetic letters are so numerous that to print them all would be to repeat the same thoughts dozens of times over, only in different expressions. I do not append the signatures, since this might perhaps be unpleasant for the writers. Perhaps it would be better not to print the place of dispatch either, so as not to expose a correspondent from some small town or village to police search. The whole enormous quantity of foreign letters, received from all ends of the earth, is left aside: these I have not touched, for two reasons. First, the attitude toward L. N. of people living beyond the bounds of Russia was always expressed freely and could not meet with those obstacles to which the disclosure of sympathy toward L. N. is subject in Russia, and therefore it is already known to the whole educated world. Second, transcribing from foreign languages is too laborious, and especially from such tongues as Spanish, Hungarian, Czech, Dutch, and other little-current dialects.
>
> What matters is that abroad they should learn how the enormous majority within Russia itself responded to the attempt of the men of darkness and falsehood to dim and disgrace the name of the greatest man of all times and peoples, who lives among us. And that part of the letters which you will print will give an idea of what an explosion of indignation and of self-contempt was called forth by the defenders of falsehood with their vile sally — hypocritically masked under a supposed love — against him to whose word the whole world listens; and of how much love and sympathy for him lies hidden in the soul of the Russian people, 0.99 of whom dare not openly express their true sympathies for fear of being subjected to violence and persecution. How one longs to live to see the day when the whole Russian people will openly show with whom it stands, and will turn away from that immoral gang of deceivers who imagine that they are still strong in the superstition of the people, and who drive from it every man bearing it light."

Thus, there were at our disposal about 100 letters and telegrams. We made a further selection, taking the most characteristic; in this way the letters here given make up approximately one hundredth of all that L. N. received during February and March, the months following the excommunication.

Here is a letter from the well-known émigré and publicist Alisov; we take from his letter the most essential part:

> "The excommunication conceals within itself a great moral: it has shown plainly to all that a great, fearless, humane writer, even though he hold the rank of a retired lieutenant, may prove incomparably stronger than the tsar, the government, and the Holy Synod. The whole autocratic, monstrous clique does not dare to lay a finger on you, feeling that the public opinion of the whole civilized world stands behind you, that the least encroachment would arouse universal indignation. The government, lacking the courage to destroy you outright, as it once destroyed dozens of the best Russian writers, has resorted to incitement, to indirect murder… The Synod, imagining that it has some significance for the people, resorted to excommunication as a means of delivering you up to be torn to pieces by an obscurantist mob; it consecrated the knives in advance… Had the savage, hypocritical scheme succeeded, the gentle pastors, at the very moment when the mob was tearing you to pieces, would have been washing their hands in holy water. The bloodthirsty, thoroughly ecclesiastical design has turned into a madly absurd farce; the murderer, ready to strike his victim from behind in the dark, suddenly stumbled unexpectedly and fell headfirst into a cesspit. The Holy Synod has become a worldwide laughing-stock."

Here is a specimen of a letter from a man who frankly declares that he is not a follower of L. N. and is nonetheless outraged by the Synod's message; he writes:

> "Permit me, though I do not belong to your disciples, to congratulate you on the occasion of the Synod's message of 21–22 February of this year, published today in the general press. You, the mighty writer of the Russian land, have achieved the impossible — to rouse the Orthodox from their somnolence and to stir up the age-old bog of our clergy. Naturally, the bog first spattered mud, but time will pass — the silt will settle, and the springs you have called to life will cover it with a stream of living water, for life is movement.
>
> No one who reads Russian, save the 'humble ones' who signed the message, understands 'those near' in the sense of church-police statistics, but always in the sense of nearness in spirit; and such men will not be touched by an anonymous slander, which only besmirches those who created it.
>
> May, then, what has been achieved give you, dear Lev Nikolaevich, new strength to create new wondrous images in the continuation of your struggle, and give us Russians the hope of greeting more than once again the victory of the spirit over darkness."

And here is what his ardent young disciple says in a letter:

> "Know, then, that the greater part of the young forces are on your side, and if we live, we shall go by the same road as you, by the road of the true teaching of Christ. Accept from your younger brother a sincere wish for everything good. May truth, love, and freedom shine forth!"

Characteristic is the confession of one old Catholic; he writes, among other things:

> "We are of the same age, much-respected Lev Nikolaevich — I was born on 16 September 1828, and almost until my sixtieth year I lived, like the majority of people, believing in those rites and fables which were presented to us under the guise of the teaching of Christ. I am a Catholic, and my children, eight souls, are Orthodox. My life was, in the moral sense, dark and empty, and if ever I did good, it was by chance.
>
> But in 1888 I managed, with great difficulty, to obtain your works *What I Believe*, *A Confession*, and *The Translation of the Gospels*. I read them and saw clearly, as in the light of the sun, and all the phenomena of life became meaningful to me, and life became joyful, full of content. I copied out these books with my own hand, and they became my favourite, my constant companions, until my death."

Here is a modest but sincere message from a group of intellectuals:

> "Lev Nikolaevich, if anyone's praise or blame of your captivating activity can strengthen its beneficial influence upon mankind's capacity to think and to feel, then, in our opinion, the Orthodox Synod has rendered the world a service that, though involuntary, is useful. Even we — lazy and timorous, but ardently loving you, your readers and admirers — have come to ourselves and felt such an uplift of spirit that we cannot deny ourselves the satisfaction of the need to speak out regarding the movement of thought now taking place in society, called forth by the Synod's message. One may disagree with some of your positions, but one cannot but feel upon oneself, and notice in those around us, the influence of that loving, kind, and honest thing which you sow, and which is precisely what called forth the famous message of the 'humble ones.'"

Soon after the excommunication L. N. fell ill, but fortunately not for long. The news of his recovery again called forth a whole swarm of expressions of sympathy. Such, for example, is the telegram from Kiev, from the students of the Kiev Polytechnic.

> "Lev Nikolaevich, we, the people of Kiev, send you, the greatest and noblest writer of our day, an expression of profound joy on the occasion of your recovery, and we hope that the higher justice will preserve your life for many more years yet, for the good of suffering fellow men and for the service of the pure ideals of evangelical love, truth, goodness, and freedom."

There follow 1,080 signatures, sent separately by post. Interesting in its content is a letter from the Russian colony in Geneva:

> "Dear Lev Nikolaevich. We are fully confident that the absurd decree of the Synod of 22 February of this year could not disturb the calm and the cheerfulness of your spirit. But, present at the fact of this insolent hypocrisy, we cannot refrain from expressing to you our ardent sympathy and our solidarity with you in many of the 'crimes' laid to your charge by the Synod. We should sincerely wish to be deemed worthy of the honour which the Synod has done you, in setting off by so sharp a line its own shameful existence from your honest life. In its short-sightedness the Synod has overlooked your chief 'crime' against it — namely, that by your searchings you scatter the darkness which it serves, and give a powerful moral impulse to the true progress of mankind. For this we bring you our deep gratitude, and from our hearts wish the prolongation of your life for many years yet."

Very originally was sympathy for L. N. manifested at the travelling exhibition of pictures in Petrograd, which coincided with the excommunication. At first a group of visitors to the exhibition sent the following telegram:

> "The public present at the travelling exhibition, at the sight of your portrait, merged in a single impulse of goodwill and ardent gratitude to the great teacher of life."

598 persons signed at once. But, not being sure of the proper delivery of this telegram, the visitors sent a copy of it by post, accompanied by the following letter:

> "Up to now we do not know for certain whether this telegram has been handed to you, and therefore we consider it our duty to try to convey it by another route, in the present letter, and at the same time to send the original signatures and to report briefly on what took place before your portrait.
>
> The appearance of the portrait at the exhibition gave society occasion to express its condemnation of the Synod and to declare its sympathy for you — for your constant responsiveness to all the phenomena of Russian life, for your tireless and bold summons to the search for truth and to the struggle for it. The public, gathered with this purpose, began as early as twelve o'clock to crowd before your portrait and awaited with impatience the initiative of arranging an ovation. Toward two o'clock students began to adorn the portrait with garlands of living flowers, and loud applause broke out. Then, in the course of three or four hours, the portrait was several times showered with masses of greenery and flowers; cries were heard of 'Down with Pobedonostsev!' and 'Hurrah for Lev Nikolaevich!', taken up in unison by all. Everyone unanimously accepted the proposal to send a telegram of greeting, and soon people of every rank and station covered it with their signatures. As they dispersed, each one carried away a flower from the portrait as a keepsake. All were united by a feeling of heartfelt gratitude toward the fighter for freedom of conscience and the preacher of true love for one's neighbour."

The portrait was found "scandalous" and, by order of the authorities, was removed from the exhibition. When a new group wished to adorn it once more with flowers, then, learning of its removal, they sent L. N. a garland of flowers to Moscow, with the following note:

> "Not having found your portrait at the exhibition, we send you our love."

Very precious is a letter from the factory workers of Kovrov, Vladimir Province:

> "Most honourable and much-respected Count Lev Nikolaevich! We hasten to attest to you our profoundest respect and to congratulate you on the high and solemn festival in the name of Him who, 1,900 years ago, preached to men holy brotherhood, freedom, and equality. And we too, having read a number of your works, see in them the striving of your mind and heart to follow, and to lead the human race to, the actual fulfilment and realization of these words in deed.
>
> O Lev Nikolaevich, how great and glorious you are; we thank you, we thank you, that you have remembered us, the paupers stinted in life. The fruits of you and of men like you we already see upon ourselves: with us, poor ragged folk, unlettered men, good educated people enter into communion and conversation, and they do all this kindly and lovingly, not scorning us; and this alone is a great thing — we feel how something good, at times breathtaking, pours into our hearts.
>
> And when, in the dark moments of our life, there come into our heads the thoughts: who am I? and what have I, besides a wife and children? I shall die just as my father did, who, lying on his deathbed and seeing us children in a helpless condition, could only turn to and put his hope in the feeble strength of his wife, and to that feeble strength he made his plea: do not, please, forget the little ones. And my mother dragged out a wretched life, full of suffering and of daily deprivations, and that life she passed on to us, and our children will inherit it. And yet I shall die happier than my father, because today such men as you, Lev Nikolaevich, are working for poor mankind, and consequently for my children too. And this thought will pour into us the hope that our children will be left after us not only with a mother, but also good men, like you.
>
> And so, Lev Nik., we thank you and pray God to prolong your life and health, so precious to us paupers, because we know that as long as you are alive and well, we have in your person our protector, who cares for us, and the scourge of men's vices. Your name, Lev Nikolaevich, is passed with rapture from mouth to mouth among us, as that of a truly believing Christian.
> With respect for you, and loving you, the factory workers."

Next we shall give a letter from a peasant:

> "Deeply respected and dear Lev Nikolaevich. The council of the wicked has published in its *Gazette* a decree concerning your religious views. The hypocritical Sanhedrin has evidently become quite confused, and now, with humbly cunning speech, attempts to call down misfortune upon you, in the reckoning that it will thereby prop up its tottering kingdom. The hypocritical serf-owners of the human soul — what have they gained by this? The answer to them is the general contempt of the whole enlightened world. Dear Lev Nikolaevich, great teacher, defender of the persecuted, apostle of the holy teaching of Christ, draw, then, from this reverent chorus of millions of hearts beating with ardent love for you, new strength for your further holy preaching for the good of mankind! You cannot doubt that these millions of people who adore you will henceforth, with still greater fervour of divine love and human devotion, look upon you, the bearer of truth, and will pray to our common god, called mercy, to prolong your priceless days for the common joy and happiness of all that lives. They say that all letters addressed to your name are read by the police. From fear of falling into misfortune, I dare not sign as would be fitting for an honest man. And indeed it is not a matter of my name. I count myself happy to add one more voice to those that have already expressed to you their feelings, both of deep rapture and of boundless love and devotion. A Russian peasant who has managed to read and understand all that you have written, and who belongs to no sect or persuasion whatever. Was Orthodox from childhood."

From the pupils of the school for the blind in Petersburg. L. N. brought joy to them too:

> "Gracious sir, Count Lev Nikolaevich! Permit us to express our sincere gratitude and the heartfelt adoration which you have inspired in us by your immortal works; through them you have taught us to think seriously, have awakened in us many beautiful feelings, and have filled our inner world with the images you have created, which afford us great spiritual delights. We are wholly happy if we manage to assimilate even one thought from your works, so little accessible to our understanding. What a gladdening animation comes over our companionable circles when only your name is uttered! With what eagerness we catch the slightest piece of news touching upon your precious life and your matchless activity. Forgive us, then, deeply respected Lev Nikolaevich, that we have dared to trouble you: this letter is called forth by an irrepressible need to pour out before you those inexpressibly beautiful feelings of ours which have been inspired by your genius. May it not seem to you an impertinence if we permit ourselves, in conclusion, to express our sincere wish that your earthly course may be prolonged for many years yet, giving us the right to hope to experience more than once again the gladdening effect of the irresistible power of your creative work. The pupils of the school for the blind."

And here is the ardent word of a student:

> "Dear teacher! 'They' have understood you and ask you: why have you come to hinder 'them' from committing iniquities in Christ's name, why do you hinder 'them' from crucifying Christ anew, from perverting and reviling His holy teaching? But know, great teacher, that upon the earth redeemed by Christ there appear other men also — apostles, who sacredly keep His holy precepts and proclaim them to the world when it forgets them. Accept, then, the holy respect and the deep, reverent homage paid to your high apostolic ministry, and go to the end along this hard road of the service of God.
> A student."

Yet one more touching message:

> "Your excommunication from the church has plunged all into the fiercest indignation, even those who do not love you. By this excommunication they have only brought it about that you have become dearer than ever to all. I have a little nephew, not yet a year old; we have named him Lev in your honour; he knows your portrait, and when one says: 'Lyovushka, show where Lev Nikolaevich is,' — he reaches out his little hands toward your portrait. We are teaching him to love you and to live as Christ teaches, and as you do, His true and unhypocritical follower."

And again the serious voice of the workers:

> "We, the workers, deeply sympathize with you over your unjust condemnation by the Synod, that is, by several men who in proud delusion call themselves the 'Church of Christ,' as though the other millions were not members of that great society which is called Christians. We Russians, simple labourers, feel and understand the truths of Christ's covenant well-nigh more deeply than do the members of the Synod. It is not you who ought to be excommunicated from the Church of Christ, but those who do not themselves enter into the kingdom of heaven, and do not let others in either. We understand that your literary works are aimed not at the overthrow of the great truths, but, on the contrary, at making them clear. But we know, too, that in all ages the men who stood on the side of piety, and the great leaders of the people along the path of love of mankind and of truth, have always been estranged by those who trampled freedom, virtue, and honour underfoot. Did not our own great teacher suffer on the cross for this very thing? But that was one time — now it is another. In our days even the rabble understands what advanced mankind did not understand. Your word has not fallen on barren soil; it will pass from age to age for the salvation of mankind from many temptations. And we honour you as a great man, to whom a monument not made by hands is being raised in our hearts.
> From the workers of the N— factory, 4 March."

Women students write, among other things:

> "Forgive us the boldness of our address to you; we have resolved upon it because we wished somehow to pour out all the feelings called forth by what has happened. Look upon this wish not as the prank of a little, insignificant circle of students, only beginning to live, but as a faint semblance of a protest against the enslavement of another's conscience, against the desire to lay fetters upon the flight of that titanic thought to which we already owe so many beautiful creations, so many artistic and moral delights — as a tribute brought to an incomparable artist and a great Christian."

There follow 6 signatures of women students.

There were abusive letters too, of the following content:

> "To the beastlike Lev in human hide. Be thou henceforth anathema accursed, spawn of hell, of the spirit of darkness, old fool. Lev is a beast, not a man; you ought to drop dead sooner, sooner, you brute. One of those who grieve over your ruined soul, once human…"

But such abuse, by its small number and its absurdity, was drowned in a sea of expressions of sympathy and goodwill.

Besides letters, L. N. was also sent various gifts. One of the most interesting was a block of green glass, beautifully polished in the form of a paperweight. On one side there is an inscription made in gold letters:

> "You have shared the fate of many great men who go ahead of their age, deeply revered L. N. Even before them, men were burned at the stake, left to rot in prisons and in exile. Let the Pharisees, the high priests, excommunicate you as they please and from whatever they please. The Russian people will always be proud, counting you their own — great, dear, beloved."

To this follow many signatures from the employees and workers of the Bryansk glassworks.

To A. Endourov, who sent this gift, L. N. replied with the following brief letter:

> "I have received your splendid gift, in which the inscription is especially dear to me, and I beg you to convey my most lively gratitude to all who signed it."

All the responses enumerated above are a direct reaction to the Synod's decree excommunicating L. N. from Orthodoxy. Besides these direct responses, we have testimonies of L. N.'s growing influence upon Russian society, independent of the synodal act — although in all probability that act had an indirect influence, increasing L. N.'s popularity among the youth of those years. The information communicated relates to 1901 and to the first half of 1902.

We place below the testimony of one woman in charge of a public library, who collected about 2,000 responses, for the most part from young readers. The question put was this: whom do you consider your favourite writer, and what is that writer's most beloved work? Of the 2,000 answers, about 700 fell to the share of Lev Nikolaevich; then came Gorky (about 600) and Dostoevsky (about 500). It is interesting to note that these three Russian writers enjoy the greatest popularity at the present time in Germany.

The most-read book proved to be L. N. Tolstoy's novel *Resurrection*. It is mentioned as a favourite book about 300 times.

A quite special place is occupied by the responses concerning *The Kreutzer Sonata* and certain of the religious-philosophical works of the last ten to fifteen years. One third-form gymnasium pupil writes: "Pozdnyshev is an intelligent man, but weak of character: he could have held his wife back from her fall, but, owing to some embittered unwillingness, did not. I think that in my own future personal life what is described in *The Kreutzer Sonata* will not happen; reason ought always to restrain a man's impulses. For this work, my great thanks to Tolstoy."

Another (also a gymnasium pupil) writes: "*The Kreutzer Sonata* opened my eyes to the true meaning of the relations between man and woman that exist in our society. These relations are nothing but bestiality. It is terrible, but just. And I shall apply all the powers of my soul to avoiding such a relation, unworthy of a human being."

In a whole series of responses it is indicated that "*The Kreutzer Sonata* made one take a conscious attitude toward woman," "changed my way of life." "Tolstoy, by *The Kreutzer Sonata*, set me on a new path, from which I shall surely never now stray, and along which I shall walk with a firm tread in my future life," and so on.

"Tolstoy's philosophical works were, for my correspondents," says the librarian, "a veritable revelation: in some they 'produced an upheaval in the soul'; in others they 'utterly changed, at the very root, all their views on contemporary reality'; in others 'spiritual blindness came to an end, and intellectual and moral enlightenment began'; in others 'there arose true conceptions of the most important sides of our life: of human relations, of faith, of society, of the state'; in others 'at last there was found a point of departure from which one ought to begin practical work: to serve righteousness and truth and to do good…' Or: Tolstoy's well-known works "are important not in respect of whether the author justly resolves the questions he has posed, but in that they open one's eyes to the unsoundness of the important foundations of our life and, beyond doubt, lead a man out of an inert attitude toward these foundations — so convincing are the author's arguments" (a first-form gymnasium pupil).

The raptures before Tolstoy's genius know no bounds. Judging by the responses, many of the young have made of Tolstoy an idol for themselves, and some read and reread certain of his works many times over. Some had read *The Prisoner of the Caucasus*, *Childhood and Boyhood*, *The Sevastopol Sketches*, and others a "countless" (!) number of times, and many passages they even know by heart. Tolstoy calls forth in the youth a "reverent" attitude toward himself, not only as a writer, but also as a "rare man, appearing once in centuries." Here is a series of the most varied responses about Tolstoy:

> "I love Tolstoy for his constant striving toward truth, for his untiring thirst for it, for his sincerity, for the plasticity and artistry of his literary works, and, finally, I love him as a man rare and unique of his kind" (second-form gymnasium pupil).

> "I like all the works of L. N. Tolstoy. Why his works please — it is needless to say: what is in the book is so in deed too; what he says in the book, that he does himself" (second-form gymnasium pupil).

> "Tolstoy is, beyond doubt, the greatest novelist, a great philosopher, and a rare (if one may so put it) religious thinker. His novels are a perfection above which none is to be found in any of the world's literatures. His searchings after such forms of life as would be worthy of man astonish and captivate by their sincerity, their fervour, their impetuosity, and their unbending striving to find what is sought. His thoughts on the relation of man to his surroundings, to God, and to himself — thoughts on that in whose name and for the sake of which we live — astonish by their simplicity, on the one hand, and by their grandeur on the other" (a seminarist).

> "With Tolstoy the foremost place is always given to the resolution of questions of honour and conscience. To order one's life on the principles of love for God and for man, so that the conscience may be clean and at peace — that, it seems to me, is what Tolstoy strives toward. There is no, nor can there be, a striving higher than this" (a third-form pupil of a teachers' seminary).

> "Tolstoy opened my eyes to the most important sides of human life. Only after my acquaintance with Tolstoy did I grasp the true value, meaning, and purpose of the state, the church, and society, and of all of contemporary life in general. I now know well that all this is not what is needed for man; now I know clearly what I am to do, how to order my life" (third-form gymnasium pupil).

> "Of all Russian writers, Tolstoy interests me most. He is so great a man, and so original, that I have not even the words for an exact definition of him. Though I know literature only poorly, yet for some reason I believe that writers like Tolstoy there have not been and will not be. His peculiarity is that with him everything is his own. I love Tolstoy and know him well, because I have the good fortune to be the owner of all his works. I read and studied him for four years, and carried away this conviction: that the majority, both of the public and of the critics, do not understand Tolstoy. They assert that he is a preacher of non-resistance to evil, an enemy of science and civilization. All this is sheer falsehood or want of thought. The Tolstoyan non-resistance to evil is, in its essence, higher than any resistance, and to be a follower of it is a thousand times harder than to be an adherent of resistance. Tolstoy, by his non-resistance, teaches us not to support evil deeds, and that is all. Do not support an evil deed, and it will fall of itself, like a house without a foundation, like a man without legs. Is Tolstoy an enemy of science and civilization? No. He says only that science is in hostile hands, or in the hands of soulless people. And it is our task to give scientific acquisitions their proper direction: to serve the good of the majority. It is not science's fault that men, making use of its acquisitions, have made themselves rifles and cannon, in order to exterminate mankind. So too with civilization. All this is like blaming bread because vodka — that is, poison — is made from it. Or, better still: in a year of famine, a rich landowner's barns are bursting with grain, while all around men are dying of famine typhus. Surely no one will blame the stores of grain for lying idle, for letting men die a starving death" (one who has finished the classical gymnasium).

Tolstoy pleases many because he has widened in them "their conceptions of good and evil," has "taught them to love people, to love mankind," "will convince them that the moral life is more advantageous," has "shown real life in his numerous artistic and journalistic works," has "made clear the true meaning of the activity of those who lord it over life," has "sharply and convincingly exposed the hypocritical order of life, in which it is well only for the strong, while the weak man is downtrodden and leads a beastly life, the life almost of a slave," and so on, and so forth.

"Having excommunicated this man from the church, its representatives have branded themselves with shame and prepared their own imminent downfall."

Thus was accomplished the absurd act of excommunication, which raised Lev Nikolaevich to an unprecedented height of spiritual and moral influence upon all mankind.
