# Volume IV, Chapter 11. 1907. Lessons with the Children. The Land Question

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-11.md`](../ru/chapter-11.md).

---

The year 1907 is not rich in external facts in the life of L. N. It passed quietly and peacefully at Yasnaya Polyana, but this period may be marked out by the intense inner work that L. N. lived through, and which undoubtedly drew him nearer to eternal life. His diary for this year is very characteristic. Each day's entry consists of two parts. In the first part he gives, as it were, a brief history of his life, outer and inner, for the days he has lived through, and then proceeds to set forth, point by point, the most important thoughts that have come into his mind during these days. Those of these principles which make it possible to mark the stages in the development of L. N.'s soul we shall give here either in full or in brief summary.

There were many visitors in the course of this year, many letters written, and a whole series of significant articles.

Besides the inner work indicated above and his communion with people, L. N.'s time was occupied chiefly with two matters: the reworking of *The Circle of Reading* and the lessons with the children. One may also note one particular phenomenon of this time — the weakening of memory and of physical powers in general, of which L. N. more than once makes mention in his diary. We shall set forth below the chief episodes of his active life, so far as possible across all its departments. At the beginning of the year he writes in his diary:

> "Today I thought about how it is impossible to live peacefully with a high opinion of oneself, that the first condition of a peaceful and good life is that which Francis spoke of about himself, when they would not let him in. And today all morning I was occupied with this diminishing of my denominator. And, it seems, not uselessly: I vividly recalled to myself all that which I now condemn in my sons: the passion for gambling, hunting, vanity, debauchery, avarice... The chief thing is to understand that you yourself are below the average level in morality, in weakness, in mind, and especially in knowledge — a man declining in his mental faculties — and not to forget this, and how easy it will be to live. One must prize the valuation of God, and not that of people."

This entry calls for explanation on two points. The expression "that which Francis spoke of, when they would not let him in" is an allusion to the well-known story from the life of Francis, conveying his conversation with his disciple as to "wherein lies perfect joy." To his question the disciple runs through a whole series of "joys" that come into his head, but to all of them Francis says — no, perfect joy is not in that, not even in this, that Christianity should spread over the whole earth. The disciple, in bewilderment, asks: "Wherein, then, master, is perfect joy?" — and Francis answers that if they, wayfarers, worn out on the road, soaked through and hungry, should not be let into a night's shelter, but should be driven off and abused, and they should bear this with love, then in this would lie perfect joy. L. N. greatly loved this story and often made use of it to express the true religious feeling of humility.

Here too L. N. has recourse to a mathematical comparison he was fond of. He speaks of the diminishing of the denominator. By L. N.'s definition, the worth of a man is measured by a fraction, of which the numerator is that which the man possesses, and the denominator is that which the man thinks of himself. With one and the same qualities, the worth of a man is the higher, the less the man thinks of himself, just as the value of a fraction increases with the diminishing of the denominator.

On 2 February L. N. writes:

> "I read the splendid book Baba-Bharaty by Krishna."

And then, a few days later:

> "I have, it seems, not noted down that I have written a long letter to Baba-Bharaty.
>
> I am afraid that he is fond of glory."

In March L. N. occupies himself diligently with the peasant children of Yasnaya Polyana.

On 17 March he writes in his diary:

> "During this time I have been occupied only with lessons with the little ones. The further I go, the more I see the great and ever greater difficulty of the matter, and at the same time the greater hope of success. All that has been done up to now is hardly fit for anything. Yesterday I divided them into two classes. Today I pondered with the smaller class. During this time there have been various visitors and good letters. Yesterday Kuzmin came from the Malevantsy. Very joyful."

Iv. Fed. Nazhivin, who visited L. N. at this time, gives an interesting account of these lessons, at which he managed to be present.

> "These occupations consisted in his telling the children something out of the Gospel, and then making them retell it. The aim of these occupations was, in the first place, the instruction of the children in religion, if one may so express it, and in the second place, the compiling of a Gospel from the retelling of the children themselves. Of these occupations of his he could not speak without tears, and he often repeated: 'the children come to study, and I study along with them...' Well then, at one of such lessons it was my good fortune to be present too; so as not to embarrass the children, we, I and the resident doctor of Yasnaya Polyana D. P. Makovitsky, sat quietly in the next room, the door of which Lev Nikolaevich had purposely left ajar. Having talked of the Gospel, Lev Nikol., somehow apropos, began to tell the children the following legend:
>
> "There lived in the old days in the desert a certain hermit, who passed all his time in prayer. And once he went to his preceptor, an even more pious elder, and asked him what else he might do in order to deserve well before God. And the elder sent him to a neighbouring village, to the muzhiks, who always brought him food.
>
> — Go to them, — said the elder, — live with them a little day, perhaps you will learn something from them...
>
> The hermit went to the village to the muzhiks, and sees: a muzhik has risen from sleep, muttered 'Lord,' and quickly to work. And so the muzhik laboured until evening, and in the evening, having returned from the field, again only muttered 'Lord,' and quickly to sleep. And the hermit returned to the elder and says:
>
> — No, there is nothing to learn from them... They remember God in all only twice a day, morning and evening...
>
> Then the elder took a cup, filled it to the very brim with oil, and handed it to the hermit:
>
> — Here, — he says, — take this cup and in the course of the day go round with it about the village, and so that not a single drop is spilled...
>
> And the hermit took the cup and went off, and in the evening returned.
>
> — Well, good, — said the elder, — now tell me, how many times in the course of the day did you remember God?
>
> — Not once... — answered the hermit, abashed, — I kept looking at the cup the whole time, I was afraid of spilling it.
>
> — Well, you see... — said the elder (here Lev Nikolaevich's voice began to break and tremble). — Well, you see, you only thought about the cup and even so did not once remember God, while he, the muzhik, feeds both himself and his family, and us two into the bargain, and yet remembered God twice."
>
> Lev Nikolaevich, with eyes full of tears, could scarcely, scarcely finish, moved as he was, the last words — or rather, the children finished them, the little muzhiks, who had received the legend with extraordinary animation."

In April L. N. writes in his diary:

> "I have not written for more than half a month. During this time I have lived decently. I had a bad head-cold, and now I feel very weak. The children's lessons and the preparation for them absorb me entirely. I notice a weakening of powers, both physical and mental, but inversely proportional to the moral ones. I want to write much, but much I have already left forever unfinished and even unbegun."

At the same time he writes a touching letter to his sister:

> "Dear friend Mashenka. I often think of you with great tenderness, and these last days some kind of voice keeps telling me about you, about how I long, how good it would be to see you, to know about you, to have communion with you. How is your health? About your state of soul I do not ask; it must be good, with your way of life. May God help you to draw near to Him.
>
> With us all is well. Sonya is healthy, vigorous, as always.
>
> With us, to our joy, lives Tanya with her dear little girl. Her husband is for a time abroad with his sick son.
>
> I feel very keenly the loss of Masha, but His will be done, as they say among you, and I say it with all my soul. About myself, except for the good that I have not deserved, I can say nothing. The older I grow, the more peaceful and joyful it becomes in my soul. Often death becomes almost desirable. So good it is in my soul, and so I believe in the nearness of Him in whom one lives, both in life and in death.
>
> Sonya has today arrived from Moscow, she saw your dearest Varya, whom it is always a joy not only to see but even to remember.
>
> Give my bow to all your nuns. May God help them to save themselves. In the world now there is such a terrible, unkind life, that they have chosen the blessed path, and you with them. I love you very much. Write many little words about yourself. I kiss you.
>
> Your brother both by blood and by spirit — do not reject me.
> Lev Tolstoy."

The relations between them became ever more touching and heartfelt. One could see how they, drawing near to God from different sides, drew near to one another, involuntarily and joyfully, which is confirmed too by their further communion.

In his diary L. N. makes a brief review of past events:

> "27 June. The Chertkovs came, lived with us three days. It was very joyful. Nesterov is living here — agreeable. Sergeyenko was here. Yesterday there were 800 children. My state of soul is good. Only, alas, I have already begun to lose the communion with God as I understood it, or rather, felt it, a week ago. But something, and something very important and needful, has remained."

The children and the lessons with them continue to occupy his mind, and he sets down such reflections:

> "I wanted to say this to the children who are expected: you all know that there was a beloved disciple, John. This John lived long, and when he had grown very old, could scarcely move and barely speak, then to all whom he saw he said only one and the same four short words. He said: 'children, love one another.' I too am old, and if you expect of me that I should say something to you, then I can say nothing of my own and will only repeat that which John said: 'children, love one another.' Nothing better than this can be said, for in these words is all that people need. Let people fulfil these words, only let people try to wean themselves from all that which is contrary to love: from quarrels, envy, abuse, condemnation, and all manner of unkind feelings toward their brothers — and all would find it good and joyful to live in the world. And all this is not impossible and not even difficult, but easy. Only let people do this, and all will find it good. And sooner or later — people will come to this. So then let each of us now begin, little by little, to accustom himself to this (Very poor)."

A page of the July diary shows us his intense life. He writes thus:

> "The inner events, however, were the most important: at first such a vivid consciousness of God within me and of the divine life — of love, and therefore of truth and joy, such as I had never before experienced. It continued strongly for about a week, then began to weaken, and the novelty, the joy of the consciousness of this feeling, vanished; but there remained, surely there remained, a rise onto the next, though a small, step of the unconscious, a higher state as against the former, likewise unconscious, one. It began with my trying to remember, on meeting with each person, that within him is God. Then this passed into a consciousness of God within myself. And this consciousness for the first while produced a kind of new feeling of quiet rapture. Now this has passed, and I can call up only the recollection, but not the consciousness. What will come further?
>
> During this time my health is decent, 'for our sins' too good.
>
> I have left off *The Circle of Reading* and, on the occasion of Felten's imprisonment, have been writing the pamphlet 'Kill No One.' Yesterday, though I had not finished it, I read it to Ch. and others. Now I want to write a letter to Stolypin and 'Hands Up,' which came into my head during Goldenweiser's playing.
>
> People have been occupying themselves with me too much of late, and this harms me. I look for my name in the newspaper. It very, very greatly darkens, conceals life. I must struggle."

And L. N. does indeed struggle with this temptation of vanity, and now falls under it, now overcomes it, and writes thus:

> "Before I had time to look round, I was tempted, I began to ascribe to myself a special significance as the founder of a philosophical-religious school; I began to ascribe importance to this, wishing that it should be so. As though this had any significance for my life. All this has significance not for, but against my life, deadening and perverting it."

Having learned of Felten's being sentenced to imprisonment for keeping and distributing his writings, L. N. wrote to him:

> "I have received your letter, dear Felten, and I will not say that I am grieved, but I am weakened: I fear for you, whether you will hold out, whether you will turn to the use of your own self, your real self, this bodily deprivation and condemnation. It ought to be so, and from your letter I see that you wish it to be so.
>
> Hold fast, watch closely, that it should be so, dear friend. This too is a great delight — to be swept swiftly in this spiritual boat along the wind of God's will, that is, to merge more and more so as not to feel it, as one does not feel the wind, being borne along with it. Striking and sad is the cause of your imprisonment. That position which Christ in his time considered already not only acknowledged by all, but such that he spoke only of the further, higher, moral demand — for that position men are now judged and punished. Yours is a good role. May God help you. I kiss you."

The pamphlet for which Felten was chiefly sentenced to prison was "Thou Shalt Not Kill," written by L. N. on the occasion of the murder of King Humbert; its chief idea consisted in this, that the true means of destroying the power of emperors, kings, and presidents lies not in killing them (in their place others are always found), but in this, that men themselves refuse to kill at their command, refuse to be armed murderers upholding the power of the rulers.

Felten, who had the pamphlet in his store, was sentenced to a year in prison. On this occasion, wishing to affirm his own guilt, L. N. wrote a new article, "Kill No One," in which with fresh force he summons people to a union founded not on violence, but on love.

In the diary entry given above L. N. mentions his intention of writing to Stolypin. The letter to Stolypin Lev Nikolaevich did write and send. In this remarkable letter L. N. says, among other things:

> "The causes of those revolutionary horrors which are now taking place in Russia have very deep foundations, but one, the nearest of them, is the discontent of the people with the unjust distribution of land."

Comparing this oppression of landed property with serfdom, L. N. says:

> "The injustice consists in this, that just as there cannot exist a right of one man to own another (slavery), so there cannot exist a right of any one man whatsoever, rich or poor, tsar or peasant, to own land as property."

As one of the means of resolving this contradiction, of abolishing the injustice, L. N. proposes to Stolypin to introduce the system of the "single tax" according to the project of Henry George. L. N. attached enormous significance to this reform and, speaking of it in his letter, expressed himself thus:

> "I propose to you a matter great in its importance. Now do you, the government, relate yourselves to this land question not with pitiful palliatives that achieve nothing, but as one ought, according to the essence of the question, set it up in such a way that you do not wish to cajole some one estate or to make concessions to revolutionary demands, but in such a way that you wish to restore the justice violated from the most ancient times, without thinking of whether this or that has or has not yet been done in Europe; and you will at once — I do not know whether you will pacify the revolution or not, that no one can know, but it will surely come about that one of those chief and lawful pretexts by which the irritation of the people is called forth will be taken away from the revolutionaries forever.
>
> I advise this to you not in view of any governmental or political considerations, but for the sake of the most important matter in the world — if not the destruction, then the weakening of that enmity, that exasperation, that moral evil which now the revolutionaries, just like the government struggling with them, bring into the life of people."

To this letter L. N. appended Henry George's book *Social Problems* and his own exposition of the system of the single tax.

Iv. Fed. Nazhivin, who was at Yasnaya Polyana at this time, tells thus of the fate of this letter:

> "In the summer of 1907, when the peasant world was in ferment, demanding land for itself, L. N. could not contain himself and wrote then to Stolypin a letter in which he implored him to make use of his enormous power in order to stop the horrors that were taking place then in Russia. The first step toward this, in L. N.'s opinion, was the transfer of the land to the peasants on the bases proposed by Henry George. Soon L. N. received an answer through the minister's brother, a contributor to *Novoye Vremya*; this answer, a series of florid phrases, came down to this, that the theory of Henry George is inapplicable. L. N. smiled bitterly and said:
>
> — The only good thing there is in this letter is this little sheet of wonderful paper.
>
> And right there he carefully tore off the blank page from the letter and put it away; he always cherishes every scrap of clean paper, and in this sacred attitude toward the labour of man his loving human soul is seen at full height.
>
> L. N. more than once gave himself his word not to address such letters, but could not hold out and wrote again."

In the letter L. N. recommended that Stolypin should converse with S. D. Nikolayev, a translator and connoisseur of the works of Henry George. Stolypin expressed a wish to make his acquaintance, but for some reason this meeting never took place.

Here is a little page from the August diary:

> "Today I have thought out well the sequence of *The Circle of Reading*. Perhaps I shall change it still, but this too is good. More and more I free myself from the care for the opinion of people. What freedom this is, what joy, what strength. May God help me to free myself entirely.
>
> Just now I read in the newspaper about murders and robberies with threats of murder. Murders and cruelty grow ever stronger and stronger. How then is one to be? How to stop it? They lock up, exile to hard labour, execute. The villainies do not diminish, on the contrary. What then is to be done? One thing and one thing only: for each man himself to put all his powers into living after God's way, and to implore them, the murderers, the robbers, to live after God's way. They will beat, will rob, and I, with my hands raised aloft at their command, will implore them to cease living badly. 'They will not listen, will go on doing the same.' What then is to be done? There is nothing more for me to do. Yes, one ought to speak well about this."

In September the same work over *The Circle of Reading* and new alarms for the soul of Lev Nikolaevich, the struggle for the protection of property, so hateful to his soul. He writes of this with misgiving in his diary:

> "7 Sept. I have occupied myself only with *The Circle of Reading*. I have done little that is visible, but for the soul it is good. I grow stronger, especially in the struggle with the condemnation of people. About this I thought today very importantly. I will set it down later. I have received a hard letter from Novikov and have answered him. Communion with Ch. is as joyful as ever. I am afraid that I am bribed by his partiality for me. Yesterday I looked over the 'collection of thoughts.' It would be good if it were so useful to people as it seems to me to be in the minutes of my self-conceit. During this time my state of soul has been rather good than bad. Just now I felt my own constraint in the writing of this diary by the fact that I know it will be read by Sonya and Chertkov. I will try to forget about them. These last two or three days a hard state of soul, which until today I could not overcome, because at night thieves of cabbage were shooting, and S. complained, and the authorities appeared and seized four peasants, and the women and the fathers come to me to plead. They cannot admit that I, especially living here, should not be the master, and therefore they ascribe everything to me. This is hard, and very, but it is good, because, by making impossible a good opinion of me among people, it drives me into that region where the opinion of people weighs nothing. These last two days I could not overcome a bad feeling. The news about Boulanger. I hope and believe that he has escaped. Just now the governor was here, tout le tremblement. It is both repugnant and pitiful. It was useful to me in that it confirmed me in direct compassion for these people. Yes, I have drawn up the explained subdivision of *The Circle of Reading*, subject to correction. During this time I have made the acquaintance of Malevanny. A very reasonable, wise man."

Refusals of military service were becoming a usual occurrence. Each conscription gave several refusals. And when the news of them reached L. N., he, of course, answered them with warm sympathy. We adduce here a touching letter of his to the peasant Ikonnikov, who refused in October of this year. Here is what L. N. wrote to him.

> "I have received your letter today, dear, beloved brother Ikonnikov...
>
> I rejoice, rejoice, and rejoice at your state of soul, and thank God for it — not for you alone, but for all those people — myself the first — who find, and will yet find, support and confirmation of their faith in the truth, in that the true life is not in the flesh but in the spirit, which is so clear in your life. I thank you very much, not for your life — that is a matter above all gratitude — but for this, that you have so truthfully and so well described to me both that which happened to you and your state of soul. May God help you, dear brother, to continue that same inner spiritual life, in whatever conditions you may be: in confinement or at liberty. Although I cannot but wish and hope that your trial will some day come to an end. I will try to learn in more detail how those who have power over your body can and must act with regard to you. Your loving brother Lev Tolstoy.
>
> Your present letter has called forth in my soul a particularly vivid and tenderly moved feeling of gratitude and love toward you.
>
> Can I not serve you in some way? Do not deprive me of this joy."

The kingdom of violence, of course, was even then not in Russia alone. In Germany the Poles especially suffered from the Prussian laws directed against them. Henryk Sienkiewicz, seeking protection for his compatriots, resolved to turn to the mighty word of Lev Nikolaevich. He sent him his works and a letter, with a request to speak his word in defence of the oppressed.

Lev Nikolaevich answered him thus:

> "Kind Henryk Sienkiewicz,
>
> This strange manner of address is called forth by the wish to avoid the unpleasant, coldly hostile 'gracious sir' and the equally distancing 'Monsieur,' and to enter, in a letter to you, into those close, friendly relations in which I feel myself toward you ever since I read your works *The Połaniecki Family*, *Without Dogma*, and others, for which I thank you. This same cause prompts me to write to you in my own language, in which it is easier for me to express myself clearly and exactly.
>
> The matter of which you write is known to me and has called forth in me not surprise, not even indignation, but only a confirmation of that truth, indubitable for me — however paradoxical it may seem to people who have fallen under the governmental hypnosis — that the existence of violent governments has outlived its time.
>
> In the pagan world there could be a virtuous ruler, Marcus Aurelius, but in our Christian world even the rulers of past ages, all the French Louis and Napoleons, all the Fredericks, Henrys, and Elizabeths, German and English, in spite of all the efforts of their eulogists, cannot in our time inspire anything but loathing... One must struggle not with people, but with that superstition of the necessity of governmental violence which is so incompatible with the contemporary moral consciousness of the people of the Christian world, and more than anything else hinders the humanity of our time from taking that step for which it has long been ready...
>
> As to the requirements of that matter of which you write, the preparation of the Prussian government for the robbing of the Polish landowning peasants, then in this matter too I am more sorry for those people who are arranging this robbery and will carry it into execution, than for those whom they rob. These latter ont le beau rôle. They, on other land too, and in other conditions, will remain that which they were; but the robbers are to be pitied, those are to be pitied who belong to a nation, to a state of robbers, and who feel themselves in solidarity with them. I think that even now for every morally sensitive man there can be no doubt as to the choice: to be a Prussian, in solidarity with his government, or a Pole driven from his nest.
>
> So that is my opinion of what is being accomplished or prepared now in Poznań, and if not an opinion of the matter itself, then those thoughts which it called forth in me. Forgive me if my letter does not answer to that which you desired of me. If this letter is of no use to you, throw it into the basket or make of it that use which you find needful.
>
> In any case I was glad to enter into communion with you.
> Your loving comrade in authorship Lev Tolstoy."

Let us glance into the October diary. L. N. gives a picture of his life:

> "I have not written for a long time. During this time I was one day in a depressed state because of the guards, who were troubling the peasants. Here are Aunt Tanya and Mikh. Serg. and the two little Tanyas. There was an unpleasant, unexpected and unpleasant abuse for my letter about my having no property. There was an offensive feeling — and an astonishing thing: this was precisely that very thing which I needed — liberation from human glory. I feel a great step in this direction. More and more often I experience a kind of special rapture, joy of existence. Yes, only to free oneself, as I am freeing myself now, from temptations: anger, lust, wealth, in part voluptuousness, and chiefly human glory, and how suddenly the inner light is kindled. Especially joyful. During this time I am working over the children's *Circle of Reading*, introducing it into the subdivisions of the large one. The work requires great exertion, but it goes decently."

Here is that letter of L. N.'s, printed in the newspapers, which brought him unpleasantness and contributed, as he says, to "liberation from vanity," that is, for which he was mercilessly abused both in print, and in writing, and in person.

> "More than 20 years ago I, on certain personal considerations, renounced the possession of property. The immovable property belonging to me I made over to my heirs, just as though I had died. I renounced also the right of property in my writings, and what was written from the year 1881 became public domain.
>
> The only sums of which I still dispose are those moneys which I sometimes receive, chiefly from abroad, for those who have suffered from crop failure in certain localities, and those small sums which certain persons present to me in order that I should distribute them at my own discretion. I distribute them in the nearest district for widows, orphans, those burned out of their homes, and the like. Meanwhile such a disposal of mine of these small sums and the frivolous newspaper correspondence about me have led and are leading into error very many persons who more and more often and on an ever larger scale apply to me for monetary help. The grounds for the requests are most various: ranging from the most frivolous to the most well-founded and touching. The most usual are requests for monetary help to make it possible to complete an education, that is, to obtain a diploma; the most touching are requests for help to families left in a wretched condition. Having no possibility whatever of satisfying these demands, I tried to answer them with brief written refusals, expressing my regret at the impossibility of fulfilling the request, but for the most part received in reply new letters, irritated and reproachful. I tried not to answer, and again received irritated letters with reproaches that I do not answer. But these reproaches are not important; what is important is that hard feeling which the unpropertied must experience.
>
> In view of this I consider it needful now to ask all persons in need of monetary help to apply not to me, since I have at my disposal for this purpose absolutely no property whatever. I, less than anyone of men, can satisfy such requests, since, if I have really acted as I declare, that is, have ceased to own property, then I cannot help with money those who apply to me; but if I am deceiving people, saying that I have renounced property, while I continue to own it, then it is still less possible to expect help from such a man."

Whether it was because this letter was printed in *Novoye Vremya*, or simply from want of reason, but it called forth in the liberal press a whole heap of mockery and abuse.

Articles appeared bearing such titles as: "The Bankruptcy of Tolstoy" and the like.

It happened that I had occasion, not long before this, to be at Yasnaya Polyana and to experience for myself all the enchanting charm of that great, peaceful old age, radiating a general benevolence, and it grew bitter to me from this newspaper railing.

I tried to express my bitter feelings in a small note, which I adduce here from the draft preserved by me:

> "I have only just come from Yasnaya Polyana. How petty, how vulgar all the slanders and gossip strewn by the newspaper curs at the address of the great old man seem to me, when one feels close at hand the strength of his soul! How cruel, how shameless seem the malicious attempts to disturb the peace at the close of his most laborious life! You have forgotten, you petty, vile people, those soul-seizing raptures which he gave you by his works, you have trampled into the mire those pure thoughts and images by which he summoned you to the light out of your everyday worldly mire. But the people still remember his mighty voice, which responded first to the calamity in the years 1873 and 1891, and often in other cases summoned people to friendly help. The peasants will not forget his mediation, his defence of their human rights; the Moscow vagabonds will not forget the warm word he spoke to them in the year of the census.
>
> And many, many people, scattered over the whole world, to whom he opened the eyes and pointed out the path to the light, cannot forget him, if only because they have become one with him, have united into one spiritual, great family.
>
> Live then in peace, great old man, and may these petty little folk, content with the possibility of saying their stupid word, not trouble thy wise path."

Let us rest on a beautiful page of the October diary, where the prophet-denouncer comes forward with new force:

> "10 October. They say, and I too say, that printing has not contributed to the good of people; but this is too little. Nothing that increases the possibility of the action of people upon one another — railways, telegraphs, telephones, steamships, cannon, all military contrivances, explosive substances, and all that is called culture — has in our time in any way contributed to the good of people, but on the contrary. And it could not be otherwise among people the majority of whom live an irreligious, immoral life. If the majority is immoral, then the means of action will, obviously, contribute only to the spread of immorality. The means of action of culture can be beneficial only when the majority, even though a small majority, is religious-moral. The desirable relation of morality and culture is such that culture should develop only simultaneously with, and a little behind, the moral movement. But when culture outstrips it, as it does now, then this is a great calamity. Perhaps, and I even think, that it is a temporary calamity; that in consequence of the raising of culture above morality, although there must be temporary sufferings, the backwardness of morality will call forth sufferings, in consequence of which culture will be held back, the movement of morality will be hastened, and the right relation will be restored.
>
> I am occupied, and very diligently, with the children's *Circle of Reading*, and though slowly, I make progress. Today I thought that I had made 3 *Circles of Reading*. One, by departments, for children, another the same for grown-ups, a third without departments, but the old one corrected. And here again an inner struggle and a fall and a rise."

> "26 October. For a long time, three weeks, if not more, I was in a low state of spirit. There was no longer the joy of life and of the shadowy, joyful, needful, and important (for me) thoughts and feelings. During this time I have done nothing especially bad. I kept working over *The Circle of Reading*. Today I resolved to change much in it. For some 6 days I have resumed the lessons with the children. Not especially well; worse than I expected. Guseff has been arrested. There were visitors: Novikov, Liza. Today Olsufyev, Varya, Natasha. Today is the first day on which I have awoke spiritually, have risen to my former step, perhaps even a little higher. Today in bed — it was still dark — I awoke and began to think. So astonishingly well (for myself), that I came into rapture; but I did not write it down, and when I went, I began to recall it, and already recalled it far from what it was and not as it shone for me in the first minute."

The arrest of L. N.'s secretary, N. N. Guseff, greatly agitated L. N. He drove to him in prison, spoke with the police-officer, was with the governor, wrote letters, certificates, and, as always, repented and tormented himself that those near to him should suffer for him. The pretext for Guseff's arrest was comical. There was a denunciation against him, that he was conducting anti-government propaganda. They made a search of him and found a Russian edition of the pamphlet "The One Thing Needful," by L. N. As in this edition there were censorial cuts, Guseff procured the complete foreign edition and — inserted in pencil in the margins the omitted passages. Of course, these passages were the sharpest condemnations of the government of that day, and so there arose the legend that Guseff "abuses the tsar." For this he had to serve about a month in prison at Krapivna, until his release was obtained.

Nik. Nik. Guseff tells in his diary how the soul of Lev Nikolaevich was subjected to sufferings from the elements hostile to him. On the 7th of October Guseff writes in his diary:

> "Recently at dinner L. N. said: 'Why are the rich worse off than the poor? Because the poor satisfy their needs, while the rich satisfy their pleasures. The first is more joyful than the second.'
>
> And of this joy L. N. is deprived. Of late the life at Yasnaya Polyana, long ago hard for him, has become still harder. Yesterday, during his usual pre-dinner walk, driving past two muzhiks, one of whom was drunk, L. N. heard how the one who was drunk shouted to him:
>
> — Your Excellency! May God grant that you croak the sooner! — There followed a foul oath.
>
> 'I,' L. N. related, 'drove up to them, ask:
>
> — What for? what have I done to you?
>
> The one who had been cursing is silent, and the other began to say: "Why, we, nothing, we did not say anything."
>
> — But how is that, I say, you said nothing — why, I heard it.
>
> Then the one, the drunken one, who had been cursing, shouted:
>
> — Why have you fastened on me! What, will you shoot me from a gun, or what? Be off with you... — and again he let fly.'
>
> This embitterment of certain local peasants against L. N. is called forth chiefly by the fact that now, at the summons of S. A., two guards are living on the estate, who do all sorts of unpleasant things to the peasants. The guards were summoned by S. A. after some strange attack upon the Yasnaya Polyana gardener by peasant lads, who had supposedly even fired several shots from revolvers, several bullets having struck the walls of the threshing-barn.
>
> The lads pointed out by the gardener were arrested and put for one month under police custody."

N. N. Guseff adds that when he himself was afterward arrested, he met one of these lads in the Krapivna police office. He assured him that not a single shot had been fired on their side, that they had only climbed in after vegetables, but that the gardener had fired, who had also slandered them.

This occurrence was at the beginning of September. It gave the newspapers a pretext to print sensational articles under the title "The Shelling of L. N. Tolstoy's House," in which there was hardly a single word of truth.

The publications hostile to L. N. printed on this occasion articles in which they declared with malicious glee that here, you see, was a preacher of non-resistance, and yet when the matter touched his own hide, he himself shouted "help" and called the police.

"What strength of spirit one must have," adds N. N. Guseff, "to bear patiently this cross of universal embitterment, of slanders, of mockeries."

These guards long troubled the soul of L. N. In a letter to his wife of 10 November he says:

> "And with us all is well, in spite of the guards. It would be time to dismiss them, these two idle muzhiks.
>
> My Guard, and the real Guard of us all, is neither seen nor heard, but He guards us so that it could not be better."

The well-known writer, the art critic Vladimir Vasilyevich Stasov, died in this year. His friends resolved to honour his memory by compiling a volume dedicated to his name. One of these friends, the sculptor Ilya Yakovlevich Ginzburg, applied to Lev Nikolaevich — whom Stasov adored and called Lev the Great, and always wrote these words in capital letters — with a request to take part in the compilation of the volume. L. N. answered this letter thus, characterizing in it his relations to this friend and admirer of his:

> "Kind Ilya Yakovlevich. I feel my guilt before all the friends of Vl. Vas., and I ask them, especially Dm. Vas., to forgive me. I feel the clumsiness of old age, and besides, of late I am so absorbed, probably, in the last work that seems to me, as always when I am strongly occupied with something, very important. Moreover, to write about Vl. Vas. and my relations to him would be difficult for me, because of that misunderstanding which existed between us. This misunderstanding consisted in this, that Vl. Vas. loved and passionately prized in me that which I did not prize and could not prize in myself, and in his kindness forgave me that which I prized and prize in myself above all else, by which I lived and live.
>
> With any other person such a misunderstanding would have led, if not to hostility, then to coldness; but the dear, spontaneous, ardent, and at the same time childlike — in its clearness and simplicity — nature of Vlad. Vas. was such that I could not but yield to his influence and not love him, without any considerations as to the difference of our views.
>
> I always recall with tender emotion our good, friendly relations.
>
> If they find this letter worth printing in the volume, then give it.
> I press your hand. Lev Tolstoy."

On 29 November L. N. was exposed to great danger, of which he tells in his diary:

> "I fell from my horse, hurt my arm: now it is passing off. During this time there have been more and more good letters. I am not in the least carried away and do not wish for any spreading, as used to be the case before, but am simply glad that I could and can serve people at least with something. How strange it is that together with kindness comes humility, modesty. I have no need now, as before, to pretend to be humble. As soon as the work is within oneself, one at once sees that there is nothing not only to be proud of, but even to rejoice in. I rejoice only in this, that it is undeservedly good for me, and that the nearer to death, the better and better it is."

He ended the year still over the same work of *The Circle of Reading*, which he strove to bring to the possible perfection.
