# Volume IV, Chapter 18. 1910. The Departure

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Text:** P. I. Biryukov (1860–1931), *Biography of L. N. Tolstoy*, Volume IV. Public domain.
**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-18.md`](../ru/chapter-18.md).

---

The tragedy of L. N.'s life, which culminated in his departure from Yasnaya Polyana, has not yet entered the period of that historical impartiality in which events distant from us come to be reflected. Although the two chief actors, Lev Nikolaevich and Sofya Andreyevna, have both gone down to the grave, there are still many living witnesses of this tragedy, and these living witnesses are of differing opinions, hard to reconcile with one another. And I myself cannot consider myself impartial, and therefore cannot consider my exposition complete. A particular caution constrains me to refrain from any appraisal of certain facts, and I shall confine myself to a consecutive setting-forth of what is known to me, leaving the reader to draw his own conclusions and appraisals. There is no need to hurry with this. Time will sift out the truth.

Striving to be truthful, I must confess that, in spite of the fact that I have exhausted all the chief sources describing this event, it remains for me not wholly clear.

In the very fact of L. N.'s departure one may distinguish several motives: first, his attitude toward S. A.; secondly, his attitude toward the whole setting of his life; thirdly, a particular desire for peace and seclusion; fourthly, a desire for a new manner of life, in conformity with his conviction; and finally, fifthly, his attitude toward his own fame and renown and his desire to get away from it.

All these causes acted each one separately and all together in their interaction, and all of them determined the outcome.

We have already mentioned those facts in L. N.'s life which may be regarded as the forerunners of the departure or as trial runs of this future, final decision.

Such was his attempt at departure in 1884, before the birth of Alexandra Lvovna. L. N. then set out for Tula, but turned back on the way. The next attempt at departure, described by us in its place, was at the end of 1885, when L. N., exasperated by the luxurious gentleman's life of Moscow, wanted to flee from it. The third serious attempt at departure was in 1897, when L. N. wrote S. A. that remarkable letter, already cited by us above.

In this letter L. N. already definitely sets forth several motives for departure: disagreement in convictions, the surrounding setting, the desire for peace and seclusion, an indication of the Hindu custom of the departure of old men, the wish to lead his own manner of life, the freeing of himself from the guidance of his already grown-up children, and so on.

Interesting is the history of this letter, related by Nik. Leonidovich Obolensky, the husband of Maria Lvovna, in a letter to Tatyana Lvovna after L. N.'s death:

> "Here is how the matter was, — writes Nik. Leon. — At Gaspra one time, when Masha had been left alone with papa in his room, during the most grievous period of his illness, he, thinking he would die, bade Masha, when she should arrive at Yasnaya, to go to his study and there to take out, from one of the armchairs upholstered in oilcloth, from under it, from the lining, two papers, two white sealed envelopes addressed to no one and without any inscription upon them, to take them out and to write on these envelopes the following (this I remember word for word, that is, I then and there wrote it all down and learned it by heart) — on one: 'To be opened fifty years after my death, if the episode of my autobiography is of interest to anyone.' And on the other: 'All that is written here about Seryozha was written by me in a bad moment. He has by all his subsequent conduct and attitude toward me fully redeemed all his guilt before me.' When he had said this to Masha, she asked him: 'Well — these papers, are they to be given to Chertkov?' — He answered: 'Why to Chertkov? Leave them with you.' Nothing more was said — he was very weak then, after all. Masha there and then wrote this down from his words in his little notebook and afterward tore out the leaf, which I at first kept with me, and then, having learned it, destroyed. Then papa recovered, we all gathered in the summer at Yasnaya. We lived in the wing both that summer and the beginning of the winter. This was, accordingly, in 1902. In October or November papa came to us in the wing and asked Masha: 'And where are those little papers which you took out of the armchair?' Masha says: 'I did not touch them — I thought that, since you had recovered, it had not been needful to do it.' He says: 'Well, and excellent, let them remain there.' And so the matter ended.
>
> It seems to me that he had looked for them before this, but had not found them, and had thought that Masha had taken them out; but to find them was difficult, because Masha and I examined that armchair (it was marked) and could not see even any signs that anything was hidden there, so deeply had he stuffed them in under the lower lining. But still, after these conversations they were there for a long time, because Masha told me more than once that, when she would be in his study alone with him, he would sometimes wink at her and laugh, indicating the armchair with his eyes. But say anything more he did not. Then we left Yasnaya, traveled abroad, and even forgot about these little papers. Then Masha died. Then I, without asking papa about it, told Sasha of them, so that besides me someone else too should know of it, but she, no doubt, forgot. In the spring of 1907 I was at Yasnaya in the month of May; we were all dining, and mama began to say at dinner that on the morrow the upholsterer would be re-covering the furniture in the study. I then remembered the papers and looked at papa; he, as it seemed to me, looked at me. From this I understood that he remembered the papers, and that, no doubt, they were still in the armchair. And after dinner, when he and I were left alone, he says: 'I must speak with you.' I say: 'About the papers in the armchair?' — 'Yes, what a memory you have! Do this: tomorrow, very early in the morning, when everyone is still asleep, take out these papers and keep them.' — In the morning I went early to him in the study. But he met me at the door and was already carrying in his hands only one envelope, and a gray one, not a white one, and on it was written 'to be given after my death to Countess S. A. T.' or something of the sort. This he told me to take and to keep with me for the time being. Which I did, until I gave it to Misha to be passed on to mama now."

When, after L. N.'s death, this gray packet was handed over to S. A., she took from it two letters: having read one, she at once tore it up; the other letter was precisely about his departure, contemplated in 1907."

As we have stated before, we suppose that one of the causes of the postponement of the departure was the Doukhobor migration, in which L. N. took such active, important, and useful part.

Then again a whole series of external events, requiring his participation, pushed back the decision of this question, which concerned him personally.

Again, a year of famine set in; the winter of 1898–99 called forth anew public activity, which, unfortunately, did not proceed so successfully as at the beginning of the nineties. Now the local administration set up every kind of obstacle.

Then there took place L. N.'s excommunication from the church, and, finally, his Crimean illness. No sooner had he managed to recover from his illness, than the war began, and then the unrest, and finally the revolutionary years of the so-called liberation movement.

L. N.'s decision to depart had been put off by him, but not forgotten. A new impetus to its fulfillment was that nervously irritated atmosphere which surrounded L. N. in the last months of his life at Yasnaya Polyana. On his side the decision had ripened long ago. His remaining at Yasnaya was hanging by a thread — by his pity, by his fear of grieving those close to him and of acting selfishly, for his own benefit.

In July 1908 L. N. lived through one of those spiritual crises in connection with the conditions of family life, which not infrequently ended for him in a serious illness. So it was this time too: directly after this he fell ill, and was for some time at the point of death.

Here are several extracts from his diary, written by him in the days preceding the illness:

> "If I were to hear about myself from the side — about a man living in luxury, taking away everything he can from the peasants, putting them in prison, and confessing and preaching Christianity, and giving away five-kopeck pieces, and hiding for all his vile doings behind a kind wife — I should not hesitate to call him a scoundrel. And this is just what is needed for me, that I should free myself from human glory and live for the soul...
>
> Everything is just as tormenting as before. Life here, at Yasnaya Polyana, is utterly poisoned. Wherever I go out — shame and suffering...
>
> One thing is ever more and more tormenting: the untruth of mad luxury amid the unbecoming poverty, the need, amid which I live. Everything is becoming worse and worse. Heavier and heavier. I cannot forget, cannot not-see...
>
> There came into my mind doubts whether I do well in keeping silent, and whether it would not be better for me to go away, to hide. I do not do this preeminently because this is for myself, in order to escape from a life poisoned on all sides. And I believe that this very bearing of this life is what is needed for me...
>
> I can endure this no longer, I cannot, I must free myself from this tormenting position. To live so is impossible. I, at least, cannot live so, cannot and will not...
>
> Help me, Lord. Again I wish to go away. And I do not resolve to. But I do not refuse either. The chief thing: is it for myself that I shall do it, if I go away. That I am not doing it for myself by remaining — that I know..."

V. G. Chertkov writes in his notes on L. N.'s departure:

> "I remember how, returning one day in those days from a solitary walk in the woods, L. N. — with that joyously-inspired expression which in the last years so often lit up his face — met me with the words:
>
> — And I have thought much, and very well. And it has become so clear to me that, when one stands at a crossroads and does not know how to act, then one ought always to give preference to that decision in which there is more self-renunciation."

A very hard time L. N. lived through in 1909, when, having received an invitation to a congress at Stockholm, he wished to go, and S. A. opposed it. We have mentioned this in its place. L. N. courageously lived through this trial too.

Finally, a fresh onslaught upon L. N. from Sofya Andreyevna on account of the diaries compelled him to write her on 14 July a letter, already cited by us in Chapter XVI.

In this letter L. N. clearly sets forth the conditions on which he can remain at Yasnaya, and those on which he will have to go.

And, having written this letter, he still resolved to endure. But new circumstances continued to burden his life.

L. N.'s diary of that time, especially the little pocket diary, abounds in notes pointing to the troubled state of his soul under the influence of surrounding events; we adduce some of them:

> "6 August. I think of going away. To leave a letter, and I am afraid, although I think it would be better."

> "15 August. On the way to Kochety I thought that if only these alarms and demands begin again, then I shall go away with Sasha.
>
> And so I said."

> "20 August. I rode on horseback, and the sight of this kingdom of lordship so torments me that I am thinking of running away, of hiding."

> "25 August. V. M. writes of a meeting with Almedingen. This irritates Sasha. To me it is all the same, but it worsens my feeling toward her. Ah, if only one could go away, gently, but firmly."

> "12 September. Received a letter from Ch., underlining the advice of all to firmness, and my own decision. I do not know whether I shall hold out."

> "17 September. How comical is the opposition in which I live, in which, without false modesty, I bear and utter the most important, significant thoughts, and side by side with this, the struggle and participation in feminine caprices, to which I devote the greater part of my time. I feel myself in the matter of moral perfecting wholly a boy, a pupil, and a pupil who is a poor one and little zealous."

> "29 September... Today for the first time I saw the possibility of subduing her by goodness, by love. Ah, if only..."

> "16 October. Very, very hard. Help, God. I said that I would give and do give no promises, but I shall do all I can in order not to grieve her. The departure of tomorrow I shall scarcely carry out. And yet it is needful,"

> "21 October. I bear my trial very heavily. At night I thought of departing."

> "25 October. The same heavy feeling. Suspicions, spying, and a sinful wish that she should give me cause to go away. So bad am I. And then I think of going away, and the general situation, and pity, and again I cannot."

> "26 October. M. A. (Marya Alexandrovna Schmidt) bids me not to go away, and my conscience too does not let me. To bear with her, to bear, not altering the outward position, but working upon the inward. Help, Lord."

> "27–28 October. There has taken place that jolt which compelled me to undertake it. And here I am at Optina, on the evening of the 28th."

A week before the carrying-out of this decision Lev Nikolaevich had a detailed conversation about it with his friend, the peasant Mikhail Petrovich Novikov, in which conversation he expressed to him his firm intention of going away in the very near future and, taking leave, said: "We shall see each other soon."

Here are some extracts from this remarkable conversation, related by M. P. Novikov himself.

> "Questioning me about my family, about how the peasants regard my departure from the old faith and my children, not baptized into any faith, Lev Nikolaevich unexpectedly asked:
>
> — And I have never been at your place in the village?"

I said that "you have promised several times to visit me, but have forgotten." Lev Nikolaevich laughed and said:

— Well, and very good, now I am free and at any time can fulfill my promise.

I took this for a joke and said:

— And do you remember, L. N., two years ago you wrote in answer to my call: "even if I wished to, all the same I could not come to you." For me, I say, it has so remained incomprehensible why you could not come.

— At that time, — L. N. interrupted me jestingly, — the time was a strict one, but now there is a constitution, I have shared things out with my own, or, as you say it, — he asked me, — it seems, have departed from the family. Now I am superfluous here, as are your old men too when they live to my years, and therefore wholly free.

Noticing that I took this for a joke and listened to him incredulously, Lev Nikolaevich passed to a serious tone and spoke again:

— Yes, yes, believe me, I am speaking with you frankly, I shall not die in this house. I have decided to go away to an unknown place where no one would know me. And perhaps I shall really come to die in your hut. Only I know beforehand, — he continued, — you will begin to scold me, for old men are nowhere loved. I have seen this, he says, in your peasant families, and I have indeed become just as helpless and useless, — he uttered in a sunken voice.

It cost me great efforts not to break into tears at these words, and to Lev Nikolaevich, evidently, this confession was hard. We were long silent; at last Lev Nikolaevich said:

— And you, of course, will spend the night with us, as always.

I said that I was ashamed to trouble others, compelling them to be concerned about me, but that otherwise I did not know what to do, since I was afraid to go alone to the station in the middle of the night.

— Well, and very good, — he said, — and do you think that you are spending the night at my house. When I drop in on you some time and likewise spend the night, we shall be quits...

Seeing me off and taking leave of me, as usual, in the evening, L. N. would long not release my hand, as if foreseeing that we were seeing each other for the last time, and several times repeated:

— We shall see each other soon... God grant that we shall see each other soon.

I was already lying in bed and getting ready to fall asleep when I heard close beside me light steps. In the half-gloom I again saw him and was ready to take him for a phantom, so light and soundless were his movements.

Seeing that I was reaching out my hand to turn up the lamp brighter, L. N. restrained me and, sitting down beside me on the bed, said softly and in broken phrases:

— Don't, it is better so, I have come to you for a minute, I am glad that you are not asleep. I did not wish to speak to you about myself, but I have only just felt that I was not right in saying to you why I both then and always could not visit you. I have, after all, never hidden from you that I am seething in this house as in hell, and have always thought and wished to go away somewhere into the forest, into a watchman's hut, or to the village to a bachelor peasant, where we might help one another, but God did not give me the strength to break with the family; my weakness, perhaps, my sin, but I could not, for my own personal pleasure, force others to suffer, even those of my own family...

— But then, in order to see your friends, — I said, — you did not need to leave the family, for it is only for a time...

— That is just the trouble, — he interrupted me, — that here too they wished to dispose of my time in their own way.

— You will forgive me, — after a moment's pause he said bitterly, — I have been chattering on to you, but I so much wished that you should understand me in your soul and not think ill of me. Two more words: I told you that I am now free, and you may believe that I am not joking, we shall certainly see each other soon. At your place, at your place, in your hut, — he added hurriedly, noticing my perplexity. — I have indeed departed from the family, only in soul, without a formal sentence, as among you, — he jested. — For myself alone I would not have done it, could not have done it, but now I see that for those of my family too it will be better, there will be less dispute, less sin, on my account.

A few days after this conversation, on 24 October, L. N. wrote Novikov:

> "Mikhail Petrovich, in connection with what I said to you before your departure, I turn to you with yet the following request: if it should really come about that I should arrive at your place, would you not be able to find for me in your village even the very smallest, but separate, warm hut, in such a way that I should put you and your family out for the very briefest time? I also inform you that, if it should be necessary for me to telegraph you, I should telegraph not in my own name, but in that of T. Nikolaev. I shall await your reply, and clasp your hand in friendship.
>
> Bear in mind that all this should be known to you alone."

This letter was long on the way, lay at the post office, and when Novikov received it, L. N. was already lying ill at Astapovo, and there was received the reply of Novikov, who of course promised to do everything as Lev Nikolaevich wished.

The entries of the last days of L. N.'s diary give us an exact picture of the state of his soul:

> "25 October. Got up very early, but all the same did nothing; walked to the school and to Prokofy, talked with his son, given over for a soldier. A good lad, promised not to drink. Then a little about socialism. Drove to the school with Almedingen, and afterward with Dušan, far. In the evening read Montaigne. Seryozha arrived. He is agreeable to me. S. A. is still just as troubled."

> "26. Had a dream, Grushenka, a novel, as it were, by N. N. Strakhov. A wonderful subject. Wrote a letter to Ch. Made a note for 'On Socialism.' Wrote to Chukovsky 'On Capital Punishment.' Drove with Dušan to M. A. Andrei arrived. It is very hard for me in this house of the mad. I lie down."

> "27. Got up very early. All night I had bad dreams. Walked well. At home a letter. Worked a little on the letter to N. 'On Soc.,' but there is no mental energy. Drove with Dušan. Dinner. Reading of Syutaev. A beautiful letter from the Little Russian to Ch. Corrected Chukovsky. There is nothing to set down. Bad, it seems, but in essence good. The heaviness of relations is growing."

> "28. Lay down at half past eleven. Slept until the third hour. Awoke and again, as on the previous nights, heard the opening of doors and steps. On the previous nights I had not looked at my door, but tonight I glanced and saw, in the cracks, a bright light in the study and a rustling. It is S. A. searching out something, probably reading. The day before she had asked, demanded, that I should not lock the doors. Both her doors are open, so that the slightest movement of mine is audible to her. By day and by night all my movements, my words, must be known to her and be under her control. Again steps, the cautious opening of the door, and she passes through. I do not know why, this called forth in me an irrepressible aversion, indignation. I wished to fall asleep, I cannot, I tossed about for about an hour, lit a candle, and sat up. The door opens, and S. A. enters, asking 'about my health' and marveling at the light by me, which she had seen by me. Aversion and indignation grow. I am stifling, I count the pulse: 97. I cannot lie still, and suddenly I take the final decision to go away. I write her a letter, I begin to pack, only the most necessary, only to get away. I wake D., then Sasha, they help me to pack. I tremble at the thought that she will hear, that there will be a scene, hysterics, and that thereafter without a scene there will be no getting away. By the sixth hour everything is somehow packed, I go to the stable, give orders to harness. Duš., S., V. finish the packing. Night — pitch black, I lose the little path to the wing, fall into the thicket, prick myself, knock against the trees, fall, lose my cap, do not find it, hardly get clear, go home, take a cap, and with a little lantern make my way to the stable, give orders to harness, S., D., V. come up. I tremble, expecting pursuit. But here we drive off. At Shchekino we wait an hour, and every minute I expect her appearance; but here we are sitting in the carriage, we start, the fear passes off, and pity for her rises up, but not the doubt whether I have done what was needful. Perhaps I am mistaken, justifying myself, but it seems that I was saving myself — not Lev Nikolaevich, but saving that which sometimes, even if only a little, is in me. We have reached Optina. I am well, although I have not slept and have eaten almost nothing. The journey from Gorbachevo in a third-class carriage packed with working folk is very instructive and good, although I took it in but weakly. It is now eight o'clock, we are at Optina."

Here is the letter left by L. N. for Sofya Andreyevna:

4 o'clock in the morning. 28 October 1910.

> "My departure will grieve you, I regret this, but understand and believe that I could not act otherwise. My position in the house is becoming, has become, unendurable. Besides everything else, I can no longer live in those conditions of luxury in which I have lived, and I am doing what old men of my age usually do — they go away from worldly life in order to live in seclusion and quiet the last days of their life.
>
> Please, understand this and do not come after me, even if you should learn where I am. Such a coming on your part would only worsen your position and mine, but would not alter my decision.
>
> I thank you for your honest 48 years of life with me and beg you to forgive me for everything in which I was at fault before you, just as I, with all my soul, forgive you for everything in which you might have been at fault before me. I advise you to come to terms with that new position in which my departure places you, and not to bear any unkind feeling against me. If you should wish to communicate anything to me, then pass it on through Sasha — she will know where I am and will forward me what is needed. As to where I am, she cannot say, because I have made her promise not to tell this to anyone.
> Lev Tolstoy.
>
> To gather my things and my manuscripts I have charged Sasha."

And so, Lev Nikolaevich left.

A description of the further journey we borrow from the account of Sergei Lvovich (from the words of Dušan Petrovich Makovitsky), communicated by Sergei Lvovich at a gathering of friends which took place at his house in November 1910.

> "When father took his seat in the carriage, he grew calm, felt himself well and even vigorous. When they arrived at Gorbachevo, the train from there ran only with one third-class carriage, packed full. L. N. looked for a place. Then some official came along; father talked with him for a long time; the official rejoiced at the chance to express his little-interesting views and tired father out. There was heavy smoke, and father went out onto the platform to escape it. Then they arrived at Optina Pustyn, which is two or three versts from the station. Father entered the guest-house. The guest-master, a very good-natured monk, received him very well. In the evening he wished to go to the elder Joseph, who interested him, as it seems to me, not from the point of view of religion, but as an old man who had gone away into a hermitage and was living in seclusion."

From Optina, on his arrival, L. N. wrote to Alexandra Lvovna:

> "We have arrived safely, Sasha dear — ah, if only at your end things were not very unsafely. It is now half past seven. We shall spend the night and tomorrow we go, if I live, to Shamordino. I try to be calm, and I must confess that I am experiencing the same uneasiness which I always experience when awaiting anything heavy, but I am not experiencing that shame, that awkwardness, that unfreedom which I always experienced at home. We had to travel from Gorbachevo in the third class — it was uncomfortable, but very inwardly pleasant and instructive. I ate well both on the way and at Belyov; we are now going to drink tea and to sleep, to try to sleep. I am almost not tired, even less than usual. As to you, I decide nothing until I receive news from you. Write to Shamordino and send telegrams there too, if there is anything urgent. Tell Bate (*) to write, and that I read the place marked in his article, but in haste, and should like to read it over — let him send it. To Varya say that I thank her, as always, for her love for you, and beg her and hope that she will take care of you and check you in your impulses. Please, my dear, few words, but mild and firm."
>
> (* To V. G. Chertkov. *)

On the next morning, — Sergei Lvovich continues his account, — the young Sergeenko arrived, who related that he had been at Yasnaya and that nothing particular had happened there. Dušan Petrovich asserts that, thanks to Sergeenko's influence, father did not go to Joseph, but it seems to me that he simply changed his mind. He even went to the hermitage, met a constable, walked through the wood, but did not enter the hermitage. This was on the morning of the 29th. Then he went to Maria Nikolaevna at Shamordino. The road was bad, the carriage too, an icy rain was falling, and father did not feel well. With the coachman he spoke of the harm of smoking and of wine. At Shamordino, besides Maria Nikolaevna, there was her niece, Obolenskaya. Father felt himself in a kindred atmosphere and grew merry. He told Maria Nikolaevna that he would have remained to live in the hermitage, were one not made to go to church.

L. N. records in his diary about that day thus:

> "29. Slept uneasily; in the morning Alyosha Sergeenko. I, not understanding, met him cheerfully. But the news he brought is terrible. S. A., having read the letter, cried out and ran to the pond. Sasha and Vanya ran after her and dragged her out. Andrei arrived. They guessed where I was, and S. A. begged A. at all costs to find me. And I now, the evening of the 29th, await the arrival of A. A letter from Sasha. She advises not to lose heart. She has sent for a psychiatrist and awaits the arrival of Sergei and Tanya. It was very hard for me all day, and physically I am weak too. Walked, yesterday inscribed the note in *Rech* on capital punishment. Drove to Shamordino. The most consoling — joyful — impression from Mashenka, despite her account of the 'enemy,' and of dear Lizanka. Both understand my position and sympathize with it. I drove along and kept thinking of a way out of my and her position, and could think of no way; and yet there will be one, whether one will it or not, there will be, and not the one which one foresees. Yes, think only of not sinning. And what will be, will be. That is not my affair. Got from Mashenka the *Circle of Reading* and, reading the 28th, was struck directly by an answer to my position: the trial is needed for me, is beneficial for me. I am now going to bed. Help, Lord. A good letter from Ch."

Sergeenko left, and L. N. writes by him to Alexandra Lvovna:

9 October 1910. Optina Pustyn.

> "Sergeenko will tell you all about me, dear friend Sasha. It is hard. I cannot but feel a great heaviness. The chief thing is, not to sin, in this is the labor. Of course, I have sinned and shall sin, but if only as little as possible. This is, chiefly, before all else, what I wish for you. The more so since I know that there has fallen to you a strange task, beyond your strength, beyond your youth. I have decided nothing and do not wish to decide. I try to do only that which I cannot but do, and not to do that which I might not do. From the letter to Ch. you will see how I, not so much regard, as feel. I have great hopes of the good influence of Tanya and Seryozha. The chief thing is that they should understand and try to instill in her that for me, with this spying, this eavesdropping, these unceasing reproaches, this disposing of me at her own caprice, this unceasing control, this affected hatred of the man closest and most necessary to me, with this open hatred of me and this pretense of love, that such a life is not pleasant to me, but simply impossible; that if anyone is to drown himself, it is in no wise she but I; that I desire one thing — freedom from her, from this falsehood, pretense, and malice with which her whole being is permeated. Of course, this they cannot instill in her, but they can instill in her that all her actions in regard to me not only do not express love, but as if they had the evident aim of killing me, which she will achieve, since I hope that in the third fit, which threatens me, I shall deliver both her and myself from this terrible position in which we have lived and to which I do not wish to return.
>
> You see, dear, what a bad man I am. I do not conceal from you. I do not yet send for you, but I shall send for you as soon as it shall be possible, and very soon. Write how you are. I kiss you.
>
> 29. We go to Shamordino."

On the next day L. N. continues his diary:

> "30 October. If I live. I am alive, but not quite. Very weak, drowsy, and this is a bad sign. Read the Novosyolov philosophical library, very interesting about socialism. My article on socialism is lost. A pity. No, not a pity. Sasha arrived. I rejoiced greatly. But it is hard too. Letters from the sons. A letter from Sergei — good, businesslike, gentle, and kind. Went in the morning to hire a hut at Shamordino. Very tired. Wrote a letter to S. A."

Here is that letter:

30 October 1910.

> "Our meeting and the more so my return now are wholly impossible. For you it would be, as everyone says, in the highest degree harmful, and for me it would be terrible, since now my position, in consequence of your agitation, irritation, morbid state, would become, if this is only possible, — still worse. I advise you to come to terms with what has happened, to settle yourself in your new position for the time being, and, chiefly, to seek treatment.
>
> If you not so much love me, as only not hate me, then you must enter at least a little into my position. And if you do this, then you will not only not condemn me, but will try to help me to find that peace, the possibility of some kind of human life, will help me by an effort over yourself, and you yourself will not now desire my return. Your present frame of mind, your wish and attempt at suicide, more than anything else show your loss of power over yourself, and make for me now my return inconceivable. To deliver from the sufferings they undergo all the people close to you, me, and, chiefly, yourself, no one can, save you yourself. Try to direct all your energy not toward having everything be that which you wish for — now, my return — but toward pacifying yourself, your soul, and you will receive what you wish.
>
> I have spent two days at Shamordino and Optina, and I am going. I shall send a letter on the way. I do not say where I am going, because I consider it necessary, both for you and for myself, that we be separated. Do not think that I have gone away because I do not love you; I love you and pity you with all my soul, but I cannot act otherwise than I am acting. Your letter — I know that it is written sincerely, but you have not the power to perform that which you would wish. And the matter is not in the fulfillment of any desires, demands, of mine, but only in your equilibrium, your calm, reasonable attitude toward life. And while this is not, life with you for me is inconceivable. To return to you, when you are in such a state, would be for me to renounce life. And I do not consider that I have the right to do this. Farewell, dear Sonya, God help you. Life is not a jest, and to cast it away by our own will we have no right, and to measure it by the length of time is also very unreasonable. Perhaps the months which it remains to us to live are more important than all the years lived, and one must live them well.
> L. T."

On the next day, before departure, he writes a touching letter to his elder children:

4 o'clock in the morning, Shamordino, 31 October 1910.

> "I thank you very much, dear friends — true friends — Seryozha and Tanya. Your letter, Seryozha, was especially joyful for me: short, clear, and substantial, and, chiefly, kind. I cannot but fear everything, and cannot free myself from responsibility, but I had not the strength to act otherwise. I wrote to Sasha through Chertkov about what I beg to be communicated to you, the children. Read this. I wrote what I felt and feel — that I cannot act otherwise. I write to her — mama. She will show it to you — I also wrote, thinking it over, and all that I could. We are leaving now, we do not yet know where. Communication will always be through Chertkov.
>
> Farewell, thanks to you, dear children, and forgive me that I am, after all, the cause of your suffering. Especially you, dear darling Tanechka. Well, that is all. I hasten to get away so that, what I fear, mama may not catch me here. A meeting with her now would be terrible. Well, farewell.
> L. T."

L. N.'s stay at Shamordino is excellently described by Elizaveta Valeryanovna Obolenskaya, the daughter of Maria Nikolaevna, the sister of L. N. She was staying with her mother at the time when L. N. arrived there with Dušan. Here is what she relates in a letter to one of her relatives:

> "On 29 October in the daytime a nun who had arrived from Optina Pustyn told us that she had seen Lev Nikolaevich there and that he would today be at our place. This news agitated us very much. The fact that he had taken it into his head to come in autumn, in bad weather, by a bad road, seemed very strange. He came in to us into the front room about the sixth hour and seemed to me very pitiful: he was muffled in his brown bashlyk, his grizzled little beard stuck out forward somehow piteously. The nun who had escorted him from the guest-house to mamasha said that, as he walked, he seemed very weak, even staggered. Mamasha met him with the words: 'Lyovochka, I am very glad to see you, but I fear that this means that things are not well at home with you.' He said: 'at home it is terrible,' and his voice trembled and broke off in tears. As he related how Sof. Andr. had thrown herself into the pond, and about that impossible life which he had been leading of late at Yasnaya, he was so agitated, was so often broken off by tears, that I offered him water, but he refused. We both wept, listening to him. He was terribly pitiful. Speaking of his last fit (a swoon accompanied by convulsions), he said: 'one more fit, and surely there will be death: a pleasant death, because complete unconsciousness, but I should have liked to die in my memory.' And again he wept. The last impetus which had compelled him to go away from Yasnaya were Sof. Andr.'s nightly visits and the deceit accompanying them. Each night she would come in to rummage in his papers on the writing-table, and if she noticed that he was not asleep, then she would come into his bedroom, ask about his health, and show a tender care for him. 'She pretended, I too had to pretend that I believed her, and this was terrible.' — When mamasha uttered the thought that Sof. Andr. was ill, he thought a little and said: 'yes, yes, of course; but what was I to do; one would have had to use force, and this I could not, and so I went away; and I want now to take advantage of this in order to alter wholly my life.' Little by little he grew calm, and said that Dušan and Sergeenko were at the guest-house. We sent for them. Sergeenko did not even take off his outer clothing, and, taking leave of Lev Nik., he asked him: 'so you are well off here?' To which he answered: 'yes, very.' Mamasha began to question him about Optina Pustyn and why he had not been to the elder Joseph. Optina pleased him very much (he had, after all, been there more than once before), and he said: 'I would gladly remain to live there and would bear the heaviest obediences, only let them not make me go to church and cross myself, because on my part this would be a falsehood. To the elder I did not go, because I thought that he would not receive an excommunicated man.' He dined with great appetite, and the rest of the evening spoke calmly of extraneous subjects: questioned mamasha about the monastery; as always, with especial tenderness spoke of those who had refused military service; said that he was thinking of staying for a while at Shamordino; with interest heard out that near Optina one can rent a separate little house, and that many so arrange things. He went off to the guest-house fairly early; I went to see him off, and on the way he asked me how much one had to pay a day for two rooms, if one stayed here. As I was leaving, he asked me to send him coffee at nine o'clock, and said: 'I shall take my customary walk, then occupy myself a little, and come to you.'
>
> On the next day (the 30th), at 10 o'clock in the morning, I went to him. He was lying on the divan and reading; said that he did not feel well, that nothing hurt him, but he was weak and could not occupy himself. He related that he had walked to the village to look whether he could rent an *izba* there, but he had as yet found nothing suitable. I said to him: 'Uncle, dear, it will be hard for you to live in an *izba*, and they will not leave you here either (I said this to him because Sergeenko had informed him that they were on his trail, and that Sof. Andr. had begged Andryusha at all costs to find him and bring him back, so that he was expecting that Andryusha would arrive on the 30th). To this he answered: 'You have so consoled me, that I am calmed, and now I am still more confirmed in not returning to Yasnaya; if I cannot remain here, I shall go farther, and to Andryusha I shall say so: I cannot return in the state in which I am now, the return would be equivalent to death; one more scene — and the end.' He was very agitated as he spoke; I too was agitated. I asked him what one might expect from Andryusha, in what disposition he was. He said: 'just now in a good one.' Shortly afterward I left, and he asked me to send a chair from mamasha. At about 2 I went to him again; I found him in the same physically depressed state, but in spirit much calmer: he talked of the *Circle of Reading*, of the little books which he had taken from mamasha, asked what newspapers we received, and said: 'when I come to dinner I shall read them.' I went next door to Dušan's room to ask him about Lev Nik.'s health. According to his words, such a state of drowsiness, of depression, comes upon Lev Nik. before a swoon; he had no fever, but his pulse was 94. At 4 o'clock both came to us for dinner; Lev. Nik. looked well, was fairly vigorous and animated, ate with appetite. At dinner he questioned much about the monastery, said that he would inspect everything: the orphanage, the printing house; he did not abandon the thought that it would be possible to find some kind of lodging in the village. At 6 o'clock they went back to themselves, and to the question — would he come to drink tea — he answered: 'as I shall feel.'
>
> No sooner had he gone than Sasha came; she had missed them, since from mamasha's to the guest-house there are two roads. Sasha began to relate about Yasnaya, and I hurried her to return to the guest-house, because I feared that Lev. Nik., learning that she had arrived, would be agitated. And indeed, she had not yet had time to leave when he came with Dušan and Varv. Mikh. I do not to this day know whether he had summoned Sasha, whether he had been awaiting her, or whether she had come of herself. But, recalling his agitated, frightened, almost lost face, when he came in to us, I think that their arrival was for him a surprise, and a surprise not joyful. In any case, from that moment his calm came to an end. In the next two hours, which he sat with us, he spoke little. He made Sasha relate in detail how Sof. Andr. had thrown herself into the pond, read the letters of his sons, of Tanya and of Sof. Andr., which Sasha had brought him. There and then she and Varv. Mikh. began to say that it was necessary to leave Shamordino as soon as possible, because Sof. Andr. might arrive, who had said: 'now I shall not be so stupid; I shall not take my eyes off him, I shall sleep at his door.' They began to lay plans where to go; they spoke of the south, of the Caucasus, of Bessarabia. Lev. Nik. listened to all this in silence, then said: 'I do not like all this...'
>
> Over tea Sasha rightly noticed that he was troubled, since she said to him: 'do not lose heart, papa dear, all is well.' To which he answered: 'no, it is not well'; and, having thought, repeated again: 'it is not well.' Soon he went off with Dušan, saying that he needed to sit in quiet and to think it all over.
>
> When we came to the guest-house, Lev Nik. was sitting in his room and writing a letter to Sof. Andr., and Dušan in his own over a map. Sasha wished to speak alone with father; they sat together rather a long time; she came out from him pensive and said: 'it seems to me that papa already regrets that he has gone away.' To the question why she so thought she answered nothing definite, but I think that here too she did not understand his mood. A little later Lev Nik. came to us in the room and, seeing Dušan over the map, said: 'only not to any colony, not to any acquaintances, but simply to an *izba* with the muzhiks.' Then, having spoken a little about the trains, he said: 'I am very tired now, I want to sleep; the morning is wiser than the evening, tomorrow we shall see.' On that I took leave of him.
>
> About 5 o'clock in the morning (on the next day) I heard the bell at our porch. The first thought was: Lev. Nik. has fallen ill. It seemed to me wholly impossible that all these agitations should not have told upon him. I went out into the front room, and I see — Dušan is standing with a lantern and says: 'we are leaving at once.' What? why, where? — After I had left, Lev Nik. went to sleep, slept not long; at three o'clock he awoke and began to wake everyone and to hurry them, so as to catch the 8-o'clock train, which runs south. He was very nervous and agitated. Dušan came to us to ask where to hire a coachman. I told him to go to the stable yard, wake the workman, and send him to the village for horses, and to bid the monastery coachman harness the *proletka* and come for mamasha. The guest-house was rather far off, she could not walk there; and I knew that she would want to take leave of him. I was sorry to wake mamasha at once: she had been agitated, was tired, and had fallen asleep late, and by my reckoning about an hour would pass before Dušan would wake the workman, before he would reach the village, before the coachman would come. And in this I was not mistaken, for when mamasha and I drove up to the guest-house, the coachman was not yet there, but neither were Lev Nik. and Dušan, only Sasha with Var. Mikh. I did not know that Sasha had kept her own coachman, with whom she had driven from the station, and it was with him that Lev Nik. had left. Sasha said that he was in a great hurry, was agitated, was afraid to be late. He left us a very tender, affectionate note, thanking us for our part in his 'trial,' and explained his hurried departure by the fact that he was afraid that 'Sofya Andreyevna would catch him here.'

Here is this letter:

1910, Oct. 31, 4 o'clock in the morning.

> "Dear friends Mashenka and Lizanka. Do not be surprised, and do not condemn me, that we are leaving without taking proper leave of you. I cannot express to you both, especially to you, dear Mashenka, my gratitude for your love and for your part in my 'trial.' I do not remember, having always loved you, having experienced toward you such tenderness as I have felt these days and with which I am leaving."

Already after L. N.'s death, several months later, his sister Marya Nikolaevna wrote to S. A.:

> "When Lyovochka came to me, he was at first very oppressed, and when he began to relate to me how you had thrown yourself into the pond, he sobbed; I could not see him without tears; but about himself he told me nothing, he only said that he had come here for a long stay, was thinking of renting an *izba* from a peasant and of living here. It seems to me that he wished for seclusion, that Yasnaya Polyana life weighed upon him (he told me this the last time I was at your place) and the whole setting, contrary to his convictions; he simply wished to settle in to his own taste and to live in seclusion, where no one would disturb him. So I understood from his words.
>
> Up to Sasha's arrival he was not intending to go anywhere, but was preparing to go to Optina Pustyn and was determined to speak with the elder. But Sasha by her arrival turned everything upside down on the next day. When he went off that day in the evening to spend the night at the guest-house, he did not even think of leaving, but said to me: 'goodbye, we shall see each other tomorrow.'
>
> What then was my astonishment and despair on the next day, when at 5 o'clock in the morning (still dark) I was awakened and told that he was leaving. I at once got up, dressed, gave orders for a horse to be brought, drove to the guest-house, but he had already left, and so I never saw him."

We borrow the description of the last evening and night spent at Shamordino from the notes of Alexandra Lvovna. Here is what she relates of this:

> "We were sitting at the table and looking at the open map; the air-vent was open, I wished to shut it.
>
> — Leave it, — said father, — it is hot. What is it you are looking at?
>
> — The map, — said Dušan Petrovich, — if one is to travel, one must know where.
>
> — Well, show me.
>
> And all of us, leaning over the table, began to confer where to travel. Taking advantage of this, I imperceptibly to father shut the air-vent with one hand. He was overheated and could easily catch cold.
>
> We supposed we should travel as far as Novocherkassk, at Novocherkassk to stop with Denisenko (the daughter of Aunt Masha), to try to take there, with the help of her husband, Ivan Vasilyevich Denisenko, foreign passports, and, if this should succeed, to go to Bulgaria. If, however, they should not issue passports to us, then to go to the Caucasus.
>
> Conversing thus, we imperceptibly to ourselves grew ever more and more carried away with our plan, and discussed it hotly.
>
> — Well, enough, — said father, rising from the table, — there is no need of any plans, tomorrow we shall see.
>
> Suddenly it became unpleasant to him to speak of this, unpleasant that together with us he had been carried away and had begun to lay plans, having forgotten his cherished rule of life: to live only in the present.
>
> — I am hungry, — he said. — What might I eat?
>
> Varya and I had brought with us oatmeal-Hercules, mushrooms, eggs, a spirit-lamp, and quickly cooked him oatmeal. He ate with appetite, praising our cooking.
>
> Of his departure we spoke no more. Father only several times sighed heavily, and to my questioning glance said:
>
> — It is hard.
>
> My heart contracted as I looked at him: so sad he was and so troubled this evening, he spoke little, sighed, and went off early to sleep.
>
> We too at once dispersed to our rooms, and as we were very tired from the journey, fell asleep as if killed.
>
> About 4 o'clock in the morning I heard that someone was knocking at our door. I jumped up and opened. Before me, as a few days before, again stood father with a candle in his hands. He was fully dressed, and as then, said:
>
> — Dress quickly, we are leaving at once. I have already begun to pack my things; come, help me.
>
> He had slept badly, he was tormented by the possibility of S. A.'s arrival. At 4 o'clock he had wakened Dušan Petrovich and sent him for our coachmen, whom we had, for any eventuality, kept overnight. He did not forget to give orders for horses for Varya and me too, and sent a lay-brother from the monastery guest-house to the village for the local coachman.
>
> It was wholly dark. By the light of a candle I hurriedly gathered things together, tied up the trunks. Duš. Petr. came in; the Kozelsk coachmen brought up the horses, but our own coachman was still not there. I implored father to leave without waiting for us. He was very agitated, several times sent to the village for horses, and at last decided to leave. On one coachman father sat down with Duš. Petr., on the other they laid the things. I barely had time to throw a tunic on father and, with the help of the lay-brother, to load the things into the other tarantas, so was he hurrying; and they left, without even taking leave of Aunt Masha and Lizanka."

Of the end of this journey Lev Nikolaevich himself tells us in his diary; here is his last entry:

> "3 Nov. All there, at Astapovo. Sasha said they would overtake us, and we set off. At Kozelsk Sasha overtook us, we sat down, set off. We traveled well, but at the 5th hour I began to shiver, then 40 degrees of fever, we stopped at Astapovo. The obliging stationmaster gave us two beautiful rooms. The night was a hard one. I lay in fever for two days. On the 2nd Ch. arrived. They say that S. A. By night Seryozha arrived, he touched me much. Today, the 3rd, Nikitin, Tanya, then Goldenweiser and Iv. Iv. There is my plan... Fais ce que doit, adv...
>
> And all to the good both of others and, chiefly, of me..."

So was accomplished L. N.'s departure, which had been long preparing and which culminated in his illness and end at the station of Astapovo.

My ardent and immeasurable love for the great teacher of life does not permit me to analyze and to criticize his act. I bow down before the greatness of his feat. It is hard for us to judge where it was needed to manifest more strength of self-sacrifice: in remaining, or in going. He chose the latter.

Such was his will.
