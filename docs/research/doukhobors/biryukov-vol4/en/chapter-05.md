# Volume IV, Chapter 5. 1901–1902. The Crimea

**Source:** http://az.lib.ru/b/birjukow_p_i/text_1922_tolstoy06.shtml

**Translation:** English translation for the tolstoy.life research platform (2026-05-27), from the continuous Russian capture in [`../ru/chapter-05.md`](../ru/chapter-05.md).

We borrow the description of this historic journey of L. N.'s from the interesting article of his friend, P. A. Boulanger, who accompanied the train as far as the place itself.

> "On one of the dark, cold autumn nights, having dressed Lev Nikolaevich in a fur coat, we set off for Tula, 15 versts from Yasnaya Polyana. The road was dreadful; the short distance from the estate to the highway, about a verst, we had to drive lighting the road with torches. With Lev Nikolaevich there set off Sofya Andreyevna, his daughter Marya Lvovna with her husband Prince Obolensky, his third daughter Alexandra Lvovna, and the artist Igumnova, a close friend of the family, who had nursed Lev Nikolaevich during his illness in 1899.
>
> At about 10 in the evening we arrived at last at the station and at once moved Lev Nikolaevich into the waiting carriage. Here the rest of his children, who had come together, had gathered to take their leave. The train was to depart at about 3 in the morning. I remember clearly what a tormenting night that was. From the drive L. N. had grown considerably worse; he began to gasp for breath, the fever appeared again, and we all, in alarm, held a hurried council: what was to be done, was it possible to risk carrying the sick man any farther in such a state. We decided at 12 in the night to call a doctor from the town, who came, looked at him, and gave an indefinite opinion. A certain faith, however, was hidden within me that, if the sick man needed warmth and sun, then tomorrow we should be beyond Kursk and should see, instead of this rain, cold, fog, and gloom, bright sun and warmth, and the sick man would be better. I infected the others, I think, with my faith, and then too we considered that to carry him now 15 versts back over that abominable road was worse than to carry him 500 versts in a warm, dry carriage. We decided to go on.
>
> Almost no one slept that hard, memorable night, and we listened with dread to the slightest rustle in the compartment that Lev Nikolaevich occupied. Toward morning he grew a little better, and at 10 o'clock we were in Kursk, where it really was warm, dry, and bright. The sick man too grew much better, and we all cheered up; the hope arose of reaching the place safely.
>
> In such a buoyant, elevated mood, hoping for the best of everything, we drove up to Kharkov, where everyone hoped at least to eat a little during the train's long 20-minute halt. We had already divided the roles among ourselves — who would go to the station, what to bring into the carriage, who would stay with Lev Nikolaevich. Standing in Lev Nikolaevich's compartment and looking out the window at the station platform when the train stopped, I was struck by the extraordinary throng of people on the platform. What struck me most of all was that even on the crossbars of the awning over the platform people were, by some miracle, perched, with strained, excited faces, peering at our train.
>
> Suddenly a thought dawned upon me:
>
> 'Lev Nikolaevich,' I said, 'why, this crowd at the station must have gathered on the occasion of your passing through.'
>
> 'What are you saying! That cannot be,' he objected. Then, after a moment's thought, he said, 'draw the window curtain, please, just in case. For that would be dreadful.'
>
> And I saw how a certain alarm instantly seized him, and he at once grew weak.
>
> Meanwhile, from outside, through the hum of the crowd, there came at times voices: 'Tolstoy, Tolstoy... in this train... the last carriage...,' and so forth. When I came out of the compartment and wished to go onto the platform, it was already impossible to do this: everything was barricaded by the crowd. Excited faces stood on the carriage's platform, on the steps, saying something to Sofya Andreyevna, and the crowd turned its gaze upon our carriage. Some student begged to be admitted to L. N. to convey the greeting of a deputation; behind him stood a gentleman in civilian dress, saying something at the same time as he; and behind these there could be seen the figure of an officer, who was also trying to say something. Sofya Andreyevna entreated them imploringly to calm themselves, told them that Lev Nikolaevich was very ill, very weak, that he would be agitated if he received them, and that agitation was deadly for him. It was pitiful for me to look at these agitated people, evidently sincerely longing to see the man whom they ardently revered. Again I went into the compartment to Lev Nikolaevich. He was very agitated.
>
> 'Ah, my God, how dreadful this is,' he uttered. 'Why are they doing this? Listen, can it not somehow be arranged that we move on more quickly...'
>
> But this was impossible; we were travelling with a supplementary express train, and until the first express train reached the next station, we could not be dispatched. I told him of this, and also that, in my opinion, it would be proper to receive those who were asking for it.
>
> 'Ah, why this, why, all this is needless, and I simply cannot,' he groaned, somehow helplessly, burrowing still deeper into the corner of the divan.
>
> There remained some ten minutes before the train's departure. The crowd looked, somehow at a loss, at our carriage, and through it there ran: 'he is ill, fallen dangerously ill, he is lying down...' Several people made their way into the vestibule of the carriage and again entreated Sofya Andreyevna to admit them to the sick man, swearing that they would not agitate him, and so forth. The Countess went to Lev Nikolaevich and persuaded him to receive them. They were let in, and, getting muddled in their expressions, they mumbled a few words — that they had come to greet him as the representatives of an enormous number of his admirers, that he was dear to all, that all were extremely agitated by the news of his illness, that they longed to hear good tidings of his recovery for the good of all mankind, and so forth.
>
> Lev Nikolaevich asked them who they were, and, learning that one of them was a student, expressed the wish that they should preserve within themselves that pure youthful ardour with which they now burned, and asked them to thank, for their sympathy toward him, those who had sent them.
>
> Hardly had they left the carriage when several more people asked to be let in as well; these too were admitted. And when they had gone and conveyed their impressions to those around them, voices were heard: 'we beg Lev Nikolaevich for a moment, only for a moment, to show himself at the window... we beg you...' Everything fell silent all around, everything was in agitation.
>
> They persuaded Lev Nikolaevich to show himself at the window. Weak, agitated, he raised himself, leaned upon the window-sill, and bowed. Instantly everything grew still, heads were bared, and all gazed respectfully and reverently at this weak, sick, helpless man, who so titanically awakened the very best in the souls of men. It was such a picture that, by its majesty, its solemnity, by that discipline of spiritual tension which had bound the whole of this crowd, it was carved into my memory for the whole of my life. The third bell sounded. And suddenly, as if from a single mouth, there rang out a thousand-voiced 'hurrah.' All waved their handkerchiefs, their caps, shouted: 'get well, come back in health, God keep you...' Our train slowly moved off, and at last we were once more left alone.
>
> When I came up to Lev Nikolaevich, he was sitting utterly weakened, distressed, his eyes moist, as they always were in moments of strong spiritual tension.
>
> After some time the sick man grew worse and worse; the heart began to beat irregularly, the temperature began to creep up, and we all grew despondent again. All were hungry; no one had managed to lay in food at Kharkov and refresh himself, but all this, of course, was trifling in comparison with the gloomy thoughts of what to do if the worsening should continue. We did not dare to carry him in a hopeless condition, but who could determine how hopeless the condition was. A doctor could be got only at Lozovaya — and even then, was it possible? If there was a doctor there, he was a railway doctor, who might always be away. And since Lev Nikolaevich was growing worse and worse, we decided to seek a doctor at Lozovaya, to send a telegram to my acquaintance, a physician at Yekaterinoslav, asking him to come out to Sinelnikovo, 40 versts from Yekaterinoslav, and to advise us what to do, whether to continue the journey or to turn back.
>
> But, as we drove up to Lozovaya, Lev Nikolaevich grew better again, his pulse became even, the temperature dropped, and he was even able to drink some milk. Once more there flashed the hope of reaching Sevastopol, and we, having taken a bite at the station, passed a quiet night after the tormentingly tense twenty-four hours.
>
> When we awoke the next day, a dazzlingly bright southern sun was looking in at the windows, and below, on both sides of the line, stretched the Sivash. It was warm, even hot. Driving up to Simferopol, we opened the windows of the carriage and breathed greedily the warm, gentle air. Lev Nikolaevich had passed the night comparatively well, his look was good, and he, evidently, inhaled this air with delight and was already thinking, in accordance with the habit acquired over his whole life, of sitting down to work in these brisk morning hours. He got out his notebook, began to write in it; then he asked for the sheets of his latest work to be got out and withdrew to himself to work. But, evidently, the noise and jolting of the train, the unaccustomed surroundings, and the anxiety of those near him that, after the agitations he had been through, he was again exerting himself harmfully, made him soon finish his work and rejoin us.
>
> At Simferopol we bought some splendid grapes, *chasselas* and *isabella*, and tempted Lev Nikolaevich to take part in our feast. All grew lively, were merry, and Lev Nikolaevich too shared our mood, and when we passed the stations whose names recalled to him the Sevastopol campaign, he remembered the past and recounted to us in detail the events that had taken place there during the defence of Sevastopol and episodes from his own life at that time.
>
> At Sevastopol there awaited us once again a demonstration, but this time a very modest one, almost exclusively ladies, who related that for nearly two weeks now crowds of people had gathered daily at the station, hoping to meet Lev Nikolaevich, but at last, having lost faith in his coming, had little by little ceased to gather, and only these remnants had been true to themselves and had waited him out. But when I looked out of the station window, I saw that in front of the station too there was a crowd, and before the crowd there paced several police officers. When we came out to get into the carriage, one of them, the police chief, got into his own *calèche* and dashed off ahead of us. Evidently the police were working with might and main, displaying their zeal, and their representative hurried on ahead in order to try to forestall an 'unlawful gathering of a crowd.'
>
> The warm, gentle, windless day, the dazzling sun, which seemed still more dazzling against the white houses and the limestone cobbles of the pavement, and the dark blue, bottomless sky, the briskness of the sick man, who tried even not to make use of the help of others when he got out of the *calèche* — everything was so good, acted so encouragingly, that, it seemed, one might set off on the further journey to Yalta without a pause. But as it was already late, and we should not have been able to reach the place by horse before twilight, it was decided that, if all should remain equally well, we would set off on the further journey tomorrow at 10 in the morning.
>
> At about two in the afternoon all went out for a walk. This was Lev Nikolaevich's usual hour for walking, and, feeling strength within himself, he wished to go at least as far as the Seaside Boulevard, situated near the Kist Hotel, where we had put up. Having rested a little on the boulevard, Lev Nikolaevich wished to try walking through the town and, leaning on my arm, went up the street in the direction of the Museum of the Defence of Sevastopol. When we reached the museum, which Lev Nikolaevich wished to see, it turned out that we were already too late; it is open only until 2 o'clock, and we were not let in. Going back, a few steps from the museum, we met an officer, who at first looked at Lev Nikolaevich in amazement, then suddenly stopped, saluted, and, coming up to him, asked permission to introduce himself.
>
> 'Allow me, Count, to introduce myself, Captain N.'
>
> Lev Nikolaevich gave him his hand and began to recollect.
>
> 'You are N.? Why, are you not the son of that N.?'
>
> 'Just so, Your Excellency.'
>
> 'Yes, yes, I remember,' said Lev Nikolaevich, 'your mother and I danced together right here in Sevastopol... why, of course, I remember... and what are you doing here?'
>
> N. proved very obliging and, learning that we had not been let into the museum, offered to take us there, and we turned back and went up the gently sloping steps of the majestic colonnade of the building dedicated to the memory of the defenders, one of whom now, 45 years after the horrors he had lived through and described, was now mounting them in completely different surroundings.
>
> The captain led Lev Nikolaevich through the rooms, giving various explanations; Lev Nikolaevich for his part recalled various episodes, the disposition of the batteries, some of the defenders. In one of the rooms was his portrait, and the captain with particular pleasure drew his attention to it, but Lev Nikolaevich, with evident displeasure, turned away from the portrait, somehow at once fell silent — evidently another group of recollections and another mood had drowned out the initial excitement, and he, complaining of fatigue, proposed to return to the hotel.
>
> 'How sad this is,' he said on the way, 'why this costly building, this careful collecting of all the old buttons, fragments. One ought to forget all this horror, this brutalization, this disgrace, and they strive to fan recollections... it is dreadful, dreadful...'
>
> He returned to the hotel exhausted, weary, and somehow withered. But, having rested a little, he set to work, began to write, and we, seeing that he was well, began to prepare for the journey by carriage on the morrow, ordered horses, two *calèches*, and passed the rest of the day quietly in the hotel.
>
> The next morning was magnificent; we managed to lay in fresh milk, bread, grapes, and fruit, and by 10 in the morning had already set off in two carriages for Yalta. Lev Nikolaevich looked over the locality we were driving through and explained to us the disposition of the redoubts, of the troops during the defence of Sevastopol. He felt well, and during the first change of horses near Balaklava went a little on foot along the highway to stretch his legs. At Baydary we made an hour's halt in order to prepare an unpretentious dinner for Lev Nikolaevich. All set to work energetically: one heated the stove in an outbuilding adjoining the post-station, another hurriedly unpacked and got everything out, and Sofya Andreyevna was an energetic cook. We hurried and all worked in concert, afraid that we should be late in reaching the place before sunset; to carry him after sunset was dangerous, given his predisposition to fever; this time was reckoned in the Crimea the most dangerous for the sick.
>
> At last we passed over the Baydar Gates. As we drove up to them, two *calèches* met us, and those who were driving had evidently been forewarned of Lev Nikolaevich's passing, since, along with noisy greetings, they pelted him with flowers. Below, beneath the Baydar Gates, groups of the curious were also waiting.
>
> At the first halt after the Baydar Gates, while the horses were being changed, Lev Nikolaevich went again to stretch his legs along the highway and began to recall the locality. As Lev Nikolaevich could not exactly recall the places lying below along the shore, he turned to a fine young fellow who had stopped on the highway — something between a shop-clerk and one of the petty traders or tenant-farmers. The fellow, seeing a poorly dressed little old man in a strange blouse, began to answer the questions with dignity and undisguised contempt. I watched this scene, and it amused me extremely, this haughty dignity of the young fellow — one could see that he set a high price on having condescended to converse with this drab little man. At last the *calèche* with Sofya Andreyevna drove up, and Lev Nikolaevich, having thanked the stranger, got in and drove off, while I stayed behind to wait for the next *calèche*. The stranger looked in astonishment after the departed *calèche*.
>
> 'You do not know who that little old man is?' he asked me.
>
> 'That is Count Tolstoy,' I answered.
>
> 'What,' he exclaimed, 'is that the very Count Tolstoy, the writer?'
>
> 'The very same.'
>
> 'My God. My God!' he exclaimed in despair and, snatching his cap from his head for some reason, hurled it onto the dusty highway. 'And I spoke to him like that! I would give, it seems, all in life, only to see him just once, and there... and I, scoundrel that I am, spoke to him like that, thought he was just some little pilgrim or other, oh, oh, oh...'
>
> I got into the carriage that had driven up, and for a long time we saw how this unhappy man stood without his cap on the highway and kept looking after the carriage that was bearing away the man with whom he had 'spoken like that.'
>
> When we drove up to Miskhor, the sun had set, and the Crimean twilight, or, rather, the night, was swiftly descending. We asked the drivers not to change horses at this station and to drive quickly to Gaspra, the estate of Countess Panina, which was already not far off, some 3 or 4 versts. At last there was Gaspra; the gates open, they are waiting; a large house, the 'palace,' lit up; and the dear, obliging German manager K. Kh. Klassen, who afterwards rendered so much solicitude and service both to Lev Nikolaevich and to his family, stood with bread and salt at the doors of the house. This was the evening of 8 September.
>
> It is curious to recall with what fear, to a certain degree, Lev Nikolaevich looked upon this house. It was a rich house, well finished and furnished — one of those *palazzi* which every rich European reckons it his duty to have on the shore of the Mediterranean Sea or at some other resort. But Lev Nikolaevich, accustomed to the modest, simple — not to say poor — surroundings of Yasnaya Polyana, where the floors in many rooms were unpainted, worn out, the frames in the windows had rotted, the paint had come off, and one had to discuss the question whether to change them now or whether one could still wait until the following year — and suddenly here an extraordinary magnificence and cleanliness in comparison with the Yasnaya Polyana house. Indeed, one felt somehow ill at ease, and all went about, bewildered, through these enormous halls. Lev Nikolaevich, looking with fear at the enormous vases in the corners, warned us to be careful, and one could see that he was altogether ill at ease. He installed himself on the lower floor, to the right, in a room adjoining the hall, with windows looking out to the west and to the south, but on the south side there was a covered terrace that sheltered it from the sun. This was not an altogether suitable room, but the quietest on the lower floor, while on the upper floor it was inconvenient, because it was unavoidably necessary to go up by a staircase, which was harmful for the sick man.
>
> The following clear, sunny days of the wondrous Crimean autumn were magnificent, and Lev Nikolaevich had neither fever nor irregular action of the heart.
>
> He began to take small walks, and since, owing to the difficulty everywhere in the Crimea of avoiding mountain walks and the unbearableness of the dusty highway, he came to love in particular the walk along the so-called 'horizontal path,' laid out from the palace of the Grand Duke Alexander Mikhailovich, adjacent to Gaspra, almost to Livadia itself, and from which there opened a wondrous view of Yalta."

On 12 October L. N. was visited by Anton Pavlovich Chekhov, who was then living in the Crimea.

S. A. records thus in her diary of this meeting:

> "A. P. Chekhov was here, and by his simplicity, by the talent acknowledged in him by us all, he pleased us all very much and seemed a man near to us in spirit. The mark of a terrible illness, consumption, lay upon him already, and the more touching for that did he seem to us."

Beautiful are the pages of L. N.'s diary of that time; here are several sketches of his first sojourn in the Crimea:

> "Terrible to say: I have not written for almost two months. Today it is 10 October 1901. Gaspra, on the southern shore. My health is just as bad as before. Now worsenings, now improvements, but slight ones. My former health is finally over. And it is well, and not only is it well, but it is precisely well. A preparation for the crossing-over. We came here with Boulanger, Masha, and Kolya. Sasha is very sweet. Now Seryozha is here. The inner work, it seems, is little by little moving forward. Adam Vasilyevich (*) has died, and it is very well. During all this time I have been working on religion. It seems to be moving forward, but I have also grown weaker mentally, I can work less time.
>
> (* Count A. V. Olsufyev. L. N. often used to go to him at his estate of Pokrovskoye, near Moscow, to rest from the Moscow bustle. *)
>
> God forbid that one should live for this world only. In order that life should have meaning, its aim must lie beyond the bounds of this world, beyond the bounds of what is comprehensible to the human mind.
>
> 11 October 1901. Gaspra. If I live. Today it is 24 October 1901. Gaspra. The words 'if I live' acquire more and more meaning. During this time I have written about religion. My health is all *chancelante* (*), going downhill.
>
> (* Unsteady, literally — wavering. *)
>
> When the stream of water flows evenly, it seems that it stands still. So it seems also with one's own life and with life in general. But one notices that the stream does not stand still but flows when it dwindles, especially when it drips — so it is with life too."

In spite of this approach to eternity, and thanks to it, Lev Nikolaevich does not cease to think about the relation of the classes, the ruling and the ruled, and in his diary of that time there occur such remarkable thoughts:

> "10 October. Entrepreneurs (capitalists) rob the people, becoming intermediaries between the workers and the suppliers of the implements and means of labour; in the same way the merchants rob, becoming intermediaries between the consumers and the sellers. Likewise, under the pretext of mediation between the wronged and the wrongdoers, state robbery is established. But the most 'dreadful deception is the deception of the intermediaries between God and men.'"

In the Crimea there forms around L. N. a circle of his more frequent visitors: A. P. Chekhov visited L. N. a second time with A. M. Gorky, who was also living in the Crimea, and between L. N. and them there are established certain particular, intimate relations.

Al. Maks. Gorky records in his diary certain sayings of L. N.'s during this meeting. We give here those which are, in our opinion, the most significant:

> "In the morning there were *shtundisty* from Theodosia; today he spoke with rapture of circles.
>
> At lunch: 'They came, both of them so sturdy, solid; one said: "Here — we have come uninvited," and the other — "God willing, we shall leave unbeaten." And he dissolved into childlike laughter, his whole frame quivering.
>
> After lunch, on the terrace:
>
> 'Soon we shall altogether cease to understand the language of the people: we here say "the theory of progress," "the role of personality in history," "the evolution of science," "dysentery," whereas a peasant will say "you can't hide an awl in a sack," and — all the theories, histories, evolution become pitiful, ridiculous, because they are not comprehensible and not necessary to the people. But the peasant is stronger than we, he is more tenacious of life, and what happened to the tribe of the Aztecs may, perhaps, happen to us as well — the tribe of which some learned man was told: "All the Aztecs have died out, but here there is a parrot which knows a few words of their language."'

For L. N. there had long ceased to exist 'neither Greek nor Jew.' And so, after the proletarian writer, he is visited by a Grand Duke. We find a note about this in a letter of S. A. to her sister:

> "...Then the Grand Duke Nikolai Mikhailovich came to Lyovochka — this is already the second time — and, they say, he is delighted with his conversations with Lev Nikolaevich. This Grand Duke is a very lively, independent man, interested in everything.
>
> Lyovochka liked him too."

Between him and L. N. there are established particular relations, and an active correspondence strikes up. We shall give, in its proper place, the most characteristic excerpts from this correspondence.

This visit is noted also by Alexei Maksimovich in his reminiscences:

> "Today the Grand Duke Nikolai Mikhailovich was there, a man evidently very intelligent. He bore himself very modestly, was of few words. He has sympathetic eyes and a handsome figure. Calm gestures. L. N. smiled at him affectionately and spoke now in French, now in English. In Russian he said:
>
> 'Karamzin wrote for the Tsar. Solovyov — long-windedly and tediously, and Klyuchevsky for his own amusement. Cunning: you read, it is as if he praises, but you look closely — he has abused.'

Someone recalled Zabelin.

> 'Very sweet. Such an old clerk. An amateur old-clothes-man, collects everything, what is needed and what is not. He describes food as though he himself had never eaten his fill. But — very, very amusing.'

Alexei Maksimovich continues his account:

> The illness has dried him out still more, has burned something out in him; he has become inwardly, as it were, lighter, more transparent, more accepting of life. His eyes — sharper still; his gaze — piercing. He listens attentively and as if recollecting the forgotten, or confidently awaiting the new, the still unknown."

Evidently Alexei Maksimovich felt himself completely at ease in L. N.'s house, since S. A. records in her diary:

> "M. Gorky played *gorodki* with Sasha and Yul. Iv. Igumnova."

At the end of November L. N. wrote an interesting letter to a member of the court, S. P. Polyakov. Evidently he wrote with joyful emotion:

> "Kind Sergei Petrovich, if a reward had been promised for the service of God, then the consciousness of such spiritual communion as has arisen between you and me would be quite sufficient for it. This communion in God with the most various people has now for many years constituted the best joy of my life, and although you afforded it to me involuntarily, I am nonetheless grateful to you for it. Even from the little book which you compiled I recognized that I had a new fellow believer and friend, and rejoiced at this; and now I am the more glad, knowing who he is, and can enter into personal relations with him. To your question about the step which you consider it necessary or desirable to take, I can say to you only this — that this question always arises before a man passing from religious indifference to the consciousness of Christian truth. This question stands before me just as it stands before all my fellow believers and friends without exception, both Russian and foreign. The solution of this question for me is this — that since the aim of all the actions of a Christian is the service of the God of love, the increase in oneself and in others of love toward God and men, then one of the first demands of such service is the not breaking of the existing mutual love toward the family, toward those near one: toward parents, toward wife, toward children. And therefore it falls to every man to weigh — and this only he himself can do — by which action he will the more depart from the law of love: by the continuation of an activity or a life contrary to love, or by the freeing of himself from it, notwithstanding the breaking, by this action, of love toward those near him, the family. The choice of the one or the other way out plainly depends both on the criminality of the position from which one has to free oneself, and on the clearness of the understanding of this criminality, and on the degree of the love binding one to the family. But in any case this question can be decided only by him whom it concerns. One thing I will add — that in case of a decision in favour of not changing one's position, the chief thing is not to strive to justify this position of one's own, and not to cease to acknowledge it as bad, but not to cease to wish to be delivered from it, so as to take advantage of the first possibility of doing this without breaking love.
>
> With all my heart I wish you to find that way out of your position which would satisfy the demands of your conscience. And this way out you will surely find, if you carry the question over from the sphere of human judgment to the judgment of God, caring only to do that which He wishes."

At the same time L. N. listens sensitively to the beating of the pulse of the people's life and, taking a sceptical attitude toward the so-called 'social movements,' he believed in the importance of that inaudible work which was being accomplished among the people.

Apropos of a certain revolutionary student proclamation which he had received, L. N. records:

> "...It is both insane and stupid. What the leaflet proposes is impractical, because it is unthinkable that unarmed, undisciplined men could take away the weapons from the armed and the disciplined. And it is stupid because for those men who wish to free themselves from killing and from the threats of killing to prepare for killing means to give their enemies the one lawful pretext for using against them every kind of violence and even killings, and for justifying all that was committed before."

The work of the people, however, in comparison with these, is enormous. He expressed this thought beautifully in a letter to me of that time:

> "With us there goes on an aimless seething, which can lead to nothing except to serve as an indicator of the abnormality of the whole course of Russian life, in the circles of the studying youth, and a quiet religious sapping in the deep bowels of the people. I welcome it and rejoice in the consciousness that the realization of what is felt to be 'at the doors' is near — though no one knows what it is. But a change of life there will be, because that which moves it has changed — the religious world-view."

And in his diary we find once more a profound, religious wisdom:

> "30 November. How good is the Jewish rule not to dare to pronounce the name of God! This prohibition shows that they understood what He is. But our hail-fellow-well-met attitude, our 'Lord have mercy,' degenerating into 'lor a' mercy, lor a' mercy,' shows that with us they have not even a notion of what God may be for religious people."

At this time the usual award of the Nobel Prize was taking place in Sweden. L. N. had been put forward as one of the candidates, but the prize was awarded not to him, but to someone else. This comic decision of the 'wise' council called forth a letter from a group of L. N.'s admirers of the following content:

> "The Nobel Prize has just been awarded for the first time for literature, and we, the undersigned Swedish authors, artists, and critics, wish to express to you our admiration. We see in you not only the patriarch of contemporary literature, but also a mighty and profound poet, who ought to have been thought of first of all, notwithstanding the fact that you have never on your own account striven for such a reward. We feel the need to say to you these words all the more in that the institution to which the awarding of the literary prizes has been entrusted does not, in its present composition, represent in the least the opinion either of artists or of the public. It is necessary that abroad it should be known that in our remote country art is understood as founded on freedom of thought and creation, and that such art is held to be the chief, and the strongest, of all."

L. N. answered this as follows:

> "Dear sirs. The award of the Nobel Prize not to me was doubly agreeable to me: in the first place, because it delivered me from the heavy necessity of disposing in one way or another of money which is considered by all to be a necessary and useful thing, but by me a source of every kind of evil; and in the second place, because it served as an occasion for the expression to me of their sympathy by people respected by me, for which I thank them with all my heart."

At the end of the year L. N.'s health worsens again. Sofya Andreyevna with alarm informs her sister of this:

> "...The walks are so tempting that Lyovochka has again overstrained his heart and his health, having driven 22 versts and walked over the mountains and down to the sea to the point of exhaustion. At last he went to Yalta, to Masha, and there took to his bed, frightening us extremely with attacks of the heart, shortness of breath, and irregular beats. Now he still complains of fever at night and pains in the legs. He has already now begun to take care of himself, sits at home for several days, and in the evenings plays *vint* or chess with Sukhotin."

L. N. lay ill at Marya Lvovna's in Yalta for a whole week, and when he had recovered a little, Sofya Andreyevna and Elizaveta Valerianovna Obolenskaya conveyed him by *calèche* to Gaspra.

Having recovered a little, L. N. again takes walks: he visits Alexei Maksimovich Gorky twice, toward whom, evidently, he is drawn by his origin from the people.

Lev Nikolaevich records in his diary the impressions of this time.

> "Today, it seems, is 1 December. Yesterday was very good. I wrote a letter to Polyakov, a member of the court, made an entry in my diary, and got on with the 16th chapter about religion. Today I slept badly, little. Pains and weakness. I wrote badly and did not finish. Our people have gone off to Uchan-Su. I am alone with Tanya at home, and I am out of sorts. A splendid chapter in Polenz's novel, which greatly eggs me on, but in vain — to write. Again I look at death better, more clearly, more nearly.
>
> It had grown better, then again worse. I went to Yalta to spend the night, and there fell ill with my heart. I stayed a week at Masha's. Again I am beginning to recover. Dear Boulanger was here. Today he has gone. I have finished about religion, but I shall probably look it over again. For about 10 days I wrote about religious toleration and got sick of it. Too unimportant. However, I do not want to write the letter to the Tsar either."

And again wise thoughts:

> "People who assimilate the Christian outlook usually strive to perform Christian feats. But what Christianity needs is not feats, but a simple Christian life.
>
> So clearly is the immediate task of life seen. It lies in this — to replace life founded on struggle and violence by a life founded on love and reasonable concord. And the enormous material which must be spiritually worked over for this lies still untouched among the working people of all races and faiths."

The New Year of 1902 L. N. begins with a very important matter. He writes a long letter to the Emperor Nicholas II. This letter is very remarkable both in form and in content. Feeling the nearness of the crossing-over into a new, unconditional world, L. N. could no longer observe the established conventions in such an important act as a letter to the head of state — that is, to the person who held in his hands all the threads of state violence, on whose will it depended to relax or to intensify the oppression over a people of many millions. And so L. N. begins it not with the customary address 'Your Majesty' or 'Sire,' but writes to him simply 'Dear brother.' And he explains at the beginning of the letter the motives of such an address. Further on, in brief but forceful expressions, he sets forth to him the picture of the condition of Russia:

> "A third of Russia is in the condition of the reinforced safeguard, that is, outside the law. The army of police, open and secret, grows ever larger and larger. The prisons, the places of exile, and the penal colonies are overcrowded, over and above the hundreds of thousands of criminals, with political prisoners, to whom now the workers too are reckoned. The censorship has reached an absurdity of prohibitions to which it did not attain in the worst times of the forties. Religious persecutions have never been so frequent and cruel as now, and they grow ever crueller and crueller, and more frequent. Everywhere in the towns and factory centres troops are concentrated and are sent out with live cartridges against the people. In many places there have already been fratricidal bloodsheds, and everywhere new and still crueller ones are being prepared and will inevitably take place."

The result of this is, of course, the impoverishment of Russia. Where is the cause of this? L. N. answers thus:

> "And the cause of all this, clear to the point of obviousness, is one — that our helpers assure you that, by stopping every movement of life among the people, they thereby secure the welfare of this people and your tranquillity and safety.
>
> But surely one might more easily stop the flow of a river than the perpetual forward movement of mankind established by God."

Having pointed out to the Tsar the untimeliness of upholding Orthodoxy and the autocracy, which have long since lost their meaning among the people, he warns him how little one can trust the so-called expressions of 'the love of the people.'

> "You are probably led into error about the love of the people for the autocracy and its representative, the Tsar, by the fact that everywhere, when you are met in Moscow and in other towns, crowds of the people run after you with cries of 'hurrah.' Do not believe that this is an expression of devotion to you; it is a crowd of the curious, which will run just the same after any unaccustomed spectacle. And often these people whom you take for the expressers of the people's love for you are nothing other than a crowd assembled and arranged by the police, which is supposed to represent a people devoted to you, as, for instance, was the case with your grandfather at Kharkov, when the cathedral was full of people, but the whole of the people consisted of policemen in disguise.
>
> If you could also walk about during a royal passage along the line of peasants stationed behind the troops the whole length of the railway, and listen to what these peasants say — the elders, the *sotskie*, the *desyatskie*, herded together from the neighbouring villages and, in the cold and in the slush, without remuneration, with their own bread, waiting for several days for the passage — you would hear from the most genuine representatives of the people, simple peasants, all along the line, speeches utterly out of agreement with any love for the autocracy and its representative. If 50 years ago, under Nicholas I, the prestige of the Tsar's power still stood high, then over the last 30 years it has, without ceasing, fallen — and has fallen of late so far that in all the estates no one any longer scruples boldly to condemn not only the dispositions of the government, but the Tsar himself, and even to abuse him and to laugh at him.
>
> The autocracy is a form of government that has outlived its time, capable of answering the demands of a people somewhere in Central Africa, cut off from all the world, but not the demands of the Russian people, which is more and more enlightened by the enlightenment common to all the world; and therefore to uphold this form of government and the Orthodoxy bound up with it is possible only, as is indeed being done now, by means of every kind of violence, the reinforced safeguard, administrative exiles, executions, religious persecutions, the prohibition of books and newspapers, the perversion of education, and in general every kind of bad and cruel deed."

And Lev Nikolaevich proposes the true measure for the regeneration of Russia:

> "By measures of violence one can oppress a people, but not govern it. The only means in our time really to govern a people lies only in this — that, having placed oneself at the head of the movement of the people from evil to good, from darkness to light, one should lead it toward the attainment of the aims nearest to this movement. But in order to be in a condition to do this, it is necessary first of all to give the people the possibility of expressing its desires and needs and, having heard these desires and needs, to fulfil those of them which will answer the demands not of one class or estate, but of the majority of it, of the mass of the working people.
>
> And the desires which the Russian people would now express, if it were given the possibility of doing this, will, in my opinion, be the following:
>
> First of all the working people will say that it wishes to be delivered from those exceptional laws which place it in the position of a pariah, not enjoying the rights of all the other citizens; then it will say that it wishes freedom of movement, freedom of instruction, and freedom of profession of the faith proper to its spiritual needs; and, chiefly, the whole hundred-million-strong people will say with one voice that it wishes freedom in the use of the land, that is, the abolition of the right of landed property.
>
> And it is just this abolition of landed property that is, in my opinion, that immediate aim the attainment of which the Russian government ought in our time to make its task."

Urging the Tsar not to trust self-interested counsellors, L. N. again, in conclusion, addresses him in touching expressions:

> "Dear brother, you have only one life in this world, and you can either spend it tormentingly upon vain attempts to stop the movement of mankind, established by God, from evil to good, from darkness to light, or you can, having entered into the needs and desires of the people and devoted your life to fulfilling them, pass it quietly and joyfully in the service of God and men."

The unhappy Tsar did not heed the entreaties and wise counsels of the great elder. Inexorable fate was preparing for him a terrible lot. By a cruel death he expiated the guilt of himself and his forefathers, and it is not for us to judge him. But we cannot pass over in silence the strange result of this remarkable letter.

In order that this letter should reach the Emperor, L. N. turned for mediation to his new friend, the Grand Duke Nikolai Mikhailovich, expressing his request in the following letter:

> "Dear Nikolai Mikhailovich!
>
> If you remember, in one of our meetings at Gaspra I told you that I had the intention of writing a letter to the Emperor. My health is not improving, and, feeling that the end is near, I have written this letter, not wishing to die without having uttered what I think about his activity and about what it might be.
>
> Perhaps something of what I express there will be of use.
>
> The question for me now is how to deliver this letter so that it should fall straight into the Emperor's hands.
>
> I remember your advice and have followed it, and this letter, although it frankly condemns the measures of the government, yet the feelings by which it is called forth are undoubtedly good, and I hope it will be so received by the Emperor.
>
> Could you not help me to deliver this letter directly to him for whom it is intended? If for some reason it is inconvenient for you to do this, be so kind as to telegraph me 'no.' But if you agree to do this, then telegraph 'yes.' And I will at once send the letter to you or to St Petersburg, to whomever you may indicate.
>
> Please forgive me for, perhaps, abusing your kindness. I do this because, it seems to me — excuse me my presumption — this letter may have good consequences for many. And to this, as far as I have understood you, you cannot be indifferent.
>
> With perfect respect and sincere sympathy I remain ready to serve you. Lev Tolstoy."

Evidently the Grand Duke answered 'yes,' since the letter was sent off and reached its destination, but the answer was the most unexpected. Probably L. N., in sending or conveying the letter, stated that he would not wish to give this letter publicity, that is, to show it to the ministers or to anyone else, and therefore chose the most direct way of conveying it. Whether it was conveyed thus to the Emperor is unknown, but the Emperor, having received and read the letter, asked that it be conveyed to L. N. that he should not be uneasy, that he would show it to no one. And with that the matter ended. L. N., in answer to the Grand Duke's telegram, wrote to him thus:

> "Dear Nikolai Mikhailovich. Very, very grateful to you for your kind telegram, from which I can once more be convinced that you do not fear 'scarlet fever' and stand above such fear, which greatly gladdens me for you and for my relations with you.
>
> I enclose the letter to the Emperor, written, unfortunately, not in my own hand. I had begun to do it, but felt myself so weak that I could not finish. I ask the Emperor to excuse me for this. I send the letter unsealed, so that, if you find it necessary, you might read it and decide once more whether it is convenient for you to convey it. The letter may seem in some places harsh — the truth, or what one considers the truth, cannot be uttered by halves — and therefore you may, perhaps, not wish to be a mediator in a matter disagreeable to the Emperor. This will not prevent me from being heartily grateful to you for your readiness to help me. In that case I shall choose another way. But for the present keep the letter with you. You are in St Petersburg, and now, probably, that question is being decided of which you spoke to me. With all my heart I wish that it should be decided in accordance with the higher demands of your conscience, that is, not in view of personal happiness, but of the true good of others. Then surely all will be well. My health goes worse and worse, and I am rapidly approaching that end, or rather the great change of life, which is sometimes alien to me, and sometimes near and even desirable.
>
> Farewell, once more I thank you and from my heart wish you all that is truly good."

In January L. N.'s health worsens again, and his friends and family are alarmed once more. Gloomy tidings fly all round the world. Sofya Andreyevna writes to her sister on 7 January:

> "...We have again lived through both fear and grief with Lyovochka. All the same phenomena... They ascribed everything to fever; Lyovochka has taken 320 grammes of quinine in all, 30 subcutaneous injections of arsenic have been made — and to little purpose...
>
> ...Today he is better again; he sits in an armchair, reads, jots something down, but he is gloomy and again refuses to be treated. From St Petersburg Bertenson offers to come gratis and is uneasy, it would seem, that Lyovochka is not being treated rightly and that they are harming him. I have heard that quinine produces obstructions in the liver, and how much of it he has taken here! One of the local doctors has found a strong induration of the liver."

And here is the attitude of L. N. himself toward his illness; in his diary of that time he records:

> "23 Jan. Gaspara. 1902. If I live. I am still weak; Bertenson has come. Trifles, of course. Wonderful verses:
>
> Why, old fellow, do you keep on groaning,
>
> Why, old fellow, do you keep on coughing?
>
> It is time, old fellow, to go under the shroud,
>
> Under the shroud, and into the little grave.
>
> What a delight is the speech of the people! It is both picturesque, and touching, and serious. I thought: there is no clearer proof of the false path on which science stands than its certainty that it will get to know everything."

After Bertenson's departure there occurred a worsening of Lev Nikolaevich's health.

On 28 January Marya Lvovna wrote to her friend Marya Aleks. Schmidt:

> "...Papa is worse and worse; today the inflammation of the lung and the pleurisy have spread further, and the heart is very bad. There is almost no hope; he now suffers less; today he told me that he is well; the hard thing, chiefly, is that it is difficult for him to breathe, and therefore he groans and keeps asking that the windows be opened. He speaks very little and is often in a swoon, and in the swoon he groans and is delirious, but when he is a little better he always says something affectionate or even a jest. Ah, M. A., my dear, how hard it is — only one thing consoles me, that it is not hard for him. In soul he is just as lofty and good as ever, and now at least he does not suffer much physically."

The doctors diagnosed in Lev Nikolaevich a creeping inflammation of the lungs, which, given his general weakness, was extremely dangerous.

In his lucid moments L. N. tried to calm and hearten his alarmed family and friends; thus, on 31 January he sent the following telegram to his brother Sergei:

> "It is joyful to be on the height of readiness for death, from which it is easy and tranquil to change the form of life. And I do not want to part with this feeling, although the doctors say that the illness has turned for the better. I feel your love and rejoice in it. Lyovochka."

The whole telegram he dictated to Marya Lvovna and only himself signed it 'Lyovochka,' grew agitated, and wept. This form of his name — 'Lyovochka' — was used in his family and recalled to him his childhood.

For several days L. N. was between life and death. All the children gathered to the dying man.

Sofya Andreyevna wrote to her sister:

3 February 1902. Gaspra.

> "The course of the illness is so indefinite, so slow, that not a single doctor undertakes to foretell anything... The inflammation holds in the left lung, near the heart, and may at any minute pass to the walls of the heart, which has become so very bad this year. When yesterday morning Lyovochka felt himself better, he met the doctor with the words: 'and I am still not giving in.' Every worsening calls forth in him a gloominess; he is silent and thinks, and God knows what is going on in his soul. With all of us around him he is very affectionate and grateful. But it is very hard for him to be ill, unaccustomed; in my opinion, he very much does not want to die. The power of thought is still so great that the sick man, scarcely audible, yet dictates to Masha corrections to his latest article, or has something jotted down at his dictation about the illness and his thoughts in a little notebook. Our doctor is the best Moscow one, Shchurovsky. Also an excellent one from Yalta, Altschuler, and the *zemstvo* one, the local Volkov. The last two are on duty every other night.
>
> ...The doctors say sincerely that nothing can be known in advance. I myself now pass over to full hope, now despair comes over me, I weep for hours and can do nothing. Or else I take myself in hand, so as to go on tending Lyovochka briskly to the end. At night I sit alone, and what do I not think over above him. The whole of my life passes by with the tormenting pain of recollections; and the repentance for all that with which I tormented Lyovochka in life, and the powerlessness to bring anything back or to set it right, and simply the pity for the sufferings of the beloved man — all this has lacerated my heart. Or else, when one grows weary, one takes a dull attitude to everything. Often, too, there comes a religious uplift, in the sense of 'Thy will be done!' Or else it seems that I shall not survive Lyovochka; it is as if half of me is being torn away, and that pain one does not survive."

Sofya Andreyevna notes in her diary a characteristic feature of the illness. In his delirium L. N. said: "Sevastopol is burning." Having renewed his recollections of Sevastopol during the passage to Gaspra, L. N. had stirred up his fifty-year-old recollections, and they floated to the surface at the moment when the subconscious forces were at work.

One of the Crimean doctors, K. V. Volkov, who attended L. N. and who during the worsening had been summoned from Petrograd, where he was on business, relates thus his meeting with the sick L. N.:

> "In the large Gaspra house despondency and alarm reigned. On tiptoe I entered the large room, next to the dining room, where L. N.'s bed stood. He lay upon it in a fever, with parched lips, and restlessly fingered the edge of the knitted woollen blanket. Opening his eyes, he at once recognized me and, evidently continuing the course of his thought, said: 'Well, and that is good, the end is near... And I am still the same, I confess as before that which I acknowledged as truth when I was well and far from death...'"

At the beginning of February, during a temporary easing of L. N.'s health, the doctor Shchurovsky and L. N.'s sons Ilya and Mikhail left for Petrograd; taking leave of them, L. N. said that perhaps he would die, that for the last 25 years he had lived by that faith with which he would also die, and then added: "Let those near me ask me, when I am quite dying, whether my faith was good, whether it was just; if even in the last minutes it helped me, I will nod my head as a sign of agreement."

On the night from the 6th to the 7th the situation was almost hopeless. In semi-delirium he said: "now you will arrange everything well, you will spray the camphor, and I shall die."

Sometimes his strength left him and he groaned:

"How grievous it is, you do not die from dying and you do not recover." In the reminiscences of P. A. Boulanger several episodes from the time of this illness are recorded. He kept watch at nights and recalls thus those nocturnal hours:

> "I remember those nights. One cannot, of course, sleep; Lev Nikolaevich does not sleep either, and the hours drag on slowly, slowly. One must give the medicine; you give it, and Lev Nikolaevich inquires what hour it is. About 3 in the morning. At last outside it has begun to grow grey, some little bird has chirped, and Lev Nikolaevich says that it must be dawning, asks that the blind be opened, in order to meet the new day of life, no longer expected by him. Morning comes, and Lev Nikolaevich tries, with the help of others (the women's watch), to wash, to comb his hair, to put himself in order, as though there were nothing, no illness, no bed, and he would now begin his usual work or set about finishing that which had been taking shape within him in the stillness of the night...

He could in no way see in his illness that horror which we all who surrounded him saw, as can be seen from what he wrote down (that is, dictated) at this time. Here are some of his thoughts, dictated by him:

> "Fire both destroys and warms; so too does illness. When a healthy man tries to live well, to free himself from vices, from temptations, then this one does with effort, and it is as if one lifts up one oppressive side, while all the rest oppresses. But illness at once lifts up the whole of this dirty scale, and at once it becomes easy, and so dreadful is it to think that, as one knows by experience, as soon as the illness passes, it (this scale) will again settle down with all its weight."

> Sometimes, when you sit in the evening somewhere in a corner of the half-dark room — continues Pav. Aleks. Boulanger — and watch the slightest movement of the sick man, in order to help, or when it seems that he has fallen asleep, and you await his waking in order to give him the medicine, and you come up with the medicine when you see the weak movement of his hand, Lev Nikolaevich suddenly stops you:

> 'Not this for now,' he says, pointing to the medicine. And, seeing the imploring look, he adds: 'Later; take, my friend, some paper, write it down.'

> And he suddenly begins to dictate corrections for an addition to his latest works."

Further on Pav. Aleks. relates thus:

> "On 8 February he called me and dictated to me the preface to the soldier's and the officer's memoranda. After this dictation he grew so weak that he, as it were, fell into a complete prostration. All those around were horrified that he was losing his last strength. Until 9 in the evening he remained in such a condition, but at this time he again called me, asked me to read what had been dictated during the day, and, fearing evidently that it was not fated for him to say to men that which so tormented him, gathered his last strength and dictated corrections and changes to that which had been written during the day.
>
> Here is what was then dictated.
>
> "Every thinking man of our time cannot but see that out of the heavy and threatening position in which we find ourselves there are only two ways out: the first, although a very difficult one, a bloody revolution; the second — the acknowledgment by governments of their obligation not to go against the law of progress, not to defend the old, or, as with us, to return to the ancient, but, having understood the direction of the path along which mankind is moving, to lead their peoples along it.
>
> I have attempted to point out this path in two letters to Nicholas II. The first was written in the period of the most intense agitations of 1900–1901; the second I wrote now, at the beginning of January, but, unfortunately, the thoughts expressed by me in the first letter were taken as the frivolous dream of a fantast ignorant of life and of the profound science of state government.
>
> In the last letter I said that, besides granting the people the possibility of a religious movement and of the same kind of free communion of thought, the only path, in my opinion, to the resolution of the social question consists with us in Russia in the abolition of the right of property in land; that this abolition is possible by the transfer of all the taxes onto the land (admirably set forth and worked out by Henry George and his followers). It may very well be that I am mistaken — this question concerns all and therefore must be resolved by all — one thing is certain, that it is the business of the government not to care only that its position should not change, but boldly to take the central idea of progress and with all the forces it possesses to carry it into life. Only then will governments acquire in our time some kind of meaning and cease to be objects of hatred, aversion, and contempt to all those people who either do not enjoy their privileges or do not understand the significance of governmental activity.
>
> And such people now are almost everyone.
>
> I have made an attempt in the second letter to open the Emperor's eyes. But up to now I have no data to hope that this attempt has not only attained its aim, but would even be taken into consideration. And therefore, in view of the inevitability of the first way out, that is, of revolution, I now offer for distribution these two memoranda, in the hope that the thoughts contained in them will lessen the fratricidal slaughter toward which governments are now leading their peoples."

The government officials were greatly disturbed by the growing fascination which the personality of L. N. enjoyed, and by a possible demonstration in case of his death, and the dark forces began to stir at the bedside of the great man.

> "The dangerous position in which Lev Nikolaevich was became known, of course, to the public as well, and I remember," P. A. Boulanger continues his account, "one English newspaper had already been received, in which an obituary of him had been printed. This position was known to the Russian government too. In the family the question of the burial was discussed. They reckoned with the will of Lev Nikolaevich, who did not wish there to be any fuss whatever about his body, and therefore came to the conclusion that the burial ought to be carried out right there in the Crimea; and in view of the events that followed, a small plot of land in the neighbourhood was purchased for this purpose.
>
> Having learned of the possibility of Lev Nikolaevich's near death, Pobedonostsev took the most unexpected and incredible measures. It must be said that to the house at Gaspra which Lev Nikolaevich occupied with his family there adjoined a house chapel, which, of course, might be visited by the clergy. And so, in the most anxious minutes which those around were living through, the last act of Pobedonostsev — which showed thereby how little he was scrupulous in his means — consisted in this, that he gave an order to the local clergy that, as soon as it should become known of the demise of Lev Nikolaevich, a priest should enter the house occupied by L. N. (and to this he had the right, as I have just said), and, coming out from there, should announce to those around him and to the persons waiting at the gates that Count Tolstoy had repented before death, had returned to the bosom of the Orthodox church, had confessed and taken communion, and that the clergy and the church rejoiced at the return of the prodigal son to the bosom of the church.
>
> This monstrous lie was to fly round the whole of Russia and the whole world and to do that work which, over the course of decades, neither the Russian censorship nor the persecutions of Lev Nikolaevich's writings had been able to do.
>
> Pobedonostsev's order on this matter became known to those around Lev Nikolaevich, and I cannot convey here that feeling of indignation, of outrage, which these measures called forth in everyone, especially in these hard minutes. Much thought was given to how to prevent this revolting act of falsehood from being accomplished; some hot-headed people did not stop even at the idea of forcibly preventing the clergy from penetrating into the estate and the house, but at last they settled on the following: if the catastrophe should occur, to conceal Lev Nikolaevich's death from everyone, and at that time to send agreed-upon telegrams abroad and to the capitals of Russia, and to announce his death only when news should be received of what had happened having been published everywhere. Only in this way, it seemed, would it be possible to forestall the blow being prepared against the memory of Lev Nikolaevich."

Sofya Andreyevna, in her notes, relates thus of these churchly attempts to catch L. N. back into the church:

> "15 February, in the evening, I received a letter from the Petrograd Metropolitan Antony, exhorting me to persuade Lev Nikolaevich to return to the church, to be reconciled with the church, and to help him to die a Christian.
>
> I told Lyovochka of this letter, and he told me to write to Antony that his affair now is with God — write to him that my last prayer is this: 'From Thee I came, to Thee I go. Thy will be done.' And when I said that if God should send death, then one must die reconciled with all that is earthly, and with the church too, to this L. N. said to me: 'there can be no talk of reconciliation. I die without any enmity or malice, and what is the church? What reconciliation can there be with such an indefinite object?' Then L. N. sent Tanya to tell me that I should write nothing to Antony."

The prolonged illness accustomed Lev Nikolaevich to full submissiveness.

To his wife and daughter, who tended him, he said: "Now I have decided to expect nothing more. I kept expecting recovery, but now, what there is at present, that is what there is, and one must not look ahead. A prolonged illness is a good thing; there is time to prepare for death. I am ready for everything: ready to live, and ready to die."

> "In the evening I sat with L. N.," Sofya Andreyevna relates, "during the period of recovery, and he says: 'I keep composing verses; I have paraphrased "All is mine, said the gold," and I say:
>
> "All will I break," said force,
>
> "All will I rear up," said thought!'"

Through the whole of February and March the illness dragged on with a gradual improvement. At last, at the end of March, Marya Lvovna wrote to me:

> "...What am I to tell you about us: papa is still ill — this illness has still not ended and drags on. The other day there was again a new pleurisy and an inflamed focus, and therefore again a rise of temperature and weakness. But I can say with joy that, in spite of this, he is gaining strength, and the last days the progress is considerable. They have sent him a wheeled clinical chair, and now for three days he has been sitting and lying in it, and this distracts him and gladdens him. Now we wheel him up to the window, and he admires the sea and the mountains. In mood he is just as infinitely patient, kind, affectionate, and good, and for all of us around him it is not only a joy, but the most important spiritual benefit and lesson to see him thus. Sometimes one is dreadfully sorry for him, for his tormented, old, sick body, and one would so like somehow to help him. The illness has dragged on so that we do not know when it will be possible to leave for Yasnaya. The doctors think not before the end of May. The spring this year here is late, and therefore so far it is still impossible either to take him out into the sunshine or to open the windows — which could very much hasten his recovery. Well, as God shall grant. One thing I know — that whatever may be, we remain with him, wherever it may be."

To this letter there was appended a letter to me from L. N. in his own hand. After a touching greeting he wrote:

> "...Tomorrow it will be my 2nd month in bed — I cannot stand, but little by little my strength is increasing. Today for the first time I write myself. I have received from Binstock the translations of the 1st volume and your good preface and the *Lettres* and the *Carnet du soldat* (*). The translation is good. Evidently, one must live on a little longer. How good it would be if I always knew, as I do now, that the only meaning of the term, be it long or short, of my life is given to me only that I should serve the cause of God."

(* The *Letters* and the *Soldier's Memorandum*. *)

Hardly had L. N. recovered from the grave illness when he again gives himself entirely to the service of this cause of God. Long since concerned, for the good of the people, with the carrying out of a land reform on the system of the single tax of Henry George, he writes two letters, one after another, to the Grand Duke Nik. Mikh., thinking to interest the higher spheres through him. But these spheres were impenetrable.

And meanwhile a new trial awaited him. In the middle of April he fell ill with typhoid fever. Again alarm, again his earthly life hung by a thread, and again mighty forces overcame the illness, and to him and to us was granted a reprieve and the possibility of the joy of meeting.

During his illness he dictates profound thoughts:

> "5 May. Gaspra. For three and a half months I have not written. I was gravely ill and even now have not got over it.
>
> How clear it is, when one stands on the threshold of death, that it is undoubtedly so, that one cannot live otherwise. Ah, how beneficent is illness! It at least at times points out to us what we are and wherein lies the business of our life.
>
> May the will be done of Him by whose law I have lived in this world (in this form) and now, dying, depart from this world (from this form). This will I know only by the good which it has given me, and therefore, sure of its goodness, I give myself up to it calmly and, in so far as I believe, joyfully.
>
> Study the ancient religions, not in the sense in which various narrow-minded Letourneaus do — that, forsooth, see what stupidities people confessed (not we, the clever ones) — but in the sense of by what profound thoughts and beliefs ancient mankind lived.
>
> Today is 22 May. Gaspra. The typhoid has passed, but I still lie. I await the 3rd illness and death. In a very bad mood. There is something to write, but I put it off. Just now I pray, and prayer, as always, helps.
>
> 23, 24 May. Yesterday I was very weak; today better. I wrote a little of *To the Working People*. And it is beginning to take shape. They wanted to carry me out into the air, but there was a cold wind. I am ashamed that I behaved unkindly toward Tanya for advising me not to go out. I have moved over to the armchair. My legs seem not to be there.
>
> 25, 26, 27 May. For three days I have been in the air. At first 4, 5 hours, today 6 hours. Little by little I am getting over it. These were the throes of death, that is, of a new birth, and a rest has been given. Today I received the sad news of Suller's arrest. There was a Persian peddler, a fully educated man, who says that he is a Babist. Now it is past noon. Little by little I work on the address to the people. Not bad."

Here are several more thoughts from the May diary:

> "Formerly irreligious people were enemies of society, now they are its leaders.
>
> The safety of society is secured by the morality of its members, and morality is founded on religion. The government and the ruling classes wished to justify their immoral life by religion, and for this they perverted religion; and as soon as religion was perverted, so morality fell, and with the fall of morality the safety of society was more and more destroyed, so that the government and the ruling classes had to pervert religion more and more and to employ violence for the safeguarding of the safety no longer of the whole of society, but only of themselves. This very thing both did and do our Christian governments, and the position becomes worse and worse. At the present time it seems to the government to be without a way out."

At last, in June, he records:

> "I continue to pass the days in the air. I work. I have almost finished the address. I am recovering not badly, but I see that it is unreliable."

Sofya Andreyevna with joy informs her sister of Lev Nikolaevich's recovery.

> "...Lyovochka's typhoid has passed; it is simply a miracle that he has recovered from two mortal illnesses. They simply pulled him through. And the doctors here are simply astonishing! I have nowhere met better. Attentive and intelligent, they put their whole soul into helping, and all disinterestedly. The most intelligent is Altschuler, a Jew. And he is a splendid man, still young, 36 years old, of Moscow University. He is the chief one who treats Lyovochka and has influence on him. We all tended him; most zealous of all was Seryozha. Today he has left on business for Russia, and Ilyusha has come to replace him. After the typhoid, from fear of haemorrhage from the bowels, Lyovochka is forbidden to make any exertion, and so men are needed to turn him over and lift him. Lyovochka has fairly much strength; he is just now dictating his thoughts to Kolya Obolensky, reading the newspapers, eats willingly..."

Of his spiritual condition after the illness L. N. writes in detail to Chertkov:

> "...I am recovering from the typhoid too. The 5th week is going on. All is well, I even work two days, only I cannot, I will not say walk, but even stand. My legs seem not to be there. The weather was cold, but now it is wondrous, and for two days I have been going out into the air in the armchair. We await recovery and strengthening, in order to go to Yasnaya Polyana, which they reckon may be, perhaps, about 10 June. Masha has left; Tanya was here, and now Ilya is staying. All tend me beautifully. I very much want to work, and at very much.
>
> At first the bounds of the physical human being develop, expand, grow — faster than the spiritual being grows — childhood, boyhood; then the spiritual being catches up with the physical, and they go almost together — youth, maturity; then the physical bounds cease to expand, while the spiritual grows, expands, and, at last, the spiritual, not being contained, destroys the physical more and more, until it destroys it altogether and is set free. — I am in this last phase."

At this time L. N. again writes to the Bulgarian Shopov, who had refused military service, and in this letter there are interesting autobiographical data:

> "Thank you, dear Shopov, for writing often and for giving such good news. It is very pleasant to me to see your enthusiasm and your living hope for the speedy triumph of truth, but, having already gone through the path which you are going through, I want to tell you of those dangers which are met with on this path. I, at least, since I was born to the new, true life, have passed through the following stages:
>
> 1) The rapture of the knowledge of truth.
>
> 2) The desire and the hope to realize it at once.
>
> 3) Disenchantment in the possibility of the quick realization of truth in the external world, and the hope of realizing it within oneself, in one's own life.
>
> 4) The attempt to reconcile truth with worldly life — compromises.
>
> 5) Aversion before compromises and despair, or at least doubt of the truth of the teaching.
>
> 6) Finally, the consciousness that you are not called upon to change the world in the name of truth, that you cannot, even in your own life, realize truth as you would wish, but that you can, not caring what is being done in the world (this God does), not caring either how consistent you appear to people, you can according to your strength before God realize truth, that is, fulfil His will.
>
> And this alone gives full peace. These stages, it seems to me, every man passes through, being reborn to life. And the dangers on each of these stages you will see for yourself."

L. N.'s sojourn in the Crimea, his illness, and his communion with new people strengthened still more powerfully his influence upon Russian society. Here is how one young journalist wrote of him at this time:

> "There is in him something biblical — simple and severe — the thoughtful unhurriedness of profound thought, the beautiful calm of great energy, and an aphoristic mind that makes him kin to the great sages of antiquity.
>
> And there is much in him from us, from the age and from the present day: the intelligentsia's sensitiveness, the impetuous seeking of the truth, the Slav's sadness and the sharp pain over men, and the tears, and the anguish over the falsehood and the darkness of this life. The gigantic mind of a Goliath of thought with a soul as bright and unsteady as an infant's — what grandeur in this combination, what enigmaticalness in the union of these antitheses!"

On 25 June L. N. set out from Gaspra for Yasnaya Polyana. As far as Sevastopol they travelled by steamer, in order to avoid the jolting on the road, since L. N. was after all still very weak. We borrow the description of this return again from the reminiscences of his friend P. A. Boulanger, who accompanied him:

> "On the steamer the captain placed at the sick man's disposal a comfortable cabin, although the sea was still, calm, and the weather was so fine that there was no need of it, and Lev Nikolaevich passed all the time on deck, sitting in an armchair. On arrival at Sevastopol, in order to avoid the jolting during the passage from the landing-stage to the station over the most dreadful pavement, the sick man was conveyed in a boat, and at last, at about 4 in the afternoon, we safely reached the carriage that was awaiting us, and L. N. lay down to rest; there remained some four hours before the train's departure.
>
> There stood an unbearable heat, the roof of the carriage had grown dreadfully hot, there was nothing to breathe, and L. N. wished to go out into the air. I knew that next to the station there was a shady little railway garden, and I had earlier asked the station authorities whether it would be possible, in case of need, to make use of this little garden. The authorities were very obliging: anything you wish, anywhere you wish, everything is at your service.
>
> Taking Lev Nikolaevich by the arm, we wandered quietly toward this little garden, reached it at last, and Lev Nikolaevich with pleasure sat down on a bench to rest in the shade. Although we had walked only a short distance, he was very tired, and I had already begun to consider how I might arrange for him the possibility of lying down there. But hardly had we sat there a few minutes when, from the balcony of a house situated in the garden, a lady came down with a very serious, important air and asked us to withdraw.
>
> I protested, saying that we had been permitted to spend some time in this garden.
>
> But the lady, with a very imposing air, remarked to me: 'this is the garden of the chief of the district section, and it is not permitted for all sorts of people to loaf about here.'
>
> 'But allow me,' I implored, pointing to Lev Nikolaevich, 'at least to let the sick man rest a little.'
>
> 'Move along, move along,' she continued in a peremptory tone, 'otherwise I shall call the watchman.'
>
> I did not wish to give in, but L. N. rose and in a weary voice remarked to me: 'Leave it, why cause her unpleasantness, I can walk.'
>
> There was nothing to be done; we wandered out of the little garden and passed the rest of the time in the stuffy carriage. By the time of the train's departure a great many people had crowded into the station, wishing to look at L. N. one last time, to see him off. Around the carriage there was an inconceivable crush; it was hard to get through, and one had to make one's way to one's own carriage through the other carriages. Some 5 minutes before the train's departure two ladies stood in the doorway of the carriage and entreated to be let in to see Count Tolstoy. The carriage conductor called me. The face of one of the ladies seemed familiar to me. I asked what they wanted. Then this lady began, beseechingly, humbly, to explain to me. 'I want to ask his forgiveness; ah, how dreadful this is, do understand, he was in our garden today, and you, it seems, were with him? and it was I myself who told him that one could not be in the garden. I cannot forgive myself,' she said in despair, 'but I could in no way have thought that it was Tolstoy himself.'
>
> It was both ludicrous and vexing to me — I now recognized this important lady, her appearance was pitiful. But it was utterly impossible for her to pass through to Lev Nikolaevich: in the carriage there was bustle, a crush, things stood about over which even we found it hard to clamber. At last the second bell rang, and I hopelessly spread my hands before her. 'Then at least convey at least this bouquet from our garden and ask his forgiveness on my behalf.'
>
> A few minutes later the train moved off from Sevastopol, and two days later, on the 27th of June, Lev Nikolaevich safely arrived at Yasnaya Polyana."
