## Q1 — The English translation "A Great Iniquity"

**Title.** The essay went into English as **"A Great Iniquity"** (the
Russian title is Великий грех = "The Great Sin"; the subtitle in Russian
editions is «О земельной собственности» / "On Land Ownership"). The English
title leans on the moral-economy register ("iniquity") rather than the
literal "sin."

**Translators.** Translated by **Vladimir Grigorievitch Tchertkoff (Chertkov)
and Isabella Fyvie Mayo**, with Mayo supplying an accompanying note. So
**Chertkov IS involved** in the English translation — directly, as co-translator
(per the Wikisource and marxists.org/RevoltLib headers).

**First appearance.** First published in **The Times of London, 1 August 1905**,
as a long letter on the land question over Tolstoy's name. (The original Russian
is dated "Yasnaya Polyana, July 1905.")

**Other contemporary English editions / circulation.**
- **The Public** (the Chicago single-tax journal): printed the essay in full on
  **19 August 1905**, and a **48-page pamphlet** edition is referenced in that
  orbit — i.e. it entered the **American single-tax movement's** press, not only
  the British. (Search summary of The Public / cooperative-individualism scan;
  treat the 48-pp figure as reported-not-handled.)
- **Free Age Press** pamphlet edition: catalogue records show a Free Age Press
  edition (Christchurch, **1906**, 32 pp., trans. Tchertkoff & Mayo). Free Age
  Press was Chertkov's own English-language Tolstoy press — consistent with
  Chertkov being co-translator. Carried "NO RIGHTS RESERVED" (Tolstoy's standard
  no-copyright stance).
- Later American reprint: **"A Great Iniquity"** reissued as a Freeman Pamphlet
  by **B. W. Huebsch, 1920** (land tenure / land nationalization). (Reported.)

**On the specific "Land Values Publication Dept / Joseph Fels / single-tax
leagues" question — PARTIAL / could not fully confirm.** The essay clearly fed
the single-tax movement's press on **both sides of the Atlantic** (The Times →
The Public + pamphlet in the US; Free Age Press in the UK). I found the British
movement organ **"Land Values"** (the journal of the English single-tax /
land-value-tax campaign) carrying Tolstoy land-question material in 1905, and
**Joseph Fels** (the wealthy American single-tax financier active in Britain,
the Vacant Land Cultivation Society, the Fels Fund/Commission) was the era's
chief single-tax patron — but I could **not directly confirm** that the
"A Great Iniquity" pamphlet was issued specifically by a "Land Values
Publication Department" or financed by Fels. The confirmed pamphlet publisher is
**Free Age Press** (Chertkov). Flag for the dive: the Fels/Land-Values-Dept link
is **plausible but unverified** on this pass.

> Triangulation: **confirms + extends.** Confirms the primary record that the
> essay went out through Chertkov's channels (Free Age Press) and the single-tax
> press. Extends it by showing a *transatlantic* single-tax circulation (The
> Times → The Public + US pamphlet, plus the British movement's "Land Values").
> The Chertkov-as-co-translator fact is the hinge for the marquee finding: the
> same editor who cut the anarchist paragraph from the introduction is the one
> who carried the reform argument into English.

## Q2 — Tolstoy and Henry George: the received scholarly view

**The anchor scholarly source** is **Kenneth C. Wenzer, "Tolstoy's Georgist
Spiritual Political Economy (1897–1910): Anarchism and Land Reform," *The
American Journal of Economics and Sociology*, Vol. 56, No. 4 (Oct. 1997),
pp. 639–667** (special issue marking the centenary of George's death). This is
the most focused specialist treatment in English and the dive's primary
triangulation partner. Wenzer also edited a Henry George Centennial Trilogy
(Univ. of Rochester, 1997), vol. II being an anthology of Tolstoy's "spiritual
economics."

**When Tolstoy first read George.** Two datings in circulation, both **mid-1880s**:
- Victor Lebrun ("Tolstoy and George," 1956) says Tolstoy discovered George
  **"at the beginning of 1885."**
- Wenzer ties the deep engagement to the post-1897 period (George's death
  "spurred" Tolstoy to become "the world's most noteworthy exponent" of George),
  but treats the moral-reform groundwork as already laid (Posrednik publishing
  house, 1884).
- Caveat: scholars sometimes cite ~1885; Tolstoy's own diaries through the 1890s
  show repeated re-reading and rising enthusiasm (e.g. an April 1905 diary entry
  on re-reading George "in Nikolaev's edition" and "again I become elated").

**Works Tolstoy promoted.** Above all **Progress and Poverty (1879)** and
**Social Problems**. Wenzer notes Tolstoy's (unpublished) conclusion to *What Is
Art?* praised *Progress and Poverty* as "a perfect blend of art and science."

**S. D. Nikolaev (1861–1920)** is identified (Wenzer) as **"the foremost Russian
translator of Henry George."** Tolstoy studied George *in Nikolaev's editions*
and wrote the **1906 preface to the Russian edition of George's *Social
Problems*** translated by Nikolaev.

**Tolstoy's George-related writings (Wenzer's inventory).**
- *Letters on Henry George* / two letters on George and the land question (c. 1899–1900)
- *Where Is the Way Out?*, *Need It Be So?* (1900), *The Only Means* (1901)
- **To the Working People (Fall 1902)** — "the most practical and just solution
  is George's single tax"
- *The Light That Shines Through Darkness* (reworked late 1902) — an unused act praises the single tax
- **A Great Iniquity / The Great Sin (Aug 1905)** — the "direct Georgist appeal,"
  "Land is the basis of all social questions"
- *The Project of Henry George* (1905) — synopsis of Tolstoy's Georgism
- **1906 preface(s)** to George (the *Social Problems* Russian edition)
- *An Appeal to Russians* (1906), *The Meaning of the Russian Revolution* (c. 1906)
- *Three Days in the Country* (1909–10); a famous Oct 1909 diary entry on a dream
  conversation with Henry George
- **"The One Thing Needful"** — could **not confirm** specific scholarly framing
  of this title as a George text on this pass; flag as open. (Multiple Tolstoy
  pieces carry near-identical titles; verify against PSS before attributing.)

**Mainstream biographers (Bartlett, Wilson, Maude) — PARTIAL.** Confirmed that
Rosamund Bartlett (*Tolstoy: A Russian Life*, 2011) and A. N. Wilson (*Tolstoy*,
1988) both treat Tolstoy's George enthusiasm within the land-reform / agrarian
strand, and Aylmer Maude supplied the "bridge to Vancouver Island" analogy that
Wenzer quotes (see Q3). I did **not** pull exact page-level quotations from
Bartlett/Wilson/Maude on this pass — flag as available-but-unquoted.

> Triangulation: **confirms.** The received view squarely confirms the dive's
> primary finding that George/single-tax was a *lifelong, repeatedly restated*
> Tolstoy commitment, not a one-off 1905 intervention. The Nikolaev-translation
> dependency and the 1902 / 1905 / 1906 cluster are corroborated.

## Q3 — THE CENTRAL TENSION: Christian anarchism vs. a state-administered single tax (most important)

This is where scholarship is genuinely **split**, which is exactly what the
dive's "complicates (not contradicts)" hypothesis predicts.

### The text's own conditional framing (confirmed)
The English "A Great Iniquity" contains the conditional clause the dive relies
on. Per the marxists.org/RevoltLib text, Tolstoy writes that **"under the
existing State organization and compulsory taxation it is impossible to invent
any other better, more just, practical, and peaceful solution."** And —
critically — **the translator's footnote in that very edition flags the tension
explicitly**, noting Tolstoy recommends George's scheme **"only under conditions
of State organization and compulsory taxation,"** and that this sits in tension
with his anarchist-leaning rejection of coercive government. So the
"concession-to-existing-conditions" reading is **embedded in the contemporary
English apparatus itself**, not a later scholarly invention.

### Reading A — "concession / weakness / transitional" (Wenzer; the text's own footnote)
Wenzer treats the contradiction head-on and lands on a **two-stage / lesser-evil**
reading:
- He records Tolstoy calling George's system a **"weakness"** and a
  **"concession"**: *"Henry George is a concession, a weakness… we say that
  Henry George is still better. But, I repeat, this is weakness."*
- He uses Maude's **bridge analogy** (a man bound for Vancouver Island who
  accepts Montreal as the next practical stage): *"The great majority of people
  still believe in governments and legality — then let them, at least, see that
  they get good laws."*
- Wenzer's synthesis: a two-stage vision — **(1) pragmatic/immediate:** adopt the
  single tax as the only workable reform given current political reality;
  **(2) ultimate/spiritual:** moral transformation eventually lets the state
  "melt away."
- But Wenzer also concedes it stayed **unresolved**: *"So caught up with Georgist
  thought, Tolstoy even modified his strictures against the state as a viable
  means of effecting change."* He notes a 1906 letter where Tolstoy extends the
  no-property principle to **all** property (not just land), which cuts against
  George — so Tolstoy "compartmentalized" rather than philosophically resolved it.

### Reading B — "genuine, foundational, permanent" (Georgist-movement reading)
The single-tax movement's own commentary (Prosper Australia / Victor Lebrun,
1956) reads it the **opposite** way: as a **genuine, permanent solution**, not a
temporary compromise — Tolstoy compared land liberation to the liberation of the
serfs ("This is the liberation of the earth from private ownership"). In this
telling there is **no** "weakness" hedging; the enthusiasm is foundational.
(Note: this is movement-aligned commentary reading Tolstoy as a fellow Georgist —
treat as an interested party, contrast-to-read-critically per dive method.)

### Net
The scholarship does **not** unanimously call it either a clean contradiction or
a clean exception. Wenzer (the specialist) frames it as a **concession/weakness
within a transitional two-stage logic that Tolstoy never fully resolved**; the
Georgist movement reads it as a **genuine foundational endorsement**. Both
camps agree he endorsed a state fiscal mechanism; they disagree on whether that
*compromises* his anti-statism or merely *defers* it.

> Triangulation: **complicates — strong support for the marquee hypothesis.**
> The received view independently arrives at "concession to existing conditions
> under a state-and-taxes premise" (Wenzer's "weakness"; the contemporary
> translator's footnote; Tolstoy's own conditional clause). This *complicates*
> rather than *contradicts* his anti-statism — exactly the dive's frame. The
> corpus goes *below* where scholarship stops: scholarship debates Tolstoy's
> intent in the abstract; the dive can show the mechanism concretely — **Chertkov
> physically cut the anarchist paragraph** ("the life of the people is formed not
> by external forms but by the inner activity of individuals") from the
> introduction because it undercut the reform argument. That editorial act is the
> tension made material, and it is the kind of below-the-surface evidence Wenzer's
> "unresolved / compartmentalized" verdict gestures at but cannot pin down.

## Q4 — Reception of The Great Sin, 1905

**Russian first publication + the editorial disclaimer (confirmed).** Published
in the **July 1905 issue of «Русская мысль» (Russian Thought)**, a liberal
journal. The editors **added a note distancing themselves from Tolstoy on
"существенных и важных вопросах" / "substantial and important questions,"** and
(per the tolstoy.ru journalism guide) **omitted the third introductory
paragraph** — the one beginning «Так думают либералы…» ("Thus liberals think…").
So both the *Russian* editors AND *Chertkov* on the English side performed
**editorial surgery on the introduction** — a notable convergence the dive can
exploit. (Tolstoy had wanted it in *Russian Thought* or *Vestnik Evropy*.)

**Other 1905 Russian editions.** Reprinted the same year as a separate
**Posrednik** brochure, and via **«Свободное слово» (Svobodnoe slovo / Free
Word)**, Chertkov's Russian-language émigré press, under the title «Великий грех»
with the subtitle «О земельной собственности».

**1905-Revolution / agrarian context (confirmed framing).** Written as the First
Russian Revolution broke and the **land question became the axis of Russian
politics**. Tolstoy's line — give everyone equal *use*-right to land, abolish
land *property* morally — is positioned by the sources **against both** the main
party programs:
- **Socialist-Revolutionaries (SRs)** — the peasant-land party — wanted
  **socialization** of the land (distribution to peasant communes/tenants).
- **Marxists / Bolsheviks** wanted **nationalization** (state ownership; later
  collectivization).
- Tolstoy's George-derived position rejected *both* state-socialist and
  capitalist solutions as "sinful" — he framed the single tax as the morally just
  middle that liberates land from private ownership without a violent
  redistribution or a coercive state apparatus.

**Foreign reception via The Times.** The Times letter (1 Aug 1905) drew European
attention to the land question; it was picked up by the **single-tax press** (The
Public, US, 19 Aug 1905; British movement organ "Land Values"). I did **not**
find a substantial *critical* foreign review on this pass — the foreign uptake
documented is mainly **movement amplification** rather than independent critique.
Flag: contemporary *liberal/SD/SR* polemical replies to the essay specifically —
named and quoted — could **not be confirmed** here; the documented reaction is the
*Russian Thought* editors' disclaimer plus the single-tax press. Worth a deeper
Russian-language pass if the dive wants named respondents.

> Triangulation: **confirms + complicates.** Confirms the primary record (July
> 1905 *Russian Thought*, editorial disclaimer, Posrednik/Svobodnoe slovo
> reprints, agrarian-revolution timing). Complicates the "lone prophet" picture
> by showing the essay was simultaneously (a) hedged by liberal editors who cut a
> paragraph and (b) embraced by the single-tax movement — landing in a crossfire
> between SR socialization and Marxist nationalization that the dive can map. The
> twin paragraph-cuts (Russian editors drop the "liberals think" paragraph;
> Chertkov drops the anarchist paragraph) are a strong, under-reported parallel.

## Q5 — Henry George biographical facts (for an entity page)

- **Henry George (b. 2 Sept 1839, Philadelphia — d. 29 Oct 1897, New York City).**
- American **political economist, journalist, social reformer**.
- Major work: ***Progress and Poverty* (1879)** — sold in the millions; asked why
  material progress left poverty intact, and located the cause in **private
  ownership of land** absorbing the gains of progress as rent.
- **Remedy: the "single tax"** — a tax on **land values** (land-value taxation)
  set high enough to fund government from rent alone, abolishing other taxes;
  basis of the **single-tax / Georgist movement**.
- Other work cited by Tolstoy: ***Social Problems***.
- **1886 New York City mayoral run:** finished **second**, ahead of Theodore
  Roosevelt (and behind Abram Hewitt). Ran again in **1897** and **died during
  that campaign** (stroke), 29 Oct 1897.
- George's death (1897) is the event Wenzer credits with galvanizing Tolstoy into
  the movement's leading literary champion.

> Triangulation: **confirms / context.** Standard biographical scaffolding for the
> entity page; no tension with the primary record. Note for the page: George was
> emphatically **not** an anarchist — he wanted a *strong* tax-financed public
> sphere — which is precisely what makes Tolstoy's embrace the puzzle Q3 addresses.
