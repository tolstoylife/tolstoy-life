# Scholarship — Art & aesthetics satellites (received view)

Phase-3 deliverable for the corpus-dive *Art & aesthetics satellites* (four short Tolstoy pieces that apply the *What Is Art?* (1897–98) religious-art test to specific cases). This file assembles the conventional / secondary scholarly view on each piece and triangulates it against the dive's primary close-reads. **English-first scholarship; the mainstream is here as a CONTRAST to read critically, never as the dive's own voice** — every view below is attributed to a named source. Three of the four pieces are minor occasional texts with little or no dedicated scholarship; that thinness is stated plainly rather than papered over.

The four pieces:
1. **Preface to the Works of Maupassant** (1893–94) — the seedbed of *What Is Art?*; first states the "three conditions."
2. **Preface to the English edition of *What Is Art?*** (1898) — Maude's English text named the only complete, uncensored one.
3. **On Gogol** (1909 article + 1888 unfinished note) — vindicates the penitent Gogol against the radical-critic consensus.
4. **Preface to Carpenter's "Modern Science"** (1898) — the science twin of the art test.

---

## Summary (received view of the cluster)

Mainstream Tolstoy scholarship does not treat these four as a "cluster" at all; it treats *What Is Art?* as the centre and these pieces as satellites of uneven visibility. The **Maupassant preface** is the one with a settled received status: Aylmer Maude's editorial apparatus and the standard collected-essays editions (Maude, *Tolstoy on Art*; *Essays and Letters*) present it as a "discriminating preface" that states the very "three conditions" — moral relation, clearness, sincerity — later carried into *What Is Art?* chapter 15, so secondary sources implicitly read it as the treatise's germ (Maude; ccel.org/marxists.org reprints of Maude's text). The **English-WIA preface** is well documented as the authorial note fronting the *first uncensored* text of the treatise; the standard reference (Wikipedia, *What Is Art?*; Raptis/Biblio rare-book records; Project Gutenberg) confirms the **Brotherhood Publishing Company, London, 1898** as the first English edition and Maude as the authorised translator. The **Carpenter preface** has a small but real science-critique literature (e.g. a *Visions for Sustainability* article on "Lev Tolstoy and modern science") that reads Tolstoy's endorsement as helping spread Carpenter beyond English. The **Gogol** pieces are the thinnest: the 1909 centenary article is rarely discussed in English at all. Across the cluster, critics (e.g. Bayley, Jahn) read *What Is Art?* itself as over-reaching — Tolstoy famously condemns Beethoven, Shakespeare, and his own *War and Peace* — so the prefaces' case-by-case verdicts inherit that reputation for over-application.

## Per-piece received view

### 1. Preface to the Works of Maupassant (1893–94)

- **Genesis status.** The received view, carried chiefly through **Aylmer Maude's** editorial framing, is that this preface is where Tolstoy first set out the "three conditions of true artistic creation" that *What Is Art?* would systematise. Maude (*Tolstoy on Art*, 1924) calls it "an important and discriminating preface to Guy de Maupassant's works which greatly interested Tolstoy" and dates it 1894, placing it in the same volume as the *What Is Art?* prefaces — an editorial adjacency that signals the connection without arguing it at length (Maude, *Tolstoy on Art*, archive.org). The English text of the essay states the triad verbatim: "(1) a correct, that is, a moral relation of the author to the subject, (2) the clearness of exposition, or the beauty of form, and (3) sincerity" (Maude trans., reprinted ccel.org / marxists.org, 1894). Modern summaries echo this mapping onto *What Is Art?* ch. 15's "three conditions of contagiousness" (individuality, clearness, sincerity).
- **Standard English title / translation.** The essay travels under titles like **"The Works of Guy de Maupassant"** and **"Introduction to the Works of Guy de Maupassant,"** in **Aylmer Maude's** translation. It is anthologised in Maude's *Tolstoy on Art* (Oxford, 1924), in *Recollections and Essays* (World's Classics, OUP — a 1937/1961 reissue family), and in *Essays and Letters* (World's Classics; the 1903 printing associated with Grant Richards). [Maude, *Tolstoy on Art*; Books-In-Order/dealer records — see Sources.]
- **The Tolstoy–Maupassant relationship.** Scholarship (and Tolstoy's own essay) records that Turgenev handed Tolstoy *La maison Tellier* in 1881; Tolstoy was cold to it, then reversed on *Une vie* (1883) — the relationship is one of qualified admiration, "real talent + sincerity, no moral relation." This is the standard reading and is consonant with the dive's primary findings.

### 2. Preface to the English edition of *What Is Art?* (1898)

- **Censorship history (well documented).** The received account matches the dive's close-read closely: every Russian lifetime edition of *What Is Art?* was mutilated, first by the secular censor and then by the spiritual (church) censor, and Maude's English translation was therefore the first complete, uncensored text. The Wikipedia article (*What Is Art?*) states the treatise "was completed in Russian in 1897 but first published in English in 1898 due to difficulties with the Russian censors," and Maude's own headnote (quoted widely) reproduces Tolstoy's preface: "This book of mine, *What is Art?* appears now for the first time in its true form … in each case it has been so mutilated by the 'Censor.'" Maude is repeatedly described as having produced "the first unexpurgated version of the work to appear in any language" (Aylmer and Louise Maude, Wikipedia).
- **Publisher — Brotherhood Publishing Co. CONFIRMED; the brief's "Grant Richards" is the wrong edition.** Independent rare-book records name **The Brotherhood Publishing Company, London, 1898** as the first English edition, translated by A. Maude (Raptis Rare Books; Biblio.com). This corroborates the dive commentary's **Brotherhood Publishing Co. / *The New Order*** against the dive brief's "Grant Richards." Grant Richards is associated instead with the later **World's Classics *Essays and Letters* (1903)** — a different, collected-essays volume — which is the likely source of the brief's confusion.
- **Maude as authorised translator.** Standard accounts (Maude bio; Online Books Page) describe Maude as Tolstoy's authorised English translator who worked in close consultation with the author — the same picture the dive's commentary gives (the ~23-letter query exchange).

### 3. On Gogol (1909 article + 1888 unfinished note)

- **Thin scholarship; the 1909 article barely surfaces in English.** A dedicated English-language treatment of Tolstoy's 1909 Gogol article was **not found**; English search returns the *Selected Passages* reception, the Belinsky letter, and general Gogol–Tolstoy comparative work, not the occasional centenary piece. This matches the close-read's "likely untranslated / minor occasional piece" flag.
- **The Tolstoy–Gogol relationship and the *Selected Passages*.** The received view confirms the dive's frame: Gogol's *Selected Passages from Correspondence with Friends* (1847) "was heartily appreciated by Leo Tolstoy, when he was himself engaged in a religious quest — the book was, in fact, a program of social Christianity," and Belinsky "castigated Gogol as a 'proponent of the knout, apostle of ignorance, champion of obscurantism, panegyrist of Tatar ways'" (zenosbooks.com bibliographic note on *Selected Passages*; Belinsky's 1847 letter, marxists.org). So the secondary literature independently supports the close-read's "Tolstoy vindicates the penitent Gogol against the Belinsky consensus."
- **Self-recognition reading.** The idea that Tolstoy "recognised himself" in Gogol's despised religious turn is consonant with mainstream framing (Tolstoy reading the book during his own religious quest) but is **not, in the sources found, stated as a developed scholarly thesis about the 1909 article specifically** — it is the dive's own primary-grounded contribution. The 1909 Gogol birth-centenary ("гоголевские дни") is the established occasion.

### 4. Preface to Carpenter's "Modern Science" (1898)

- **A small, real science-critique literature.** Tolstoy's preface is the most-discussed of the three minor pieces. A scholarly article, **"Lev Tolstoy and modern science"** (*Visions for Sustainability*, Univ. of Turin OJS), treats it directly; secondary notes record that **"Tolstoy's imprimatur helped to spread 'Modern Science: A Criticism' beyond the English-speaking world"** and that Tolstoy regarded Carpenter as **"a worthy successor to Carlyle and Ruskin"** (MIT redingtn course pages on Carpenter; *Journal of Leo Tolstoi* notes, marxists.org).
- **The Tolstoy–Carpenter relationship.** The received picture is an **intellectual/epistolary** connection, not a personal one: Tolstoy received Carpenter's *Civilisation: its Cause and Cure* in 1896 via the English Tolstoyan **John Coleman Kenworthy** (Carpenter's request), read the "Modern Science" essay, called it "splendid," and prefaced the Russian translation. **No documented meeting** between Tolstoy and Carpenter surfaced. The translator was Tolstoy's eldest son **Sergei Lvovich Tolstoy** (some English notes loosely say "Count Sergius Tolstoy" / even "older brother" — the latter is an error conflating son Sergei Lvovich with brother Sergei Nikolaevich; the dive's "eldest son" is correct).
- **Carpenter's dates and standing.** **Edward Carpenter (1844–1929)**, English socialist writer, poet, and early gay-rights figure — confirmed across sources (edwardcarpenter.net; Internet Archive *Civilisation*). His standing as a Carlyle/Ruskin-line social critic is exactly why his anti-positivist science essay appealed to the late Tolstoy.

## Cluster framing (how scholarship treats the satellites)

There is **no settled scholarly category** of "the *What Is Art?* prefaces/satellites"; the four pieces are handled separately and unevenly. What unites them in the secondary literature is only that *What Is Art?* is the gravitational centre and these are read, where read at all, as **application or extension** of its doctrine — the Maupassant preface as the doctrine's *germ* (Maude's editorial placement), the English preface as its *authorised vessel*, the Carpenter preface as its *science analogue* (the *Visions for Sustainability* line). The Gogol pieces sit almost outside this frame in English. The critical commonplace that the religious-art test **over-reaches** — Tolstoy disqualifying Beethoven, Wagner, Shakespeare, Dante, and even *War and Peace* (Bayley; standard summaries) — is the lens through which any case-by-case verdict in these prefaces is liable to be read as doctrinaire. The dive's close-reads push back productively here: they show Tolstoy applying the test *more discriminatingly* than the "over-reaching" caricature predicts (e.g., the Gogol read rescues only the religious *feeling*, not the moralising fiction). That nuance is the dive's own, primary-grounded contribution, not a received view — flagged so it is not mistaken for mainstream consensus.

- **Tolstoy's Gogol articles in English: still LIKELY UNTRANSLATED.** No English translation of either the 1909 article or the 1888 note surfaced; English coverage addresses only Gogol's *Selected Passages* (Tolstoy's object), not Tolstoy's two articles. Keep both on the dive's translation-gaps ledger as candidate gaps; do not assert "translated."
- **Carpenter translator naming: minor source error to carry, not adopt.** English notes variously say "Count Sergius Tolstoy" or even "older brother"; the PSS commentary (dive primary) is authoritative — translator = **Sergei Lvovich Tolstoy, eldest son**. - **Tolstoy–Carpenter personal contact: none found.** Relationship is intellectual/epistolary, mediated by Kenworthy; no record of a meeting surfaced. State as "no documented meeting," not "never met."
- **Genuinely thin scholarship (honest note).** Of the four, only the Maupassant preface and the English-WIA preface have a solid received literature. The **Carpenter** preface has a small science-critique niche; the **Gogol** pieces have essentially **no dedicated English scholarship** — the secondary material is about Gogol's reception, not Tolstoy's articles. The dive's primary close-reads are, for Gogol especially, ahead of the English secondary literature, so their readings should be carried as primary-grounded contributions, not confirmed-by-consensus.
