# Scholarly context: Hadji Murat (Хаджи-Мурат, 1896–1904)

_Phase-3 scholarly-context gather for the novel-dive. Purpose: map the received/mainstream view so the dive can attribute it and read it critically — not confirm it. Assembled 2026-06-09._

---

## Received view (summary)

The dominant scholarly consensus treats *Hadji Murat* as Tolstoy's last major work of fiction and, paradoxically, as the aesthetic capstone of a career whose public author had renounced art. Harold Bloom, in *The Western Canon* (1994), called it "my personal touchstone for the sublime of prose fiction, to me the best story in the world, or at least the best that I have ever read," and placed it at the centre of what he called the Democratic Age's canon. Bloom argued that strangeness — "originality, in the sense of strangeness" — is what makes a work canonical, and that *Hadji Murat* "centers" the canon in that sense. This endorsement has been among the most quoted in popular reception and helped reintroduce the novella to Anglophone readers.

Biographical scholarship — Rosamund Bartlett (*Tolstoy: A Russian Life*, 2010), A. N. Wilson (*Tolstoy*, 1988/2001), Henri Troyat (*Tolstoy*, French 1965, tr. 1967), and Ernest J. Simmons (*Introduction to Tolstoy's Writings*, 1968) — consistently frames the novella as a "return" to the Caucasian energies of Tolstoy's youth, written against the grain of his own public teaching. Bartlett describes Tolstoy's late career as "lit up with flashes of genius," of which *Hadji Murat* is the most Caucasian. Wilson notes Tolstoy "wrote perhaps his most powerfully beautiful piece, *Hadji Murat*, in his last years, after he had utterly rejected writing as a worthy occupation." The work is widely described as free of the heavy didacticism that marks the other late fiction (*The Kreutzer Sonata*, *Resurrection*, *Father Sergius*), and that absence of overt moralising is treated as both the novella's greatest artistic strength and its most awkward biographical fact.

The "anti-imperialist masterpiece" framing is the other major mainstream label. The novella is read as a symmetrical indictment of two forms of absolutism — Russian autocracy under Nicholas I and Caucasian theocratic power under Shamil — with Hadji Murat caught between them. This reading was reinforced by the renewed geopolitical resonance of the work after the Russian-Chechen wars of 1994–2009, which brought the 19th-century Caucasian conflict into sharp contemporary focus. Mainstream commentary across that period emphasised the novella's anti-war, anti-state critique as speaking directly to the post-Soviet Caucasus.

The reception is not without internal tension. Post-colonial and empire-studies scholarship has since complicated the simple "anti-imperialist Tolstoy" story, and formalist criticism attends to the work's construction in ways that are not primarily political. These strands are mapped below.

## By theme

### 1. Overall standing: "return to art" and Harold Bloom

Harold Bloom (*The Western Canon*, 1994; the phrase "personal touchstone for the sublime" also appears in *How to Read and Why*, 2000) is the most-cited advocate of the novella's supreme canonical status. His claim that it is "the best story in the world" circulates widely and anchors the popular reception. The Britannica entry on Tolstoy's fiction after 1880 describes the novella as "a brilliant narrative about the Caucasus reminiscent of Tolstoy's earliest fiction."

Biographers — Bartlett, Wilson, Troyat, Simmons — all treat the work as the outstanding late artistic achievement, distinguished by "studied simplicity and a minimum of literary adornment" (Simmons). The Joshua Grasso essay at Medium (2023) summarises the received framing well: Tolstoy "admitted to a friend that he had to 'write in on the sly from myself,'" acknowledging it "betrayed his narrow, didactic aims for art," and yet the work is regarded as proof that his artistic powers were "undiminished at age seventy-six."

Richard Freeborn's chapter "The long short story in Tolstoy's fiction" in *The Cambridge Companion to Tolstoy* (ed. Donna Tussing Orwin, CUP 2002) discusses *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*, *The Kreutzer Sonata*, and *Father Sergius* as exemplifying "art as infection," but does not — based on available secondary description — discuss *Hadji Murat* in the same terms, which is itself significant: the novella's relationship to the infection doctrine is apparently not a primary focus of the Companion chapter that most directly addresses the late short fiction.

---

### 2. The marquee question: does Hadji Murat betray or fulfil What Is Art?

This is a genuinely contested area, and scholarship divides roughly into three camps.

**Camp A — Hadji Murat as fulfilment of the What Is Art criteria.** Ernest J. Simmons calls it "almost a perfect example of the 'good universal art' that Tolstoy had acclaimed" in *What Is Art?*, specifically the second-highest category (good universal art, below good universal religious art). This reading holds that the novella's sincerity, its cross-cultural emotional reach (the reader feels alongside Russian peasant soldier, Chechen warrior, Avar villager, and Tsar alike), and its lack of class-specific exclusivity are precisely what the *What Is Art?* framework demands. The "infection" — transmission of shared human feeling — is achieved without didacticism, and that is counted a formal virtue, not a violation of doctrine.

**Camp B — Hadji Murat as deliberate violation of late aesthetic standards.** David Herman's "Khadzhi-Murat's Silence" (*Slavic Review* 64.1, Spring 2005, pp. 1–23) argues that the novella "stands alone in Tolstoi's oeuvre in flagrant violation of his late ethical and aesthetic standards" and is "an unprecedentedly dark apprehension of the human condition." Herman's central reading is that silence — literal, near-, figurative, implicit — structures the work as "an unspoken critique of all nontrivial language, narrative, moralizing, and teaching." On this reading the work does not satisfy the *What Is Art?* criteria; it enacts a withdrawal from the whole project of moral prescription.

**Camp C — the biographical/private-indulgence framing.** Tolstoy himself oscillated. His diaries record alternating elation ("I wrote two chapters very well") and shame ("It is a shame to write trifles"). He told a correspondent he had to "write in on the sly from myself." He did not intend to publish it and withheld the manuscript until the day he left Yasnaya Polyana for the last time (28 October 1910). Grasso (2023) follows Wilson in reading this as Tolstoy "indulg[ing] in aristocratic vices" even while preaching against them — a personal relapse, not a theoretical reconciliation.

Caryl Emerson's chapter "Tolstoy's Aesthetics" in *The Cambridge Companion to Tolstoy* (2002) analyses the *What Is Art?* framework on its own terms, identifying internal contradictions in the infection theory (the problem of duration, performer agency, polyphony), but does not — based on available secondary description — directly adjudicate whether *Hadji Murat* satisfies or violates its criteria. Emerson is a key voice for the theoretical instability of Tolstoy's aesthetics; her work provides the conceptual framework within which the Hadji Murat tension is best located, but the direct application to this novella appears to be more implied than stated in her published work.

**Status of the question:** The tension is widely acknowledged but not exhaustively argued in one place. Simmons (1968) and Herman (2005) are the most direct opposing witnesses. The dive should treat this as genuinely open in the secondary literature and engage with the primary evidence directly.

### 3. The mirrored-despots / political reading

Tolstoy himself articulated the parallel in conversation: he described the "curious parallelism" between Nicholas I and Shamil as "two poles of governmental absolutism — the Asiatic and the European." This framing is confirmed and reproduced across the received literature. The Chapter 15 (Nicholas I) was the target of tsarist censorship: censor Alexey Belgard found Nicholas "subjected to inadmissible, extremely rude and insulting" treatment; the first Russian edition reduced it to a single sentence with "." placeholders throughout.

Eric M. Souder's thesis "The Circassian Thistle: Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy's Khadzhi Murat and the Evolving Russian Empire" (Miami University/Ohio, 2014; available at circassianworld.com) examines the creation, publication, and reception of the novella as anti-imperial literature in both the Imperial and Soviet contexts, tracing how the text was absorbed into and then contested by official canon-building.

Edyta M. Bojanowska ("Was Tolstoi a Colonial Landlord? The Dilemmas of Private Property and Settler Colonialism on the Bashkir Steppe," *Slavic Review* 81.2, 2022, pp. 324–348; winner of the 2022 Ab Imperio Award) complicates the received "anti-colonial conscience" framing, arguing that Tolstoy's Samara estate operated within Russia's settler colonialism in Bashkiria and that he was "perplexingly indifferent to the suffering of the semi-nomadic Bashkirs" displaced by Russian settlers — raising the question whether the author of *Hadji Murat* held a coherent anti-imperial position or an inconsistent one.

Rebecca Ruth Gould's "Topographies of Anticolonialism: The Ecopoetical Sublime in the Caucasus from Tolstoy to Mamakaev" (*Comparative Literature Studies* 50.1, 2013, pp. 87–107) reads *Hadji Murat* alongside Chechen literary texts to examine how Caucasian topography shapes anticolonial poetics. Gould elsewhere describes Tolstoy's Caucasian writings as "ethnographic footnotes informing the reader about the history, languages, and customs of Russia's enemies" — a reading that cuts against unqualified celebration of the novella as anti-imperialist by flagging its ethnographic gaze.

The novella's renewed resonance after the first and second Chechen wars (1994–96, 1999–2009) is noted across popular and scholarly commentary, though detailed academic studies specifically focused on the 1990s–2000s reception surge have not surfaced in this search.

### 4. The Formalist reception

Viktor Shklovsky discussed *Hadji Murat* in *Energy of Delusion: A Book on Plot* (1981/82; tr. Dalkey Archive, 2007). Shklovsky called *Hadji Murat* "the best" among Tolstoy's works and argued that no "final" version of the novella exists — Tolstoy simply stopped. Shklovsky's title itself derives from a phrase used by Tolstoy to describe the impulse to write fiction as though it were "true": the novelist must believe in the "delusion," must generate energy from it. This phrase thus connects Shklovsky's late theoretical project directly to Tolstoy's late-career ambivalence about fiction.

Shklovsky developed his concept of *ostranenie* (defamiliarisation) from Tolstoy texts (principally "Kholstomer"), and while the Keith Gessen NYRB essay on Shklovsky ("The Reckless Founding Formalist," *NYRB*, 24 April 2014) does not discuss *Hadji Murat* directly, it confirms that Tolstoy was Shklovsky's primary lab for formalist analysis throughout his career.

Boris Eikhenbaum's multivolume Tolstoy biography (*The Young Tolstoi*, 1922; *Tolstoy in the Fifties*, 1928; subsequent volumes) is the other great Formalist monument. Eikhenbaum wrote on Tolstoy for forty years. The PMLA article "Boris Eikhenbaum's Unfinished Work on Tolstoy: A Dialogue with Soviet History" (Cambridge Core) notes that after 1928 Eikhenbaum shifted from immanent formal analysis toward literary biography and social context, eventually framing Tolstoy's ideas in relation to utopian socialism. The specific Formalist analysis of *Hadji Murat*'s construction (its multi-perspectival "peepshow" technique, the thistle frame as device) is not well documented in the secondary sources reached by this search; the thesis that Shklovsky and Eikhenbaum made *Hadji Murat* a centrepiece of Formalist analysis is plausible but requires direct verification in their texts.

The thistle frame: general criticism identifies the opening thistle (a "Tartar" thistle, the variety so named by Russian soldiers) as a direct symbol of Hadji Murat's resilience — "hurt, twisted, beaten, and betrayed, but still proudly standing." Scholarship on its compositional function as a framing device (what the dive brief called the "peepshow") is mentioned in context of Souder's thesis and in Tolstoy's own 1896 diary entry (he saw a burdock while riding and the image became the germ of the novella), but sustained Formalist analysis of this specifically is not well sourced in this search.

### 5. Publication and reception history

Tolstoy wrote *Hadji Murat* between 1896 and 1904, revising obsessively: the first thirteen chapters were rewritten five times; Chapter 15 (Nicholas I) eight times. He did not intend to publish and withheld the manuscript until 28 October 1910, his last day at Yasnaya Polyana.

After Tolstoy's death (20 November 1910 O.S.), his daughter Aleksandra and Vladimir Chertkov arranged simultaneous publication in 1911–12: an abridged/censored Russian edition (I. D. Sytin, Moscow) and a fuller Berlin Russian edition (J. Ladyschnikoff Verlag). The censored Moscow edition replaced the Nicholas I material with full stops throughout; the Berlin edition — Chertkov's standard strategy for Tolstoy's suppressed work — preserved the complete text. The first fully uncensored complete Russian text appeared in 1917. Over 250 editions have been published since 1912.

Souder (2014) examines this dual publication as a deliberate strategy to circumvent censorship, situating it within Chertkov's broader pattern of protecting Tolstoy's posthumous corpus. PY Rare Books (pyrarebooks.com) documents the first book edition bibliographically.

Early critical reception in Russia was constrained by censorship and by Tolstoy's contested legacy (his excommunication by the Orthodox Church in 1901; the Tolstoyan movement; the competing claims of his heirs). The Soviet period canonised the work as social-realist and anti-tsarist while suppressing its anarchist and spiritual dimensions — a pattern Souder traces in detail. The Russian Ministry of Education and Science included *Hadji Murat* in its 2013 recommended school reading list.

### 6. Chechen/Avar and decolonial reception

Ani Kokobobo's "Tolstoy's Enigmatic Final Hero: Holy War, Sufism, and the Spiritual Path in *Hadji Murat*" (*The Russian Review* 76, 2017, pp. 38–52) argues that Islam — specifically Naqshbandi Sufi practice as it operated in 19th-century Chechnya and Dagestan — has been systematically omitted from prior scholarly discussions of the novella and is essential to understanding it. Kokobobo reads Sufism as the "connecting thread between the violence in Hadji Murat and Tolstoy's later spiritual beliefs," arguing that Tolstoy's own mystical turn gave him insight into forms of Islamic spirituality that most Russian writers missed. This article was reprinted as Chapter 10 of Kokobobo's *Leo Tolstoy: The Power of Dissent* (Academic Studies Press, 2025), whose Section IV ("Defying Empire in *Hadji Murat*") also includes Chapter 11: "'Why does Russia Need Hadji Murat's Head?' — Hadji Murat, Dagestani Identity, and Russia's Colonial Exploits."

Gould's "Topographies of Anticolonialism" (2013, above) is the key ecocritical-postcolonial reading. Her broader framing — that Tolstoy's Caucasian writings function as "ethnographic footnotes" — is a contested critical position: it acknowledges the richness of Tolstoy's ethnographic attention while questioning whether that attention constitutes a genuinely anticolonial gaze or reproduces the imperial knowing-gaze in a more sympathetic register.

Irina Yefimenko's (or similar author — attribution uncertain in available sources) "Listen Then, Avars, to What I Tell You: The Unification of Chechen and Avar Oral Culture in Tolstoy's *The Cossacks* and *Hadji Murat*" (*FOLKLORICA* 26, July 2022, pp. 49–71) traces Tolstoy's engagement with the Dagestani Avar Song of Khochbar back to his 1852 transliteration of Chechen *uzami*, arguing he privileged oral tradition over writing as an "instance of ethical cultural expression" and that in *Hadji Murat* the Song's adaptation to the historical resistance fighter enacts a "sociopolitical challenge to the Russian empire's colonial hegemony."

The Soufiane L. Medium essay (2024, "Leo Tolstoy's Hadji Murat: Cultural Sensitivity or Imperialist Gaze?") represents the more popular strand of the ongoing debate about whether Tolstoy's portrait of the highlanders transcends or reproduces Orientalist paradigms.

The Lewis Bagby argument (cited via Swarthmore course materials) that *Hadji Murat* "rewrites 'Ammalat-Bek'" (Marlinsky's 1832 Romantic Caucasus tale) is an intertextual claim about genre, not politics — but situates the novella within a long Russian literary tradition of writing the Caucasus that later decolonial readers have read critically.
