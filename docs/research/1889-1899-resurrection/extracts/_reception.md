# Reception — *Resurrection* (Воскресение, 1899)

**Scope:** contemporary Russian critical and public reception 1899–1901; church and state reaction;
international reception 1899–1900; brief afterlife pointer.  
**Sources:** corpus editorial commentary (PSS vol. 33, ed. N. V. Gorbachev) and PSS vol. 34 (A. I.
Opulsky); web-sourced scholarship as attributed below. Every claim is attributed; uncertain attributions
are flagged.

---

## 1. Critical and Literary Reception in Russia, 1899–1900

### Serialisation as a cultural event

The publication of *Resurrection* was treated by contemporaries as an event beyond its purely literary
significance. *Niva* was a mass-circulation "family" weekly; the illustrated serial that began in No. 11
(13 March 1899) was the first full-length Tolstoy novel in approximately twenty-five years — since *Anna
Karenina* (serialised 1875–77) — and for many readers the first substantial fiction from an author now
known across Russia as a religious reformer and moral teacher as much as a novelist. (Source: PSS vol. 33,
N. V. Gorbachev commentary, line 311; ourcivilisation.com / Ernest J. Simmons, *Introduction to Tolstoy's
Writings*, 1968, hereafter "Simmons 1968".)

The excitement the serialisation generated is recorded in a letter to Tolstoy from the art critic V. V.
Stasov (Владимир Стасов): *"How all of us here rejoiced when we learned that the chapters of 'Resurrection'
will not be 60 or 80 but 100 or more."* (Source: Simmons 1968, ourcivilisation.com, citing Stasov's letter
to Tolstoy; the letter's date is not given by Simmons.) A second, unattributed contemporary exclamation —
"as if an angel were to take to novel-writing" — is cited by the same source (Simmons 1968), without
naming the speaker or the outlet.

### Sales and print runs

*Resurrection* outsold both *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina* in the Russian market on original
publication. Abroad (England, United States), it "enjoyed a larger sale than any other work by Tolstoy up
to that time" (Simmons 1968). No specific print-run or circulation figures for the *Niva* serialisation
have been identified in the sources consulted; the PSS editors (vol. 33) record only the contract terms
(1,000 roubles per printer's sheet, 12,000 roubles advance) and note that Tolstoy's proceeds to the
Doukhobor fund totalled 34,200 roubles (approx. $17,000 at the time) — a floor on earnings, not a
circulation figure. The complete, unaltered Russian text was not published until the 1936 Jubilee Edition
(PSS vol. 32–33); the censored *Niva* text and the Marks separate editions (1900) were what Russian readers
in 1899–1900 actually read.

### The art-versus-sermon debate

The critical debate about whether *Resurrection* succeeded as art or devolved into a sermon / moral
manifesto was present from the earliest readings but is documented more fully in English and retrospective
sources than in identifiable Russian-press reviews in these search results.

Anton Chekhov, who had heard an early draft read aloud at Yasnaya Polyana in August 1895 (PSS vol. 33, ed.
commentary, lines 83–87), offered a nuanced assessment when the finished novel appeared: he praised the
sections depicting princes, generals, peasants, and convicts but criticised the Nekhlyudov–Maslova
romantic plot as "somewhat unclear and contrived," and objected that the resolution via Gospel text was
"too theological" — readers, he argued, needed first to be persuaded of Scripture's truth before being
offered it as a solution. (Source: Chekhov's assessment summarised in the Russian Wikipedia article on
the novel, *Воскресение (роман)*, citing his correspondence; Simmons 1968 makes the same point without
quoting Chekhov directly.) Vladimir Stasov's position was wholehearted praise (see above).

Maxim Gorky is recorded as viewing the novel as Tolstoy's summative work, though no specific 1899–1900
review is cited by the available sources. (Source: Russian Wikipedia, *Воскресение (роман)*.)

Lenin's later characterisation — calling the novel "direct and sincere protest against a society of lies"
(Soviet framing) — belongs to a subsequent critical tradition and should not be taken as a contemporary
1899–1900 Russian press response. (Source: Russian Wikipedia, *Воскресение (роман)*.)

An unsigned English review in *The Bookman* (April 1900) captured the hybrid character of the novel from
an Anglo-American reader's perspective: it "might have been written by Zola in collaboration with the
prophet Isaiah." (Source: fractiousfiction.com / Ted Gioia, reprinting content from *The Honest Broker*,
attributing the *Bookman* citation without a page number.)

**Caveat:** Named Russian critics writing specifically in 1899–1900 Russian periodicals (*Русская мысль*,
*Новое время*, *Вестник Европы*) have not been recovered by the searches conducted for this file. The
Simmons 1968 and Wikipedia sources note that "progressive critics showered praises" while "conservative
foreign critics expressed indignation," but no individual Russian critical reviews by name and outlet from
this period are cited in available web-accessible scholarship. This is an identified gap; corpus-level
research (period press archives) would be needed to fill it.

## 2. Church and State Reaction

### Censorship as institutional reception

The institutional reaction of the tsarist censorship was itself a form of reception: the systematic
removal of the two most sensitive chapters — Part I, chs. XXXIX–XL (the prison-church liturgy / Eucharist
scene) — reducing them in the published *Niva* text to the single sentence *«Началось богослужение»*
("The service began"), plus hundreds of further cuts across the three parts, constitutes the clearest
record of how the state apparatus read the novel. In total, V. D. Bonch-Bruevich counted 497 censorship
distortions, omissions, and paraphrases affecting 10,240 words; only 25 of 123 chapters were printed
without any distortions. (Source: PSS vol. 33, N. V. Gorbachev commentary, line 473.)

The censorship targeted two categories: (a) anything touching the prestige of the Church and the
sacraments — the Eucharist chapters and Part II ch. XXVII (the Toporov / Pobedonostsev scene) most
prominently; (b) "indecent" vocabulary in a "family" journal (even "seduced" / «соблазнил» was replaced
throughout by "lured" / «увлёк» or "deceived" / «обманул»). (Source: PSS vol. 33, ed. commentary, lines
347–349.)

### The novel as precipitant of the excommunication

The Holy Synod's «Определение» No. 557 of 20–22 February 1901 (O.S.), published in *Церковные ведомости*
(24 February), excommunicated Tolstoy (more precisely: declared that he had separated himself from the
Church) for doctrinal apostasy. The decree named the Eucharist — the satire in chs. XXXIX–XL of
*Resurrection* — as «последняя и высшая степень» ("the last and highest degree") of his offence. However,
the decree's operative indictment was doctrinally broad: rejection of the Trinity, Christ's divinity,
the virgin birth, the afterlife, and all sacraments as a class — the cumulative content of Tolstoy's
religious writings of the 1880s–90s. The Eucharist clause was the rhetorical capstone, not the
doctrinal foundation. (Source: PSS vol. 34, T. 54 diary extracts, and A. I. Opulsky editorial commentary,
vol. 34, pp. 575–577, as analysed in *_excommunication.md* in this folder.)

The PSS editor (Opulsky, vol. 34 commentary) states the mechanism plainly: *«Отлучение Толстого от
церкви было задумано царским правительством еще в 1880-х гг. Толчком к опубликованию отлучения
послужило появление в 1899 г. романа «Воскресение»…»* — "The excommunication was conceived by the
tsarist government as far back as the 1880s. The impulse to *publish* the excommunication was the
appearance in 1899 of the novel *Resurrection*…" The PSS commentary additionally locates the personal
sting in the Toporov character — a transparent caricature of Konstantin Pobedonostsev, lay
Over-Procurator of the Holy Synod 1880–1905 — calling the allusion «недвусмысленный» ("unmistakable").
(Source: PSS vol. 34, Opulsky, pp. 575–577.)

Pobedonostsev's precise role in the decree remains contested: the Soviet PSS commentary names him as the
decree's author; some later Western accounts (summarised at socialbites.ca, citing unnamed historiography)
hold that Metropolitan Antony (Vadkovsky) of St Petersburg drove the decision while Pobedonostsev
hesitated, fearing the decree would amplify Tolstoy. The uncertainty is flagged here; neither position
can be adjudicated from the sources available.

### Public reaction to the excommunication

The excommunication provoked a notably adverse public response — adverse, that is, to the Synod. The
educated secular public "laughed at the Synod's Decree and applauded the writer" (Tolstoy expert Pavel
Basinsky, as cited at gw2ru.com/arts/1348-tolstoy-church-scandal, no original publication date given).
Sofya Tolstaya described a flood of letters and flowers; demonstrations of sympathy occurred in several
cities. Tolstoy received a Nobel Peace Prize nomination in 1901. Archbishop John of Kronstadt
(Иоанн Кронштадтский) took the opposite position, calling Tolstoy "a heretic who surpassed all the
heretics." (Source: gw2ru.com/arts/1348-tolstoy-church-scandal.)

## 3. International Reception

### The simultaneous foreign publication arrangement

The serialisation in *Niva* was accompanied by simultaneous foreign-language publication across five
countries — Russia, England, France, Germany, and the United States — for the express purpose of
maximising revenue for the Doukhobor emigration fund. Chertkov managed the foreign rights from England;
Tolstoy himself reversed his earlier renunciation of literary fees to make this possible. (Source: PSS
vol. 33, ed. commentary, lines 129–135; Simmons 1968.)

The "simultaneous" publication was in practice imperfect: gaps in *Niva* (Nos. 26 and 30) were introduced
specifically because the censored Russian text was outrunning the uncut foreign editions, and Tolstoy asked
Marks to slow serialisation so as not to expose the cuts. (Source: PSS vol. 33, ed. commentary, line 311.)

### England — the Maude / Brotherhood Publishing translation

The English translation was made by Louise Maude, published in 1900 by the Brotherhood Publishing
Company. Aylmer Maude stated that "Tolstoy authorized my wife's translation." The Brotherhood Publishing
Company was connected to the Tolstoyan Brotherhood Church (Croydon), and the translation was sold in
penny parts specifically *"in aid of the Doukhobors… settling now in Canada."* (Source: PSS vol. 33, ed.
commentary, line 517; Wikipedia, *Aylmer and Louise Maude*; Simmons 1968.) In England and America the
novel "enjoyed a larger sale than any other work by Tolstoy up to that time" (Simmons 1968).

### France — *Echo de Paris* and the Flammarion edition

The first French publication was in *Echo de Paris*, trans. Téodor de Wyzewa, for which Chertkov received
12,000 francs. However, the *Echo de Paris* was a conservative, clerical paper, and the prison-church
service scene (chs. XXXIX–XL) — already cut from *Niva* — was absent from this French serialisation as
well, "evidently because *Echo de Paris* was a conservative and clerical paper" (PSS vol. 33, ed.
commentary, line 505). A fuller French translation with Pasternak illustrations was subsequently published
by Flammarion (1900), translated by Halperine-Kaminsky. (Source: PSS vol. 33, ed. commentary, lines
505–517.)

In France fifteen editions, under authorised and unauthorised imprints, accumulated during 1899–1900.
(Source: Simmons 1968.)

### Germany — Deutsche Verlagsanstalt (Fontane)

The German first-printing was through Deutsche Verlagsanstalt (Fontane). (Source: PSS vol. 33, ed.
commentary, line 517, which names Fontane.) Twelve different translations came out in Germany alone in
1900. (Source: Simmons 1968.)

### United States — Cosmopolitan bowdlerisation and the Chertkov contract

The American serial rights were sold first to *Cosmopolitan* magazine (10,000 roubles), which published
the novel under the title *The Awakening*. *Cosmopolitan*'s editor "did not hesitate to tone down or
delete love passages which he thought might offend the sensibilities of this magazine's respectable
middle-class readers." (Source: Simmons 1968.) The PSS editors add that Chertkov broke this contract over
the bowdlerisation; the serial later appeared in *The Clarion*, and the book edition was published by Dodd,
Mead & Co. (Source: PSS vol. 33, ed. commentary, lines 519–533.)

### Piracy and the copyright problem

Widespread piracy attended the international publication because many publishers assumed Tolstoy had
renounced copyright (as he had for earlier works). "Much pirating went on which caused Tolstoy a great
deal of embarrassment" (Simmons 1968), since he had in fact sold first-publication rights specifically to
fund the Doukhobor resettlement and unauthorised editions undermined that income.

### Most-translated late work

The combination of commercial and humanitarian visibility, simultaneous multi-country publication, and the
scandal of censorship made *Resurrection* Tolstoy's most widely and rapidly translated late work. No
single total figure for translations or editions has been recovered from the sources consulted for this
file, but the Germany-alone and France-alone figures (12 and 15 editions respectively in 1899–1900) are
indicative of the scale.

## 4. Afterlife (Brief Pointer)

*Resurrection* is commonly described as Tolstoy's "third novel" — the third and last full-length novel he
published in his lifetime, after *War and Peace* (1869) and *Anna Karenina* (1877). Later critical
assessment has generally ranked it below those two works, noting one-dimensional characterisation and a
style that subordinates aesthetic quality to philosophical purpose; a minority position (Brian Aldiss) has
called it Tolstoy's greatest novel. (Source: Wikipedia, *Resurrection (Tolstoy novel)*; Simmons 1968.)

**Opera.** Franco Alfano composed *Risurrezione* (libretto: Camillo Antona Traversi and Cesare Hanau) —
his most successful work and, incidentally, the opera he was working on when later called to complete
Puccini's *Turandot*. It premiered at the Teatro Vittorio Emanuele, Turin, on 30 November 1904, conducted
by Tullio Serafin. By 1954 it had received over 1,000 performances worldwide. Translated into French
(Paul Ferrier), performed Brussels 1906, Paris (Opéra-Comique, multiple runs), and German (*Katjuscha*),
Berlin 1909. Notable revivals: Wexford Festival Opera 2018; Florence May Festival 2020. (Source:
Wikipedia, *Risurrezione*; philsoperaworld.blog, Ted Libbey / Phil's Opera World, 21 October 2018.) A
second opera, *Vzkriesenie*, was composed by Ján Cikker (1960). Shostakovich sketched a *Katyusha
Maslova* (1940–41, unfinished). (Source: Wikipedia, *Resurrection (Tolstoy novel)*.)

**Stage.** Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko staged a production for the Moscow Art Theatre (1930). An
American stage adaptation starred Blanche Walsh and Joseph Haworth (1904 tour). (Source: Wikipedia,
*Resurrection (Tolstoy novel)*.)

**Film.** Samuel Goldwyn's *We Live Again* (1934, dir. Rouben Mamoulian, Fredric March, Anna Sten) is the
most widely known English-language film. Mikhail Shveitser directed a Soviet adaptation (1960). (Source:
Wikipedia, *Resurrection (Tolstoy novel)*; Wikipedia, *Resurrection (1944 film)*.)
