# Scholarship & Historical Context — "Against the Death Penalty" cluster (1900–1910)

Phase-3 sub-step. Sources consulted: 18. Triangulation entries: 11.

---

## 1. Received view (attributed)

### 1.1 Tolstoy's anti-capital-punishment writings in biographical scholarship

The dominant English-language scholarly frame treats Tolstoy's opposition to capital punishment as inseparable from the broader crisis of the Stolypin repression (1906–09) and centres almost entirely on «Не могу молчать» / *I Cannot Be Silent* (1908). Rosamund Bartlett's *Tolstoy: A Russian Life* (2010/2011) — the most thorough recent English biography — describes that essay as "a stirring… response to multiple hangings" and as the climax of Tolstoy's political witness in his final years, but does not give sustained individual attention to the five other essays in this cluster. Aylmer Maude's *The Life of Tolstoy: Later Years* (1911, revised) — the closest English-language source to the events — translated or arranged translation for most of the cluster through the Free Age Press, but his biographical discussion likewise treats the 1908 outburst as the defining moment, with the surrounding essays treated as context or aftermath.

The cluster essays are mostly available in Maude translations (see §3 below), but they have attracted little dedicated scholarly commentary as individual works. Scholarly treatment of Tolstoy's political non-fiction of these years (Orwin, Gustafson, Paperno, McLean) concentrates on the doctrinal treatises (*The Kingdom of God Is Within You*, *The Law of Violence and the Law of Love*); the polemic essays are referenced as applications of doctrine, not studied in their own right. The result is a significant gap: the 1900–1910 anti-gallows cluster as a coherent body of writing — with its own internal development, its distinct legal argument (the death penalty was a legislative newcomer, not a Russian tradition), and its precise targets (the 1881 emergency statute, the Stolypin field courts, the Gorlovka sentences, A. A. Stolypin's Novoe Vremya article) — has not received a dedicated monograph or article in English scholarship as of this writing.

One recent exception is the volume chapter "'Do What Is Right, and Let Come What May': Tolstoy and Penal Abolition" (De Gruyter, 2025), which situates Tolstoy within the long history of anti-capital-punishment thought, but the full text was inaccessible for this research pass (HTTP 405). Its existence indicates growing scholarly interest in this dimension of Tolstoy's work.

### 1.2 Russian-language scholarship

Russian scholarship on Tolstoy's political non-fiction (Eikhenbaum, Gusev, the PSS editorial apparatus) is the most detailed primary record. Eikhenbaum's editorial history in PSS Tom 37 reconstructs the composition of *I Cannot Be Silent* in full; the PSS commentaries to Toms 35–38 document the genesis and censorship history of the other essays. These commentaries are the most authoritative source on the cluster and are the baseline against which secondary scholarship should be tested. Soviet-era scholarship tended to foreground the anti-tsarist dimension while downplaying the religious-ethical argument; post-Soviet scholarship (Zhdanov, Gusev) has restored more of the doctrinal context.

## 3. Historical facts established

### 3a. Russia's death penalty history: Elizabeth Petrovna to the Stolypin field courts

**Elizabeth Petrovna's moratorium (1744–1762).** Empress Elizabeth Petrovna signed the first formal moratorium decree in 1744 (the date 1753–54 sometimes cited in secondary literature refers to further extending and codifying decrees; the initial proscription dates to 1743–44). During her twenty-year reign no executions were carried out — Marasinova's study in *Quaestio Rossica* (2018) calls this "unique — no European state had such a thing." Her motivation was personal and religious: she refused to sign death warrants on grounds of conscience. Prisoners sentenced to death were held on Rågervik Island, creating an administrative problem the Senate found intractable. Cesare Beccaria cited "the great example of the Russian Empress" in his 1764 *On Crimes and Punishments*, which influenced Leopold I of Tuscany to abolish capital punishment in 1786. Elizabeth's moratorium was de facto, not a formal legal abolition.
*Sources: Marasinova, Quaestio Rossica 2018; TASS factbox; rbth.com history article; Wikipedia "Elizabeth of Russia."*

**Post-Elizabeth status.** After 1762 the death penalty was technically available but rarely applied for ordinary crimes. Alexander I (1801–1825): ~24 executions, mostly wartime. Nicholas I (1825–1855): ~40 executions including five Decembrist leaders (1825). Alexander II (1855–1881): death sentences were typically commuted to hard labour or exile. The 19th-century ordinary criminal code (Уложение о наказаниях, 1845/1866) did not routinely include capital punishment for common crimes; it survived in the military criminal code and in law governing state crimes (treason, regicide). Tolstoy's claim in *To the Tsar and His Assistants* (1901) that capital punishment "was formerly unknown in our code of laws" is accurate as regards the ordinary civil penal code; it is an overstatement as regards military and political-crime provisions that persisted throughout.
*Sources: Wikipedia "Capital punishment in Russia"; TASS factbox; Wikisource *To the Tsar and His Assistants*.*

**The 1881 Положение о мерах к охранению государственного порядка и общественного спокойствия (Statute of Increased Protection).** Enacted following the assassination of Alexander II on 1 March 1881, this emergency statute gave provincial governors and governors-general extraordinary powers: the power to declare "reinforced protection" (усиленная охрана) or "extraordinary protection" (чрезвычайная охрана) over any area; under extraordinary protection a military commander could transfer criminal cases from civilian courts to courts-martial and remove elected officials. This is the mechanism through which capital punishment re-entered ordinary Russian governance — not through a change to the criminal code but through emergency jurisdiction that bypassed civil courts. Tolstoy identified this statute by name in his second demand in *To the Tsar and His Assistants* (1901). The statute was renewed annually and remained in force through 1917.
*Sources: Wikipedia "Martial law in Russia" (partial); search synthesis from multiple sources; Tolstoy, *To the Tsar and His Assistants* (text).*

**Post-1905 wave and Stolypin's field courts.** Following the failed 1905 revolution, Pyotr Stolypin (appointed Prime Minister July 1906) formalized emergency justice. The Aptekarsky Island bombing on 25 August 1906 (which killed 27 and wounded Stolypin's children) accelerated the move. Field courts-martial (военно-полевые суды) were established: empowered to sentence and execute within 24 hours with no appeal. In the first six months the courts passed 1,042 death sentences. Total executions 1906–1909: estimates range from "over 2,000" to "over 3,000 (possibly 5,500)." Kropotkin's newspaper-based compilation (*The Terror in Russia*, 1909) gives: 280 executions in 1906, 508 in 1907, 802 in the first ten months of 1908. The field courts formally expired in April 1907 (Duma refused to ratify them beyond the recess period), but military district courts operating under emergency statutes continued sentencing throughout 1908–1909. The gallows became "Stolypin's necktie" (столыпинский галстук) — the phrase coined by Kadet deputy Fyodor Rodichev in a Duma speech on 17 November 1907; Stolypin responded by challenging Rodichev to a duel; Rodichev apologised.
*Sources: Wikipedia "Pyotr Stolypin"; Kropotkin, *The Terror in Russia* Part 1 Ch. 3 (marxists.org); Spartacus Educational; Britannica.*

### 3b. The Gorlovka case

**The uprising (December 1905).** The Gorlovka armed uprising (Горловское вооружённое восстание) took place in Gorlovka, Bakhmut District, Ekaterinoslav Governorate. The trigger was an announced wage cut (40–50%) at a machine-building factory on the railway line. By 17 December, approximately 4,000 armed workers from surrounding Donbas mines had converged on Gorlovka; a six-hour battle with government troops left at least 18 dead and many wounded, before the workers were dispersed. It was one of the largest armed episodes of the 1905 revolutionary wave in the Donets basin.

**The trial (December 1908).** The case was deliberately transferred from a civilian jury court to a military tribunal — the government wanted a "terrifying trial." The Odessa Military District court sat in Ekaterinoslav from 7 to 19 December 1908. Total defendants: 131. Convicted: 92. Death sentences: 32 (by hanging). The remaining 60 convicted received sentences ranging from life hard labour (12 persons) down to 2 years 8 months (5 persons).

**Commutations and executions (1909).** Of the 32 death sentences, 24 were commuted. Eight condemned men were held in solitary death cells for approximately eight months. They were executed on the night of 4 September 1909. The claim in the dive prompt that Nicholas II issued a "Даровать жизнь" order on 1 January 1909 could not be independently verified from English-language or Russian-language web sources examined here; the Wikipedia (RU) article on the uprising and the LiveJournal chronicle both state the commutation without attributing it to a specific imperial act or date. This point requires checking against Russian archival sources or the PSS commentary to «Смертная казнь и христианство». The trial concluded on 19 December 1908 — the day after A. A. Stolypin published his Novoe Vremya article; Tolstoy wrote his reply on 2 January 1909. The temporal proximity is almost certainly not coincidental.
*Sources: Wikipedia RU «Горловское вооружённое восстание»; LiveJournal erilaine/31838; admin-gorlovka.ru chronicle.*

### 3c. Alexander Arkadyevich Stolypin

Alexander Arkadyevich Stolypin (1863–1925) was a Russian journalist, poet, and political publicist. He was the younger brother of Prime Minister Pyotr Arkadyevich Stolypin. He died on 23 November 1925 in Belgrade (the émigré destination following the Revolution confirms he was a conservative). His article in *Novoe Vremya* dated 18 December 1908 defended capital punishment on Christian grounds, citing Mark 7:9–13 (Christ's statement that one who curses a father or mother "is to be put to death"). This article was the immediate trigger for Tolstoy's «Смертная казнь и христианство» / *Capital Punishment and Christianity*, dated 2 January 1909. The essay's reply to A. A. Stolypin is: "No fanatic has, until now, taken upon himself to justify capital punishment by the words of Jesus." The one-word verdict on the article itself is "Shame" (Стыдно), which forms a subsection of the essay — not a separate publication. The term «Заметки» (Notes/Remarks) in the dive prompt's description of the article matches the usual *Novoe Vremya* column format; the specific column title has not been independently confirmed here.
*Sources: Find a Grave memorial 261205774; Wikipedia "Stolypin (disambiguation)"; Presidential Library Russia (prlib.ru, inaccessible but title confirmed); nonresistance.org *Capital Punishment and Christianity* text.*

## 5. Open verification flags

- **Nicholas II's 1 January 1909 commutation order**: stated in the dive prompt as «Даровать жизнь»; not confirmed by any web-accessible source in this pass. Requires checking PSS commentary to Tom 37 or Russian archival sources. The commutation of 24 of 32 Gorlovka sentences is confirmed; the specific date and form of the imperial act is not.
- **A. A. Stolypin's column title «Заметки»**: the title "Заметки" for the 18 December 1908 *Novoe Vremya* article is in the dive prompt but not independently confirmed here. The date (18 December) and the Mark 7:9–13 citation are confirmed from the Tolstoy text itself.
- **«Обновление» / Obnovlenie vs. «Обновление» (Renovationist church press)**: the Moscow publisher jailed was Nikolai Felten (editor of «Обновление»). Some English-language sources transliterate the press as "Renovation" or "Renovationist." Felten appears in Tolstoy's own account as "a man connected with the Petersburg Renovation publications" — the *I Cannot Be Silent* dive's PSS editorial history is the authoritative source for these details. The 1907 essay (*Thou Shalt Not Kill Anyone*) is the text written in response; the nonresistance.org text (dated 18 August 1907) confirms the trigger.
- **Tolstoy's «Единственное средство» / "The Only Means" (1901)**: no English-language scholarship on this specific essay was found in this pass. The argument (all oppression rests on the threat of killing) connects directly to the cluster's internal logic but the essay has attracted no separate scholarly attention traceable here.
