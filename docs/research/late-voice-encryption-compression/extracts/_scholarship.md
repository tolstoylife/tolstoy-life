## 1. Received view, per topic

### A. Tolstoy & censorship, late period

The standard scholarly account is well established: after his religious conversion of the early 1880s, Tolstoy's non-fiction works were systematically banned in Russia by both ecclesiastical and state censors, so that many were published abroad first — in Russian or in translation — before any (often heavily truncated) Russian edition appeared. Rosamund Bartlett (*Tolstoy: A Russian Life*, 2010) treats the last three decades of Tolstoy's life as dominated by this pressure: state censorship, excommunication (formally the Holy Synod's "falling away" declaration, February 24, 1901), and Chertkov's consequent role as the conduit for uncensored texts. A. N. Wilson (*Tolstoy*, 1988) and biographers generally concur. The 1887 contemporary account by American journalist Isabel Hapgood, "Count Tolstoi and the Public Censor" (marxists.org archive), gives a near-contemporary close-up: the censor excised passages on poverty, anti-clericalism, and criticism of emperors from *My Religion* alone, and the Moscow Census article lost over a thousand words; the article notes that complete texts "printed abroad, under a different title, supply the suppressed passages" — a mechanism already operating before 1890. Inessa Medzhibovskaya (*Tolstoy and the Religious Culture of His Time*, Lexington Books, 2008, covering 1845–1887) focuses on the conversion arc rather than the censorship mechanics, and her coverage stops before the 1900–1910 period the dive centres on. Donna Tussing Orwin's edited volumes (*The Cambridge Companion to Tolstoy*; *Anniversary Essays on Tolstoy*, Cambridge University Press, 2010) and her *Tolstoy's Art and Thought, 1847–1880* (Princeton University Press, 1993) focus primarily on the pre-conversion period; Orwin's Companion includes a chapter on theater (by Caryl Emerson) that names censorship as a contextual factor for Tolstoy's theatrical works but does not develop the argument in detail.

**Gap:** no English-language monograph focuses specifically on the censorship mechanics of the 1900–1910 period as a system with deliberate counter-strategies. The standard view treats censorship as background condition, not as a generative pressure that shaped Tolstoy's genre choices.

### B. Chertkov & the Free Age Press / «Свободное слово»

Charlotte Alston (*Tolstoy and His Disciples: The History of a Radical International Movement*, Bloomsbury Academic, 2014 / paperback 2020) is the main English-language scholarly treatment of Chertkov's publishing operation as a historical phenomenon. Alston presents the abroad-publishing network as an international movement with Chertkov as its chief organiser — the first in-depth historical account of Tolstoyism as an international phenomenon, offering (per reviews) a relatively unbiased portrait of Chertkov. The key structural facts she covers, corroborated by other sources: Chertkov was exiled by the Russian government in 1897 and moved to England, setting up the Russian-language *Svobodnoe Slovo* (Free Word) press — first at Purleigh, Essex, then at Tuckton House near Christchurch, Hampshire — producing texts "smuggled back into Russia." The English-language branch, the Free Age Press, was established in 1900 with Arthur Fifield as manager. Michael J. Holman's journal article "Translating Tolstoy for the Free Age Press: Vladimir Chertkov and His English Manager Arthur Fifield" (*The Slavonic and East European Review*, Vol. 66, No. 2, April 1988) is the most detailed scholarly account of the Free Age Press's operations, drawing on Fifield's own 1933 typewritten memoir. Holman documents: the press had no physical printing facility (Fifield managed from his back bedroom, using outside printers); Chertkov served as joint editor providing direction from Purleigh; between 1900 and 1902 the press issued 43 publications totalling approximately 209,000 copies plus 250,000 leaflets; total output estimated at 424 million pages. Alexandra Popoff (*Tolstoy's False Disciple: The Untold Story of Leo Tolstoy and Vladimir Chertkov*, Pegasus Books, 2014) adds the critical perspective: Chertkov "aspired to the role of Tolstoy's editor and censor" and pursued control of the literary estate. Popoff documents that Chertkov held over 2,000 letters from Tolstoy, many suppressed in Soviet archives, and that the posthumous will designating Chertkov as editor was, in Popoff's reading, extracted under duress. Chertkov himself states in *The Last Days of Tolstoy* (1911, Gutenberg text): "The editorship and first publication of all his posthumous works he entrusted to me, with the understanding that everything brought out by me should at once become public property." The *Posrednik* (Intermediary) publishing house, co-founded by Chertkov and Tolstoy in 1884 to distribute cheap folk-tale and moral literature, is the domestic parallel operation; it issued over 1,000 titles and 20 million copies in its first nine years despite "harassment by the censors" (Wenzer, "Tolstoy's Georgist Spiritual Political Economy," 1997; Grokipedia / Wikipedia on Vladimir Chertkov).

**Gap:** English scholarship does not closely examine Chertkov's claim (reflected in the dive's primary sources) of a formal 1900 protocol that ALL Tolstoy's writings going abroad passed exclusively through him. Alston and Holman describe the operation but not the explicit protocol as a named agreement. This specific procedural claim rests on Tolstoy–Chertkov correspondence (PSS vol. 88–89) not yet closely examined in English scholarship.

### C. Wisdom anthologies (Круг чтения / На каждый день / Путь жизни)

English academic coverage is thin. The four anthologies (*Mysli mudrykh lyudei* 1903, *Krug chteniya* 1906, *Na kazhdyi den* 1909, *Put zhizni* 1910–11) are consistently described as "Tolstoy's last major work" but receive limited sustained scholarly analysis in English. The Wikipedia entry for *A Calendar of Wisdom* (the Sekirin translation title) and the Grokipedia article on the same note the compositional arc accurately — three distinct Russian editions, first organised by subject, later by calendar date, finally condensed — and give the basic history: Tolstoy's earliest diary note on the concept dates to 1884; Peter Sekirin's 1997 translation was the first complete English version. The *ourcivilisation.com* overview of Tolstoy's life (based on Ernest J. Simmons's scholarship) characterises the sequence as "vast compendia of morally and spiritually elevating extracts" compiled "in the last ten years of his life." The one peer-reviewed journal article found is Tulyakova et al., "Leo Tolstoy, Mikhail Artsybashev, and Richard Wagner: About One Case of Polemics in Tolstoy's *The Circle of Reading*" (*Slověne: International Journal of Slavic Studies*, 2018). This article argues that the second edition of *Krug chteniya* (1908) operated as "a polemical text, deeply rooted in the ideological context of the 1890s–1900s" — Tolstoy used his editorial selections to argue against contemporary cultural movements (Artsybashev's *Sanin*, Nietzschean individualism). This is the closest English-accessible scholarship comes to treating the anthologies as politically active texts rather than passive wisdom compilations. The political encoding angle (hiding critique behind unsigned foreign-sage maxims) is not addressed in any source found. The Soviet-era ban on the *Calendar* (lifted only in 1995, when it sold 300,000 copies) is noted in multiple sources but not analysed as evidence that the Imperial censors were also wary of it.

**Gap:** no English monograph or substantial article treats the four-anthology sequence as a single compression project, or examines how the progressive dropping of attributions and the shift from calendar to thematic chapters altered the censorship profile of the collection. Russian-language scholarship (Karlik on the aphoristic method; Grodetskaya on editorial principles — both cited in Tulyakova et al.) exists but was not accessible for this pass.

### D. Folk-tale-as-encryption specifically

No English-language scholar argues directly that Tolstoy chose the parable/folk-tale form as a deliberate *response to censorship* — a workaround for things he could not say in legal Russian print. The standard reading is that Tolstoy turned to folk forms because he believed "the language of sophisticated literature was less effective, artistically, than the language of the so-called popular literature" (Simmons, as summarised in *ourcivilisation.com*) and to reach peasant audiences. The Posrednik/Intermediary mission is framed in access terms: cheap booklets for people who could not afford sophisticated literature. Censorship is acknowledged as a pressure on Posrednik (confiscations, fines on distributors) but is treated as an obstacle the organisation worked around, not as a generative pressure that *produced* the folk-tale form as a container for forbidden content. The closest the literature comes is Wenzer (1997), who notes that the censors harassed Posrednik and forced reliance on Chertkov's clandestine domestic printing and foreign dissemination, and Caryl Emerson's chapter in *Tolstoy in Context* (Cambridge University Press, 2021), which names censorship as a contextual factor for Tolstoy's experimental dramatised folk tales and parables without developing the encryption argument. The Kishinev–Sholem Aleichem link is documented in biographical sources: Tolstoy contributed three stories to the 1903 Yiddish anthology (*The Assyrian King Esarhaddon*, *Work, Death and Disease*, *The Three Questions*) and wrote to Aleichem condemning the Russian government's role in the pogrom. No English scholar has examined those three 1903 folk tales as encoding the pogrom condemnation that Tolstoy explicitly stated he could not publish in legal Russian print — the «этого-то я не могу сказать в русском легальном издании» formulation.

## 4. Gaps and uncertainties (needsReview)

**B-1 — The 1900 exclusive-channel protocol.** Alston and Holman confirm the Chertkov publishing channel but do not examine a named formal agreement that ALL Tolstoy writings going abroad passed *only* through Chertkov from 1900. This claim rests on the PSS Tolstoy–Chertkov correspondence (vols. 88–89). English scholarship has not examined it as a protocol. Needs: check the PSS letters vol. 88 extracts already in the dive for the exact formulation, and flag for future cross-check against Alston's primary sources.

**B-2 — The 1904 testament.** Chertkov's own account (Gutenberg text) references a will "shortly before [Tolstoy's] death in 1910," not a separate 1904 testament. Popoff discusses a secret will but frames it as late (1909–1910). The dive should verify whether "1904 testament" refers to a separate earlier instrument or is the same document differently dated by different sources.

**C-1 — Composition of the anthology sequence.** No English-language academic work traces how the four anthologies (1903→1906→1909→1910) differ structurally — what was added, cut, re-attributed, or anonymised at each iteration. Russian-language scholarship (Karlik; Grodetskaya, both cited in Tulyakova 2018) may cover this; not accessible for this pass. The "progressive dropping of attributions" claim in the dive needs verification against a Russian source or direct comparison of the four texts.

**C-2 — Imperial censorship of Krug chteniya.** Multiple sources note the Soviet-era ban (lifted 1995). No source found confirms that the Imperial censors specifically targeted *Krug chteniya* during Tolstoy's lifetime. The anthology's censorship profile under the tsarist regime is undocumented in any source found here.

**D-1 — Kishinev folk tales as deliberate encryption.** The scholarly literature confirms that Tolstoy contributed three folk tales to the 1903 Yiddish anthology for pogrom relief, and confirms he wrote to Aleichem condemning the government. But no scholar has examined whether the folk-tale form of those three specific stories was chosen *because* Tolstoy could not publish the same content legally in Russian — the «этого-то я не могу сказать» mechanism. This is the central `extends` finding and the most significant gap in the English scholarship.

**D-2 — "Two censors" as Chertkov's framing.** The dive attributes the "two censors" formulation (state + liberal press) to Chertkov. No English scholarly source uses this framing. Needs: identify which PSS letter or Chertkov document carries this formulation; the extract `v76_116_VredakciigazetNovoevremyaiRusskievedomosti.txt` (Tolstoy to the editorial offices of Novoe Vremya and Russkie Vedomosti) may be the primary-source anchor.

**A-1 — Medzhibovskaya scope.** Her *Long Conversion* covers only to 1887, not the 1900–1910 period. No comparable English monograph covers the late period's censorship mechanics in the same depth. This gap in the secondary literature itself is a finding.
